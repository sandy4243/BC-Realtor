## Why tenancy law follows every deal you touch

> **Scenario.** You list a duplex. The upstairs is a residential rental; the ground floor is a small dental office. A buyer wants to live upstairs and keep the dentist.
>
> - Can the seller simply tell the upstairs tenant to leave by completion? **No.** The *Residential Tenancy Act* sets strict notice periods, forms and compensation, and a **fixed term** can't be cut short for the buyer's use.
> - Can the buyer raise the dentist's rent after buying? Only if the **lease** allows it.
> - Who returns the tenants' deposits? For the residential tenant, the obligation **runs with the land** to the buyer. For the dentist, it depends on the lease and the contract.
>
> Get these wrong and your buyer can't move in on time, the seller breaches the contract, and you may face discipline.

BC has **two completely different rulebooks** for renting property:

- **Commercial tenancies** (stores, offices, warehouses, factories): mostly **common law** and the **lease itself**, with a little help from the *Commercial Tenancy Act*. The parties negotiate almost everything.
- **Residential tenancies** (homes): mostly governed by **statute**, the ***Residential Tenancy Act*** (1984) and its Regulation. Many rules **can't be contracted out of**.

So the first question in any landlord-tenant problem is always: **is it residential or commercial?**

## Vocabulary first

| Word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| **Lease** | An agreement giving a tenant **exclusive possession** of land for a term, usually for rent. Creates an **interest in land**. |
| **Licence** | Permission to use premises for a purpose, **without** exclusive possession or any interest in land. |
| **Tenancy agreement** | The *Residential Tenancy Act* name for a residential lease. |
| **Fixed term tenancy** | A tenancy with a set end date. |
| **Periodic tenancy** | One that renews itself automatically each period (week, month, year) until someone gives proper notice. |
| **Tenancy at will** | A tenancy either side can end at any time. |
| **RTB** | The **Residential Tenancy Branch**: the provincial body that runs residential tenancy dispute resolution. |
| **Security deposit** | Money held by the landlord against damage or other breaches by the tenant. |
| **Distress** | A commercial landlord's right to **seize and sell** a tenant's goods to recover unpaid rent. |
| **Surrender** | The tenant giving up the rest of the term, so it merges back into the landlord's interest. |
| **Quiet enjoyment** | The tenant's right to possess and use the premises without substantial interference, including from a defective title. |
| **Non-derogation from grant** | The landlord can't take away with one hand what they've given with the other. |
| **Interesse termini** | Old rule: no estate passes until the tenant takes possession. |
| **Gross lease / triple net lease** | Rent **includes** operating costs / tenant pays base rent **plus** all operating costs. |
| **Assignment / sublease** | Transferring the **whole** remaining interest / leasing **less** than the whole remaining term (or part of the space). |

## Lease or licence?

At common law the distinction mattered in two ways:

| | **Lease** | **Licence** |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | An **interest in land** | A personal **contractual permission** |
| Sale of the land | **Runs with the land**: a buyer takes subject to it | **Doesn't** bind a buyer |
| Relationship | Landlord and tenant, with many **implied** rights and duties | Only the rights written into the contract |

In BC today there's a third, bigger reason it matters: a licensee gets **no protection** under the *Residential Tenancy Act* or the *Commercial Tenancy Act*.

**The test** comes from ***Ocean Harvesters v. Quinlan Bros.*** (SCC 1974): what did the parties **intend**? Courts look at the agreement, the circumstances and its purpose. The labels ("lessor", "licensee") **don't decide it**. The most important clue is **exclusive possession**: does the occupier control the space, even against the owner?

- Landlord provides **meals and a housekeeper** who come and go → points to a **licence**.
- Landlord can only enter **to repair, after notice** → points to a **lease**.

> 🏢 **Commercial angle.** Kiosks in a mall, desks in a co-working space, parking stalls, rooftop antenna sites and booths at a market are often deliberately set up as **licences** so the owner keeps control and the occupier gets no interest in land. Read the agreement and ask who really controls the space.

## Commercial vs residential: which rulebook?

The *Residential Tenancy Act* covers a "**rental unit**": living accommodation rented (or intended to be rented) to a tenant. That includes caretakers' suites and housing provided to employees.

**It does NOT apply to:**

- business premises with a **dwelling attached**, rented under **one lease** (e.g. a shop with a flat above, under a single lease)
- housing rented by a **not-for-profit housing co-op** to its members
- accommodation where the tenant **shares a kitchen or bathroom with the owner**
- communal accommodation: student and staff residences, health and community care facilities, emergency shelters, jails
- a tenancy agreement for **more than 20 years**
- **vacation or travel** accommodation

A "residential" lease of **over 20 years** is treated as a **commercial** tenancy.

> 🏢 **Back to the scenario.** The dentist's space is commercial; the upstairs suite is residential, **unless** both are rented together under one lease to the dentist, in which case the whole thing is commercial.

## What must be in the agreement

**Commercial.** A lease of **more than 3 years** must be in **writing** to be enforceable (*Law and Equity Act* s.59, Lesson 8). The essentials:

- the **parties**
- a description of the premises ("demised premises")
- the **start date**
- the **length** of the term (the end date must be certain or calculable)
- the **rent**
- any other **material terms**

Because the common law fills gaps with **implied obligations** that may surprise both sides, a commercial lease should deal with everything in detail.

**Residential.** The Regulation requires a tenancy agreement to be **in writing**, **signed and dated** by both parties, easy to read, in at least **8-point** type. Section 13 of the Act lists required contents, including:

- the **standard terms** (deposits, inspections, rent increases, assignment and subletting, repairs, guests, landlord entry, ending the tenancy)
- legal names, the unit address, and the date of the agreement
- the landlord's (or agent's) **address for service and phone number**
- the start date; whether it's **periodic** (weekly, monthly…) or **fixed term** (and if fixed, the end date and whether it continues or the tenant must leave)
- the rent, any variation by number of occupants, and the **due date**
- which **services and facilities** are included
- any **security or pet damage deposit** and when it's paid

The government's **standard form** is optional, but any form must meet the Regulation. The tenant must get a copy **promptly**, and no later than **21 days** after signing.

## Types of tenancy and how they end

### Fixed term tenancies

| | **Commercial** | **Residential** |
|---|---|---|
| Length | Any length (a week, 99 years), but start and length must be **certain** | Any length up to 20 years |
| At the end | **Ends automatically**. No notice needed. | **Becomes month-to-month** on the same terms (s.44(3)), unless a valid **vacate clause** applies |

**Residential vacate clauses** are only allowed in narrow cases: a **sublease**, or a fixed term where the **landlord or a close family member** intends in good faith to move in (Regulation s.13.1). If the tenant won't leave, the landlord needs an **order of possession** from the RTB.

### Periodic tenancies

A periodic tenancy renews itself for another period of the same length until someone gives proper notice.

**Commercial.** Created **expressly** ("from year to year from April 1") or **by implication**: the tenant stays on after a fixed term and the landlord **accepts rent**.

- Original term a year or more, rent stated **yearly** → probably a **yearly** tenancy (e.g. "$12,000 per year payable $1,000 monthly").
- Term under a year, or rent stated **monthly** → probably **month-to-month**.
- In practice, the lease usually has an **overholding** clause saying what happens.

**Commercial termination.** Follow the lease. If it's silent, either side gives **reasonable notice**, ending on the **last day of a period**:

- usually **one full period** for weekly or monthly tenancies, and **6 months** for a yearly tenancy
- but it's flexible: in ***Union Properties v. Johnston Terminals*** (BCSC 1988), **28 days** was reasonable for a monthly tenancy
- **no required form**, as long as the intention to end it on a certain date is reasonably clear ("on or before" a date has been upheld, and small errors in names or dates are forgiven)

**Residential.** Created expressly, or automatically when a fixed term ends without a vacate clause (s.44). Ending it is strictly controlled (below).

### Tenancy at will

Either party can end it at any time. It often arises when a **seller lets a buyer move in before completion**. Unless agreed to be rent-free, the landlord is owed compensation for "**use and occupation**". If rent is paid **regularly**, the law may imply a **periodic** tenancy. The *Residential Tenancy Act* doesn't really provide for tenancies at will.

## Ending a residential tenancy

The Act gives tenants **security of tenure**: the landlord can end a tenancy **only for reasons the Act allows**. (A tenant can't be evicted just for complaining about repairs.)

**Section 44** lists how a tenancy ends: proper notice by either side (ss.45–50), a permitted vacate clause, **written** mutual agreement, the tenant **abandoning** the unit, **frustration**, an RTB **order**, or the end of a sublease.

**Section 44.1**: a landlord must not give a notice unless the grounds exist (or they reasonably believe they do). **Frivolous notices are an offence.**

### The landlord's notices at a glance

| Ground | Notice | Tenant's time to dispute |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-payment of rent** (s.46) | Effective at least **10 days** after receipt; **cancelled if the tenant pays within 5 days** | **5 days** |
| **Cause** (s.47) | At least **one month**, effective the last day of a rental period | **10 days** |
| **Tenant ceases to qualify** for subsidized housing (s.49.1) | **Two Month Notice** | **15 days** |
| **Landlord's or purchaser's use** (s.49) | **Three Month Notice** | **21 days** |
| **Demolition or conversion** (s.49(6)) | **Four Month Notice** | **30 days** |
| **Renovation or repair** (s.49.2) | Needs an **RTB order** first; at least **4 months** after the order | (at the hearing) |

If the tenant doesn't dispute in time, they're **deemed to accept** the notice and must leave by the effective date. An **order of possession** can be enforced by a court bailiff (sheriff).

**Non-payment worked example** (rent due on the 1st):

| Day | Event |
|---|---|
| 1st | Tenant doesn't pay |
| 3rd | Landlord serves notice: pay by midnight on the 8th, or the tenancy ends on the 13th |
| 6th | Tenant applies; the arbitrator extends payment to midnight on the 10th |
| 10th | Tenant pays; the notice has **no effect** |

Unpaid **utilities** can be treated as unpaid rent once they're more than **30 days** past a written demand.

**Cause (s.47) includes:**

- deposit not paid within **30 days** of its due date
- **repeatedly** late rent
- an unreasonable number of occupants
- the tenant or their guest **significantly disturbing** others, **seriously jeopardizing** health, safety or lawful rights, or putting the landlord's property at **significant risk**
- **illegal activity** causing (or likely to cause) damage, or affecting others' safety, quiet enjoyment or rights
- **extraordinary damage**, or not repairing damage they caused within a reasonable time
- breaching a **material term** and not fixing it within a reasonable time after **written** notice
- assigning or subletting **without written consent**
- **knowingly giving false information** about the property to a prospective tenant or **buyer**
- a government order requiring the unit to be vacated
- not complying with an arbitrator's order within **30 days**

In serious cases, an arbitrator can end the tenancy early without the usual month's notice (s.56).

### Landlord's and purchaser's use (s.49)

A **Three Month Notice** is available when the landlord, or a **close family member**, intends in good faith to live in the unit. "Close family member" means the person's **parent, spouse or child**, or their **spouse's parent or child**. (Landlords must generate this notice through the RTB's **Landlord Use Web Portal**.)

**For a buyer who wants to move in**, the **seller** (still the landlord) must give the Three Month Notice, but only when **all** of these are true:

1. the sale agreement was made in good faith and **all subjects are removed**
2. the buyer is an **individual** who (or whose close family member) intends in good faith to occupy the unit
3. the buyer **asks the seller in writing** to give the notice

The notice must include the **buyer's name and address**.

**Limits:**

- Landlord's or purchaser's use **can't** end a tenancy in a building with **5 or more rental units** that's either not stratified, or stratified with all rental units owned by one owner (s.49(6.1)).
- A **fixed term** can't be ended early for landlord's or purchaser's use. The effective date can't be earlier than the end of the term. (Cause and non-payment notices aren't limited this way.)
- Notices are effective on the **last day of a rental period**.

> 🧮 **Timeline example.** March 18: buyer offers on a home with a month-to-month tenant; subject to financing by March 25; completion June 30; the contract states the buyer will occupy and the seller will give notice. March 24: buyer removes the subject and asks in writing for notice. March 26: seller serves a Three Month Notice effective **June 30**. That works only because rent is due on the 1st and June 30 is the last day of a period at least three months later.

> 🔎 **Discipline case (2016).** A buyer's licensee wrote a contract on May 7 calling for **vacant possession on June 14**, with no tenancy details and no clause requiring the seller to give notice. Far too little time under s.49. The tenants couldn't leave before June 30, and the contract had to be amended. Breach of **Rules s.30** (best interests) and **s.34** (care and skill): **7-day suspension**, **$1,250** expenses and a remedial course.

> 🔎 **Fixed term trap.** Arnie lists Vince's tenanted house and sets completion four months out so Vince can serve a Three Month Notice for buyer Paula. The tenant, Thomas, produces a **fixed term** agreement with **six months** left. Vince forgot. Paula can't get vacant possession, and Vince is in breach. **Always ask whether a tenancy is fixed term or periodic, check with the tenant, and read the agreement.**

### Compensation

- **One month's rent** for any s.49 (landlord's/purchaser's use, demolition, conversion) notice, paid on or before the effective date. The tenant can simply withhold the last month's rent (s.51).
- **Twelve months' rent** (s.51(2)) if the stated purpose isn't accomplished within a reasonable time, or the unit isn't used for that purpose for at least **12 months** (or a prescribed period of at least 6 months). The buyer is on the hook if they asked for the notice. Only "extenuating circumstances" excuse it.

> ⚠️ **Tell your seller about the cost.** Ending a tenancy for a buyer costs the seller at least one month's rent. The seller may want the buyer to **contribute**, and the buyer must understand the 12-month risk if their plans change.

### Demolition, conversion, renovation

- **Four Month Notice** (s.49(6)): the landlord has **all permits and approvals** and intends in good faith to **demolish**, convert to **strata lots**, convert to a **non-profit housing co-op**, convert to a **caretaker's unit**, or convert to **non-residential** use.
- **Renovation or repair** (s.49.2): the landlord must first get an **RTB order**, proving permits, that the work needs the unit **vacant**, that it's **necessary to prolong or sustain** the building's use, and that ending the tenancy is the **only reasonable way**. The tenant can't be required to leave for at least **4 months** after the order.
  - In buildings with **5+ rental units**, the tenant gets a **right of first refusal** to rent the renovated unit (at the landlord's new rent). If the landlord fails to give at least **45 days'** notice of availability, compensation is **12 months' rent**.

### The tenant's side

- **Tenant's notice** (s.45, periodic tenancies): at least **one month**, given on or before the last day of a rental period, effective the last day of a later period.
- After receiving a s.49, s.49.1 or s.49.2 notice/order, the tenant can leave early on **10 days' written notice** (s.50), paying rent only for days occupied; the landlord still pays the one month's compensation.
- A tenant who treats the tenancy as over because of a landlord's **material breach** takes a risk: if wrong, they owe the rent.

### Form and delivery of notices

**Form (s.52).** In writing, signed and dated, with the unit address and effective date, and (for landlords) the **grounds**. A landlord's notice is **void** unless it uses the **approved form**. A tenant's notice can be in any written form with the details.

**Deemed receipt (s.88–90)** if not handed over in person:

| Method | Deemed received |
|---|---|
| **Mail** | **5th day** after mailing |
| **Email** | **3rd day** after sending |
| **Fax** | **3rd day** after faxing |
| Attached to the **door**, or left in a **mailbox or mail slot** | **3rd day** after |

## Residential tenancy disputes

Most disputes go to an **RTB arbitrator**, not court. Courts can't hear matters that must go to arbitration under the Act, **unless** the claim exceeds the **Small Claims** limit or is substantially linked to a case already in the Supreme Court.

- The applicant files the application (with fee) and gives the other party a copy within **3 days**.
- The arbitrator has wide powers: can run the hearing as they see fit (in person, by phone, in writing), accept any evidence (even if a court wouldn't), compel witnesses, and refuse frivolous or bad-faith applications. They decide on the merits and **aren't bound by precedent**.
- Either party can be represented (by a lawyer or a real estate service provider).
- The written decision is due within **30 days** of the hearing, is **final and binding**, and can be filed and enforced as a **Supreme Court** order.
- Since **2019**, the RTB's **Compliance and Enforcement Unit** investigates repeat or serious non-compliance and can impose penalties up to **$5,000 per day**.

**Manufactured home parks** are governed by the ***Manufactured Home Park Tenancy Act***, not the RTA, but disputes still go to the RTB and the protections are similar (no contracting out). Park rules must be **reasonable**: in one decision, **$1,000** fines without warning and eviction for criticizing the park online were struck down as "wholly unreasonable".

## Key rights and obligations, side by side

### Rent

**Commercial.** Rent and the split of **operating costs** (taxes, insurance, maintenance, utilities) are the most negotiated parts of the lease.

| Lease type | Tenant pays |
|---|---|
| **Gross** | One rent figure; the landlord pays operating costs (and bears increases until a rent review) |
| **Single net** (historically) | Base rent + **property taxes** |
| **Double net** (net-net) | Base rent + taxes + **building insurance** |
| **Triple net** (net-net-net) | Base rent + taxes + insurance + **repairs and maintenance** (all operating costs, including increases) |

These labels aren't standardized; **read the lease** to see exactly who pays what.

- The landlord can't raise rent during the **term** beyond what the lease allows. In a **periodic** tenancy, though, the landlord can simply end it with notice and offer a new lease at a higher rent.
- Rent can be paid in goods or services. Other payments (repairs, taxes, improvements) aren't "rent" unless the lease says so.
- Without an agreement, rent **isn't payable in advance**. Rent due on a day is on time until **midnight**, and can fall due on a Sunday.
- **Landlord's remedies** for unpaid rent: **sue** (including an expedited possession hearing under the *Commercial Tenancy Act*), **re-enter** and end the term, or **distrain** (seize and sell the tenant's goods).

**Residential (Part 3 of the Act).** A landlord can raise rent only:

1. **12 months** after the tenant's last increase or move-in date, or
2. when the unit is **re-rented to a new tenant**. If not raised then, the landlord waits 12 months.

- A **new owner** doesn't get a fresh right to raise rent.
- **Three months' written notice**, on the **prescribed form**. Short notice is treated as taking effect on the earliest legal date.
- The maximum is set annually by the RTB, based on **BC's CPI**. Government can cap it lower (it did in **2023 and 2024**). Compliant increases can't be disputed.
- **Additional increases** need RTB approval (e.g. major repairs or renovations, or an extraordinary rise in operating costs). Being "below market" is **no longer** a ground.
- No increase above the cap **between fixed terms** with the same tenant.
- Where rent varies with occupants, no increase for a **minor** occupant (or one who was a minor at signing) (s.22.1).
- A tenant overcharged can **deduct** the excess from future rent.

### Security deposits

| | **Commercial** | **Residential** |
|---|---|---|
| Amount | **Negotiable**; often last month's rent | **Max ½ month's rent**, collected only **when the agreement is made**; one per unit. Separate **pet damage deposit** also max ½ month. Key/fob deposits allowed. |
| Interest | Negotiable | **Required** (compounded annually, by formula) |
| Return | Per the lease | Within **15 days** after the tenancy ends (with interest), or the landlord applies to the RTB to keep it. Otherwise the tenant can claim **double** (s.38(6)), often by **Direct Request** without a hearing. |
| On a sale | A **personal** obligation of the old landlord, unless the sale contract transfers it (usually with a credit on the statement of adjustments) | **Runs with the land** (s.93): the **buyer** must collect deposits and interest from the seller, because the buyer will have to repay them |

**Condition inspections (ss.23–24).** At the start (and end) of a residential tenancy, landlord and tenant inspect together. The landlord must offer **at least two** opportunities and complete a signed **condition inspection report**. A tenant who doesn't take part loses the right to the deposit's return; a landlord who doesn't comply loses the right to claim against it.

### Distress

**Commercial**: available, and often the landlord's most effective remedy (limited by the *Commercial Tenancy Act* and *Rent Distress Act*). **Residential**: **abolished** (s.26(3)).

### Mitigation

**Commercial.** If a tenant abandons, the landlord can traditionally sue for each month's rent as it falls due, with **no duty to mitigate**. But beware **surrender**:

- **By act of the parties**: tenant offers to end early, landlord accepts (e.g. a 5-year tenant pays two months' rent to walk away after one year).
- **By operation of law**: the landlord does something inconsistent with the tenancy continuing, like re-letting **without** saying it's on the tenant's account. Intention is irrelevant. After surrender, the claim for future rent is lost.

***Highway Properties v. Kelly Douglas*** (SCC 1971): a landlord can **terminate the lease and sue for damages** for the lost benefit of the rest of the term, if it **notifies** the tenant it's doing so.

**Residential.** Both sides have a **statutory duty to mitigate** (s.7(2)). A landlord whose tenant leaves early must try to **re-rent** at a reasonable rent.

> 🧮 John rents from Gordon for a year from December 1 at $500/month and leaves in March. Gordon doesn't bother re-renting. If it's residential, Gordon recovers only losses he **couldn't** have avoided. If he could easily have re-rented at $500, that's only **nominal damages**.

### Quiet enjoyment and privacy

**Commercial.** The lease carries an implied covenant of **quiet enjoyment**. It isn't about noise: it protects the tenant against **title** challenges from the landlord or anyone claiming through them, and against physical interference with normal use. It can be modified, and commercial leases often give the landlord broad rights of entry.

**Residential.** Preserved and expanded by **s.28**: reasonable **privacy**, freedom from **unreasonable disturbance**, **exclusive possession** (subject to s.29), and use of **common areas** free of significant interference.

> 🔎 ***Ashurwin Holdings v. BC*** (2012 BCSC): prolonged renovations with dust, noise, vibration and contractors swearing breached a tenant's quiet enjoyment. Award: about one month's rent (**$700**).

**Landlord entry (s.29).** The landlord may enter only if:

- the tenant consents at the time, or within the previous **30 days**
- **written notice** at least **24 hours** and not more than **30 days** before, giving a **reasonable purpose** and a date and time between **8 a.m. and 9 p.m.** (unless the tenant agrees otherwise)
- housekeeping services under a written agreement
- an RTB order
- the unit is **abandoned**
- an **emergency** threatens life or property

A **monthly inspection** is allowed with proper notice.

> 🧭 **As a listing licensee, s.29 applies to you** (you're the landlord's agent).
>
> - Contact the tenant first and agree a **written schedule** for showings and open houses, plus a way to request urgent access.
> - If the tenant won't cooperate, give **24 hours' written notice**.
> - Avoid **multiple open houses** in a short time (the RTB warns they can breach quiet enjoyment).
> - Photos and video: avoid the tenant's **personal photos and belongings**; consider letting the tenant preview them.
> - Early, open, frequent communication with the tenant makes the sale far smoother.

### Non-derogation from grant

The landlord can't undermine what they leased (e.g. blasting rock next door so hard the tenant's building is damaged). Applies to **both** commercial and residential tenancies.

### Getting possession at the start (interesse termini)

| Commercial | Residential |
|---|---|
| Landlord must let the tenant in, but if it doesn't, the tenant gets **damages only**, not specific performance | Doctrine **abolished**: the tenant can get an **order of possession**, or damages |

### Repairs

**Commercial.**

- **Caveat lessee** ("let the tenant beware"): no landlord duty to repair **unfurnished** premises; the tenant checks suitability before signing. (Furnished premises must be fit for habitation.)
- The tenant must use the premises in a **tenant-like manner** (light bulbs, unblocking sinks), mustn't commit **waste**, and must return them as found, **fair wear and tear excepted**.
- In practice, the **lease** allocates repairs, so read it.

**Residential (s.32).**

- The **landlord** must keep the property in a state of decoration and repair that meets **health, safety and housing standards** and is **suitable for occupation**, given its age, character and location, **even if the tenant knew of problems** when signing. **No contracting out.** An arbitrator can order rent paid to a third party to fund repairs.
- **Emergency repairs (s.33)**: the landlord must post or give the name and number of an emergency contact. Emergencies include major leaks, damaged or blocked water or sewer pipes or fixtures, the primary **heating** system, defective **locks**, and **electrical** systems. If the tenant tries the contact **at least twice** and nothing happens in a reasonable time, the tenant can have the work done and **deduct** the cost from rent (with receipts).
- The **tenant** must maintain reasonable **health, cleanliness and sanitary** standards and repair damage caused by them or their guests, but **not reasonable wear and tear**.

### Assignment and subletting

**Commercial.**

- **Assignment** = transferring the **whole** remaining interest. **Sublease** = leasing for **less** than the full remaining term (part or all of the space). The tenant must keep a **reversion**, typically at least the **last day**, or it's legally an assignment.
  - E.g. a 1-year head lease, sublet for 364 days with re-entry reserved on December 30 = a sublease.
- **Allowed without consent** unless the lease prohibits it.
- Courts read prohibitions **strictly**: "shall not assign" doesn't stop a **sublease**; "shall not sublet" doesn't stop an **assignment**; "shall not assign or sublet **the premises**" may not stop dealing with **part** of them.
- The landlord can forbid, limit, or **charge** for consent, if the lease says so.

**Residential (s.34).** No assignment or sublet without the landlord's **written consent**. For a fixed term of **6 months or more**, consent can't be **unreasonably withheld**. The landlord can **never charge** for consent.

## Cannabis and tenancies

Recreational cannabis has been legal since **October 17, 2018** (federal *Cannabis Act*; BC's *Cannabis Control and Licensing Act* sets the age at **19**, allows **30 g** in public and up to **4 plants** per residence, not visible from public places and not where there's a daycare).

For tenancy agreements made **before** October 17, 2018:

- a term restricting **tobacco** smoking is deemed to restrict **cannabis** smoking the same way (unless cannabis was expressly allowed)
- **growing** cannabis is deemed prohibited, unless it's federally authorized **medical** cannabis not barred by the agreement

For agreements made **on or after** that date, the parties negotiate. Landlords wanting limits must write them in.

## Commercial vs residential summary

| Issue | **Commercial** | **Residential** |
|---|---|---|
| Contract | Lease | Tenancy agreement |
| Main law | Common law + the lease | *Residential Tenancy Act* |
| Fixed term ends | Automatically, no notice | Becomes month-to-month unless a valid vacate clause |
| Ending periodic | **Reasonable** notice, no set form | **Prescribed** grounds, forms and periods |
| Disputes | **Courts** (plus self-help like distress) | **RTB arbitration** (courts in limited cases) |
| Rent increases | Per the lease; end-of-term renegotiation | Once per **12 months**, **3 months'** notice, capped at **CPI**-based maximum |
| Distress | **Allowed** | **Abolished** |
| Security deposit | Negotiable; personal to the landlord | Max **½ month**; interest; return in **15 days** or pay **double**; runs with the land |
| Entry | Per the lease | **24 h–30 days** written notice, **8 a.m.–9 p.m.**, or consent/emergency |
| Assign / sublet | Allowed unless prohibited; consent can cost money | Needs **written consent**; can't unreasonably refuse (6+ month term); **never** a fee |
| Mitigation | No duty (but beware surrender) | **Both** must mitigate |
| Interesse termini | Damages only | Order of possession available |
| Repairs | **Caveat lessee**; tenant-like manner | Landlord must repair, no contracting out; tenant fixes own damage |
| Quiet enjoyment, non-derogation | Yes | Yes (expanded by s.28) |

## Two alerts

> 🚨 **ALERT 1: Check the tenancy before you write the contract.** Before drafting an offer on a tenanted property, find out: **fixed term or periodic? Rent, due date, deposits?** If the buyer wants vacant possession, build in the **Three Month Notice**, a **written request** from the buyer after subjects are removed, completion on the **last day of a rental period**, and who bears the **compensation**. Otherwise your client may be unable to perform.

> 🚨 **ALERT 2: You're the landlord's agent at the door.** When showing tenanted homes, s.29 binds **you**: 24 hours' written notice (or consent), 8 a.m.–9 p.m., a reasonable purpose. Entering without it breaches the tenant's rights and reflects on your licence.

## Trap list: things the exam likes to twist

1. **Exclusive possession** is the key test for a lease vs a licence (*Ocean Harvesters*); labels don't decide it.
2. A **licence** doesn't bind a buyer and gets **no** tenancy-act protection.
3. The RTA **doesn't** apply to shared kitchen/bathroom **with the owner**, co-op members, vacation rentals, or terms **over 20 years**.
4. A shop with a flat above under **one lease** = **commercial**.
5. Commercial leases over **3 years** must be in writing (LEA s.59).
6. Residential agreements: in writing, signed, **8-point** type, copy to tenant within **21 days**.
7. A commercial fixed term **ends automatically**; a residential one becomes **month-to-month** unless a valid vacate clause.
8. Commercial overholding with rent stated **yearly** → **yearly** periodic tenancy; reasonable notice = **6 months** for yearly, one period for monthly (but flexible: 28 days in *Union Properties*).
9. Non-payment notice: effective **10 days** after receipt; **paying within 5 days** cancels it.
10. Dispute deadlines: **5** (rent), **10** (cause), **15** (two-month), **21** (three-month), **30** (four-month) days.
11. Cause notice: at least **one month**, effective the last day of a rental period.
12. Purchaser's use needs: **subjects removed**, buyer is an **individual** (or close family) intending to occupy, and a **written request**. The notice names the buyer.
13. "Close family member" = parent, spouse, child, or spouse's parent or child. **Not** siblings.
14. Landlord/purchaser use can't end a **fixed term** early, and isn't available in non-strata (or single-owner) buildings with **5+** rental units.
15. Compensation: **1 month** always for s.49 notices; **12 months** if the purpose isn't carried out.
16. Renovation evictions need an **RTB order first**; right of first refusal in **5+ unit** buildings.
17. A landlord's notice on the wrong form is **void**; a tenant's notice can be any written form.
18. Deemed receipt: **mail 5 days**; email, fax, door, mailbox **3 days**.
19. RTB arbitrators: decision within **30 days**, **not bound by precedent**, final and enforceable as a Supreme Court order. Penalties up to **$5,000/day**.
20. Residential rent: once per **12 months**, **3 months'** notice, prescribed form, **CPI** cap. A new landlord gets **no** reset. New tenant = new rent.
21. Residential deposit: max **½ month** (plus ½ month pet deposit); return within **15 days** or pay **double**; it **runs with the land** to the buyer.
22. Commercial deposit is **personal** to the old landlord unless the sale contract says otherwise.
23. **Distress**: commercial yes, residential **no**.
24. Commercial landlord has **no duty to mitigate**, but re-letting without notice can be a **surrender**. *Highway Properties*: terminate and sue for lost benefit, with notice.
25. **Quiet enjoyment** is about title and interference, not just noise.
26. Entry: **24 hours to 30 days** written notice, **8 a.m.–9 p.m.**, reasonable purpose.
27. Commercial tenant refused possession: **damages only** (interesse termini); residential: order of possession.
28. Commercial: **caveat lessee**; residential landlord must repair **even known defects**, no contracting out.
29. Emergency repairs: tenant tries the contact **twice**, then may fix and deduct with receipts.
30. "Shall not assign" doesn't bar a **sublease** (strict reading). A sublease must keep a **reversion**.
31. Residential assignment/sublet: written consent; can't unreasonably refuse for **6+ month** fixed terms; **no fee**.
32. Pre-Oct 17, 2018 agreements: tobacco limits extend to **cannabis**; growing deemed prohibited (except authorized medical).

## Drill

**Q1.** A café owner rents a storefront with a one-bedroom apartment above, all under one lease. The landlord wants to raise the rent by 15% at renewal. Does the *Residential Tenancy Act* cap the increase?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**No.** Business premises with an attached dwelling rented under **one lease** are excluded from the RTA. It's a **commercial** tenancy, so the rent at renewal is a matter of negotiation and the lease terms.

</details>

**Q2.** Your buyer client wants to move into a house with a tenant on a month-to-month tenancy (rent due the 1st). The offer is accepted on April 10, subject to financing by April 17. What should the contract and timeline look like?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- Get the tenancy details first (periodic vs fixed term, rent, deposits) and put them in the contract.
- Include terms requiring the **seller** to give the **Three Month Notice for Purchaser's Use** once the buyer removes subjects and gives a **written request**.
- After subject removal (say April 17), the buyer requests notice in writing; the seller serves it (with the buyer's name and address).
- Notice must be at least three months and end on the **last day of a rental period**. Served in April, the earliest effective date is **July 31**, so completion/possession should be **no earlier than July 31**, allowing for deemed-receipt days if not served in person.
- Discuss **one month's compensation** (who bears it) and warn the buyer about **12 months' rent** if they don't actually occupy for at least 12 months.

</details>

**Q3.** A commercial tenant with three years left abandons its warehouse. The landlord immediately re-lets it at a lower rent without telling the tenant. Can the landlord claim the shortfall for the rest of the term?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Risky. Re-letting **without** saying it's on the tenant's account may be a **surrender by operation of law**, ending the landlord's claim for future rent. Under ***Highway Properties***, the landlord should have **notified** the tenant that it was terminating the lease and claiming damages for the lost benefit of the remaining term. (A commercial landlord has no general duty to mitigate, but must manage this carefully.)

</details>

**Q4.** A residential tenant moved out on May 31. On June 20 the landlord still hasn't returned the $900 security deposit or applied to the RTB. What can the tenant do?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

The landlord had **15 days** after the tenancy ended (and the tenant's forwarding address) to return the deposit with interest or apply to keep it. Having done neither, the landlord may owe **double** the deposit (s.38(6)). The tenant can apply to the RTB, often through the **Direct Request** process without an oral hearing. (Note: if the tenant skipped the condition inspections, they may have lost the right to its return.)

</details>

**Q5.** You want to show a tenanted condo tomorrow at 7:30 p.m. The tenant hasn't answered your calls. What can you do?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Without the tenant's consent, you need **written notice at least 24 hours** (and not more than 30 days) before entry, stating a reasonable purpose and a time between **8 a.m. and 9 p.m.** A 7:30 p.m. showing tomorrow works only if notice is given at least 24 hours ahead (counting deemed-receipt days for email, mail or door posting, which add **3–5 days** unless delivered in person). Better: arrange a written showing schedule with the tenant.

</details>

**Q6.** A commercial lease says "the Tenant shall not assign this lease." The tenant wants to sublet half the space for two years of its remaining five. Allowed?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Probably **yes**. Courts read restrictions strictly: a ban on **assignment** doesn't prohibit a **sublease**. Because the tenant keeps a reversion (the rest of the term and half the space), it's a sublease, not an assignment. Check the whole lease for other restrictions, and suggest legal advice.

</details>

## Mindset: two rulebooks, one habit

Residential tenancy law is about **protection**: rules exist because tenants have less power, and you can't contract around them. Commercial leasing is about **negotiation**: the lease **is** the law, and anything not written down falls back on old common-law rules that may surprise both sides.

The habit that serves both: **read the actual agreement, and confirm the facts with the tenant**. Almost every disciplinary case in this area comes from a licensee who assumed a tenancy was month-to-month, or that 30 days' notice would do.

> 🏢 **For commercial specifically.** Leasing is a huge part of commercial practice.
>
> - Learn the **gross vs net** spectrum: when you compare two buildings, the cheaper base rent may cost more once taxes, insurance and maintenance pass through.
> - Every important right (renewal options, assignment and sublet, repairs, overholding, landlord entry, deposits) exists **only if the lease says so**, or falls back to default rules like **caveat lessee** and **free assignability**.
> - When selling a tenanted commercial building, confirm each tenant's lease terms (often with **estoppel certificates** in practice), and decide in the contract who takes over the **security deposits**: they don't automatically pass to the buyer.
> - Leases over **3 years** must be in writing; long leases should also be **registered** on title to protect the tenant against a later buyer.

> **This week's habit.** Download the RTB's **Three Month Notice for Landlord's Use** and the standard **tenancy agreement**. Read them start to finish. Then find one real commercial lease (many are online) and identify: base rent, how operating costs are shared, the term and renewal options, and the assignment clause.
