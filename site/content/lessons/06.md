## Why this lesson is about protecting you

> **Scenario.** You're representing a buyer on a $3.1M warehouse. In one week:
>
> 1. The listing says "**18,500 sq ft**, information believed accurate but not guaranteed". Your buyer is pricing the deal per square foot. You haven't measured it.
> 2. The buyer's numbered company is paying the deposit, but the funds arrive by wire from a **different company** in another country. When you ask who owns the numbered company, the buyer says, "Does it matter?"
> 3. You realize a warehouse you **part-own** with a friend would suit this buyer perfectly.
>
> Each of these is a way to get **sued**, **disciplined**, **fined by FINTRAC**, or all three.

| Situation | The risk | Section |
|---|---|---|
| Passing on square footage you haven't checked | **Negligent misrepresentation** | Misrepresentation |
| Funds from an unexplained third party, vague ownership | **Money laundering**: you must identify the buyer, find the **beneficial owners**, and consider a **suspicious transaction report** | FINTRAC |
| Showing your own warehouse to your client | **Conflict of interest** with your duty of **loyalty** | Ethics |

Lessons 4 and 5 covered the rules BCFSA enforces. This lesson covers the three other places trouble comes from: **the courts** (lawsuits), **FINTRAC** (anti-money-laundering), and your own **professional judgment** (ethics).

## Vocabulary first

| Word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| **Liability** | Being legally responsible for someone's loss, so you (or your insurer) pay. |
| **Negligence** | Carelessness the law punishes: you owed someone care, fell short, and it caused a foreseeable loss. |
| **Standard of care** | How careful you're expected to be. For licensees: what a **competent real estate professional** would do. |
| **Misrepresentation** | A false statement of fact that someone relies on. |
| **Deceit** | A **knowingly or recklessly** false statement made to trick someone. Fraud, in tort form. |
| **Vicarious liability** | The employer (your brokerage) is also liable for your wrongs done in the ordinary course of your work. |
| **Fiduciary** | Someone in a position of trust who must act **solely** for the other person's benefit. |
| **Money laundering** | Making criminal money look legitimate ("dirty" to "clean"). |
| **FINTRAC** | Canada's financial intelligence agency. Collects reports, analyzes them, and enforces anti-money-laundering rules. |
| **PCMLTFA** | The federal *Proceeds of Crime (Money Laundering) and Terrorist Financing Act*. The law behind FINTRAC. |
| **Reporting entity** | A business the PCMLTFA requires to identify clients, keep records and report to FINTRAC. Includes trading licensees. |
| **Beneficial owner** | The real person behind an entity. For FINTRAC: anyone owning or controlling **25% or more**. |
| **STR** | A Suspicious Transaction Report filed with FINTRAC. |
| **PEP / HIO** | Politically exposed person / head of an international organization. People with higher corruption risk. |
| **Ethics** | Moral principles about how you **ought** to act. Wider than what the law requires. |
| **Conflict of interest** | A real risk that your own interest, or a duty to someone else, will weaken your duty to a client. |

## Part 1: How licensees get sued

Courts see licensees as **professionals** with special skill. The standard isn't perfection, but it's close. E&O claims data shows four main sources of liability:

1. **Negligence**
2. **Misrepresentation** (the most common)
3. **Errors in drafting** agreements
4. **Breach of fiduciary duty**

On top of lawsuits, the same conduct can bring **BCFSA discipline** (penalties up to $250,000, suspension, losing your commission) and **board discipline** under the REALTOR® Code. A board can't touch your licence, but it can **expel** you, which cuts off **MLS®** access.

### Negligence: the three-part test

A plaintiff must prove **all three**:

| Element | Plain English |
|---|---|
| 1. **Duty of care** | You owed them care: they were someone you could reasonably foresee being affected, and there was a close and direct relationship ("proximity"). |
| 2. **Breach** | You fell below the standard a reasonable person (here, a competent licensee) would meet. Higher risk means a higher standard. |
| 3. **Damage not too remote** | The kind of loss was reasonably foreseeable from what you did. |

Miss any one and the claim fails. The classic teaching example: railway staff dislodge a passenger's harmless-looking package, it turns out to be fireworks, and the blast knocks scales onto a woman at the far end of the platform. No duty was owed to her, because nobody could have foreseen it, so no negligence.

**Common ways licensees have been found negligent:**

- Incompetent advice on **value**
- Not advising a buyer to include a **subject to financing** clause
- Missing problems with the **water supply**
- Not disclosing facts about the **buyer's ability to pay**
- Getting **boundaries** wrong
- Not verifying **listing information**
- Not doing a **title search**
- Giving misleading **rental income** estimates

> 🔎 **Disclaimers have limits.** In *Koerber v. Salmon* (BC Prov Ct, 2018), an MLS® listing with wrong square footage said only "believed correct but not guaranteed". The court said that **general** disclaimer didn't stop the buyer's claim. Make disclaimers **specific**, for example: *"Measurements should not be relied on without independent verification."* And **no** disclaimer protects you if you **know** the information is wrong.

**If you're the buyer's agent** and can't verify the seller's information: tell the buyer to hire someone who can, and consider a **subject clause** that puts the risk on the seller.

*Shaw v. Thody* (2014 BCSC): buyers said their agent should have asked about **landslide risk**. The case failed only because the expert evidence didn't pin down the standard of care. Lesson: when you don't know an area, **ask experts**.

### Vicarious liability

Your **brokerage** is liable for your wrongs committed **in the ordinary course of your work**, even fraud. Courts treat representatives like employees. If the wrong happens outside your work (the classic case: an employee crashes the dairy's truck on a family trip to the movies), the employer isn't liable.

### Misrepresentation and deceit

Two kinds:

| | **Negligent misrepresentation** | **Deceit / fraudulent misrepresentation** |
|---|---|---|
| State of mind | Careless, **no intent** to deceive | **Knew** it was false, or **reckless** about whether it was true |
| Who can be liable | Usually someone with **special skill** (like a licensee) | **Anyone** |
| Moral fault | Carelessness | Fraud |

**The five-part test for negligent misrepresentation** (*Queen v. Cognos*, SCC 1993):

1. A **special relationship** creating a duty of care
2. The statement was **untrue, inaccurate or misleading**
3. The person made it **negligently**
4. The listener **reasonably relied** on it
5. The reliance caused **damage**

A special relationship arises when an expert gives advice in their business to someone who will reasonably rely on it. The expert has three options: **refuse** to advise, advise **with a clear disclaimer**, or advise **without one** and take full responsibility for getting it right.

*Holt v. Thompson* (2006 BCSC): a licensee assured a condo buyer the building **didn't leak**. A week after completion came a water-damage assessment. The licensee was liable: she knew or ought to have known.

**Practical habits:**

- Ask yourself: **do I personally know this**, or did someone tell me? If it's second-hand, give it **in writing with the source**.
- The seller's **Property Disclosure Statement** helps but isn't armour. If you knew or ought to have known it was wrong, you're exposed.
- You can also be liable **to your seller** if you fail to disclose something that later gives the buyer a claim against them.
- Before giving an **opinion**, assume it will be **relied on**. Are you competent to give it? Do you have the facts? Should you refer them to an expert instead?
- Courts accept that licensees **aren't structural experts** (*Arthur v. Greyfriars Realty*). Offer opinions outside your field and you invite a lawsuit.

> 🔑 **Rules ss.49–50: promises to win business.** If you promise something to get a listing or make a sale ("I'll sell it for $500,000 in 3 weeks", "I'll pay $500 toward the electrical inspection"), you must give the person a **signed written statement** with **all the details**. Promises are allowed; **undocumented** promises are misconduct. A licensee was disciplined for exactly the $500 inspection promise.

> 💡 **Clients who want to talk directly to the other side.** They can, but warn them: it exposes them to misrepresentation claims and leaves you not knowing who said what. It's safer for professionals to handle all communication.

### Errors in drafting

You must know basic **contract law** (Lesson 7) and draft in **simple, accurate, unambiguous** terms. No jargon, abbreviations or vague phrases. When in doubt, add detail or get legal advice.

*Russell v. Wispinski* (1987 BCSC): an amendment about bridge financing was drafted with blanks and irrelevant terms. Once the sellers initialled it, the court treated it as a **counter-offer**, the buyer rejected it, and the deal died. The licensees were **negligent**: clear drafting would have closed the sale.

### Breach of fiduciary duty

Your client relationship is **agency**, which makes you a **fiduciary**. The core duty is **loyalty**. The others: avoid and disclose **conflicts**, **full disclosure**, **confidentiality**, and **obey lawful instructions**.

One twist: when a client alleges breach of fiduciary duty, **you** have to prove you fulfilled your duties. The burden flips.

> 🏢 ***Ocean City Realty v. A & M Holdings* (BCCA 1987)** is a commercial case worth knowing. A licensee selling a building agreed to the **buyer's** demand to split her commission. She told her managing broker, but **not the seller**. The brokerage **lost its entire commission**. It didn't matter whether she personally benefited. Any fact that could influence the client's decisions must be disclosed.

## Part 2: Money laundering and FINTRAC

### What it is and why real estate attracts it

**Money laundering** disguises where criminal money came from. It's a *Criminal Code* offence (s.462.31) to deal with property intending to conceal or convert it, knowing, believing **or being reckless** that it came from crime.

It's **not mostly about bags of cash**. Most dirty money is already in the banking system before it reaches a real estate deal.

**Scale:** an expert panel estimated **$7.4 billion** laundered in BC in 2018 (of $46.7B in Canada), possibly raising BC home prices about **5%**. Laundering through Vancouver casinos and real estate became known internationally as the "**Vancouver Model**".

**Why real estate?** High value · stable and hard to steal · simple to buy · tends to appreciate · many ways to do it · **subjective prices** (easy to over- or under-state) · perceived weak oversight · **anonymity** through companies, trusts and nominees · flipping is normal · hot markets hide overpaying.

### The three stages

| Stage | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Placement** | Get the cash into the financial system | "Smurfing": many small deposits to stay under reporting limits; casino chips cashed out |
| **2. Layering** | Move it around to hide the trail | Chains of purchases, sales, transfers and conversions |
| **3. Integration** | Bring it back as "legitimate" money | Paying cash for renovations, then selling at a "profit" |

### Common real estate schemes

- **Mortgages:** pay down with dirty money; take and quickly discharge a series of mortgages; **loan-back** (lending yourself your own dirty money through a company); acting as an **unregulated lender**; inflated income documents.
- **Rentals:** inflate rent received; or give dirty money to tenants who pay rent with it and repay clean money.
- **Construction and renovation** paid in dirty money, then sold at a gain.
- **Criminal operations** needing property (grow-ops, illegal casinos).
- **Valuation games:** overpay, or underpay on paper with cash under the table.
- **No financing**, so no lender asks questions.
- **Aborted purchases:** deposit, collapse, refund from trust (you saw this in Lesson 5).
- **HBRP abuse:** offers on several properties through different licensees, rescind each, get 99.75% back clean from trust accounts.
- **Straw buyers** and **rapid flips** through layers of companies.

### The legal framework

- **FATF** (Financial Action Task Force) set international standards from 1989; its mandate grew after 9/11 to include terrorist financing.
- Canada's **PCMLTFA** has four goals: detect and deter laundering and terrorist financing, support investigation and prosecution, meet international commitments, and protect the financial system.
- **Reporting entities** include real estate **brokers and representatives acting in purchases and sales**, plus developers, mortgage brokers, accountants, notaries, casinos and others. Licensees who **only** do rental or strata management **aren't** reporting entities.
- **FINTRAC** is independent and at **arm's length from police**. It collects and analyzes reports, discloses intelligence to law enforcement, checks compliance, and raises public awareness. It also covers **sanctions evasion**.
- In **2019**, BC's real estate regulator signed a first-in-Canada **information-sharing MOU** with FINTRAC. BCFSA can treat PCMLTFA breaches as **conduct unbecoming**: one licensee who let a buyer wire money into her **personal account** under another name got **$7,500**, a **270-day suspension** and an ethics course.

### Your four key obligations

**1. Compliance program** (the brokerage's job; you follow it). Five parts:

1. A **compliance officer** (often the managing broker, but doesn't have to be)
2. Written, current **policies and procedures**, approved by a senior officer
3. A documented **risk assessment** (clients, services, location, new technology)
4. A written **training program**
5. A **review every 2 years**

> ⚠️ Compliance isn't "a brokerage thing". **Every** trading licensee is a reporting entity and must follow the program. When in doubt, ask the compliance officer.

**2. Know Your Client (KYC).** You must verify identity when you act in a purchase or sale. **Six triggers:**

1. **Suspicious transaction** (verify before filing, using reasonable measures)
2. **Large cash**: **$10,000+** in one transaction, or in total within **24 hours** from or for the same person
3. **Large virtual currency**: same **$10,000 / 24-hour** test
4. **Receipt of funds** in any form (you identify **your own** client; the other side's licensee identifies theirs)
5. **Information records**
6. **Unrepresented parties**: every licensee in the deal verifies the unrepresented person

**Three ways to verify an individual:**

| Method | How |
|---|---|
| **Government photo ID** | Authentic, valid, current, compared to the person. Remote only with software that checks security features. **No** video-call viewing, **no** photocopies. |
| **Credit file** | Canadian credit file at least **3 years** old; name, address and date of birth match. |
| **Dual process** | **Two** of: name + address from a reliable source; name + date of birth from a reliable source; name + confirmation of a bank, credit card or loan account. |

**Entities** (corporations, trusts, partnerships): verify with records such as a certificate of incorporation or active status, plus the corporation's **name, address and directors**.

**Third party determination:** take reasonable measures to find out whether your client is acting **for someone else** (required in purchases/sales and large cash transactions), and **document** what you did. Example: a client with a modest job buying a high-value property with little financing.

**Beneficial ownership:** whenever you verify an entity:

1. **Get** the directors' names and the names and addresses of everyone owning or controlling **25%+**, directly or indirectly (trace through layers of companies to the actual people).
2. **Take reasonable measures to confirm** it's accurate, from a signed confirmation up to registry searches, depending on risk.

For a **federal** corporation in a **high-risk** situation, you must check the **Corporations Canada** registry and report material discrepancies. If you can't get or confirm the information, verify the **CEO** (or equivalent) and apply **high-risk** measures. Keep beneficial ownership records **5 years** after the last transaction.

> 🧮 **Layer math.** HoldCo owns 90% of PropCo. Rochelle owns 90% of HoldCo. Rochelle's indirect share of PropCo = 90% × 90% = **81%**, well over 25%. She's a beneficial owner even though her name isn't on PropCo's share register.

**PEPs and HIOs:** take reasonable measures at the start of a business relationship to determine whether a client is a PEP, HIO, or their family member or close associate. Also required when you receive **$100,000+** in cash or virtual currency.

**Business relationship:** starts the **first time** you must verify someone's identity. Then you must:

- **Record its purpose** (the **DORTS** form usually covers this), and
- Do **ongoing monitoring** based on risk (e.g. yearly for low risk, every 3–6 months for high risk).

It ends **5 years** after the last transaction that required verification.

**3. Reporting.** Three reports:

| Report | When |
|---|---|
| **Terrorist Property Report** | **Without delay**, if you know or believe property you hold belongs to or is controlled by a terrorist or terrorist group. Also tell the **RCMP** and **CSIS**. |
| **Large Cash Transaction Report** | Within **15 calendar days**. (Many brokerages refuse cash to avoid this.) Same idea for **large virtual currency**. |
| **Suspicious Transaction Report (STR)** | **As soon as practicable** once you have **reasonable grounds to suspect**. **No dollar minimum.** Covers attempted transactions too. |

**The suspicion ladder:**

| Level | Meaning | Must you file? |
|---|---|---|
| Simple suspicion | A hunch | No, but ask questions |
| **Reasonable grounds to suspect** | A **possibility**, for reasons another trained person would likely share | **Yes, file an STR** |
| Reasonable grounds to believe | A **probability** backed by verified facts | (Higher than you need) |

You **don't** need proof. Filing isn't an accusation; it tells FINTRAC that the pieces add up to a possibility. Base it on **facts**, **context** and **indicators**, and keep detailed notes.

**Red flags** (indicators, not proof):

- **General:** companies, trusts, nominees; no financing; unregulated lenders; foreign ownership.
- **Behaviour:** arrives with lots of cash; over-explains; unusually worried about your reporting; doesn't care about costs, risks or commission; pays renovation bills in cash.
- **Atypical deals:** price far above or below market; no inspection; quick resale at a very different price; buying back recently sold property; "record a lower price, I'll pay the rest under the table"; many properties fast with little interest in condition; luxury build in a poor location; anonymous payment instruments; unexplained rush.
- **Financial profile:** finances that can't be documented; buying for minors or people without the means.
- **Other parties:** won't put their name on documents; last-minute buyer swap; buying in someone else's name; deposit from an unrelated third party (not spouse or parent); large cash down payment plus unusual financing; an entity with no real business; fully anonymous deals through a lawyer's trust account.

**Ask questions.** A simple, natural question often resolves it, or reveals more. It also shows clients you're a professional, not an order-taker, and puts off people who are laundering.

> 🚨 **Don't tip them off.** It's an offence to disclose that you've filed (or will file) an STR with intent to harm an investigation. You **can** discuss it with your **compliance officer or managing broker**.

STRs are filed electronically. **Part G** is the key section: describe **why** you're suspicious, objectively. Not "the client was evasive", but "when asked for ID, the client changed the subject several times and never produced it".

**4. Record keeping.** Records must be producible to FINTRAC within **30 days** of a request, and kept by the brokerage for **at least 5 years**:

1. STRs and other reports
2. Large cash transaction records
3. Large virtual currency records
4. **Information records** on every party you act for, and every unrepresented party
5. **Receipt of funds** records (including money from someone else on the client's behalf, like a parent, whose identity you also verify)

### Penalties

- **Criminal:** up to **$2 million** and/or **5 years** in prison.
- **Administrative monetary penalties (AMPs):** up to **$100,000** per violation for individuals and **$500,000** for entities. **All AMPs are published, with names.**
- Recent BC examples: one brokerage paid **$206,250** for missing policies, a weak program and no risk assessment; another paid **$282,397.50** for seven violations including **not filing STRs**.

### Land Owner Transparency Act (LOTA)

BC's answer to hidden ownership (a study found **61.4%** of real estate laundering cases involved **nominees**).

- Registering a fee simple, life estate, lease over **10 years**, or certain agreement-for-sale rights requires a **transparency declaration**.
- If the new owner is a **reporting body** (most private companies, trustees of trusts, partners), it must also file a **transparency report** listing **interest holders**. For a corporation, that's anyone with **10%+** of shares or votes, or the power to control the board.
- The **Land Owner Transparency Registry (LOTR)** is separate from the land title register and **doesn't confirm ownership**. Public searches by PID or name; some details withheld; publication delayed **90 days**.
- **Lawyers and notaries** prepare these filings, **not licensees**. Refer LOTA questions to them.
- A LOTR search can help **KYC**: a "first-time buyer" who turns out to beneficially own several properties deserves more questions.

> 🔑 **Watch the two percentages.** FINTRAC beneficial owner = **25%**. LOTA corporate interest holder = **10%**. Exams love to swap them.

## Part 3: Climate risk

Floods, wildfires, heat, storms and earthquakes are getting more frequent. That can change **values**, **insurability**, **financing** and **construction costs**. A buyer near an active wildfire may not get insurance, which means no mortgage, which can mean a failed completion and a lost deposit.

**Protect your clients by:**

- Reading title charges closely, especially **s.219 covenants** about flooding or geotechnical hazards
- Knowing the local hazards in your market
- Referring clients to **insurers, mortgage brokers, lawyers and engineers**
- Using **subject clauses** for due diligence
- Telling **sellers to keep their insurance** until completion is confirmed
- Knowing which features reduce risk (e.g. fire-resistant landscaping and materials)

Protect **your own business** too: business interruption and property insurance, and a plan to keep working through a disaster.

## Part 4: Professionalism and ethics

### What makes a profession

Professionals generally:

- Put the **public's and clients' interests first**
- Have valued **expertise**
- Meet **entry and ongoing education** requirements
- Stay **objective**
- Follow **conduct rules** built to protect the public
- Are **accountable**, and hold **each other** accountable

**Professionalism** = overall competence: **skills, judgment and behaviour**. **Ethics** is one part of it: **moral principles** about how you ought to act.

**Letter vs spirit of the law.** Finding a technical loophole may keep you "compliant" while defeating the rule's purpose (protecting the public, fairness, transparency). Ethics asks you to honour the **spirit**.

### Conduct unbecoming reaches your private life

BCFSA can discipline **conduct unbecoming** anywhere: conduct that hurts the public interest, undermines confidence, or disgraces the industry. Two examples:

- A licensee **bragging** in a café about "technically complying" while dodging a rule's purpose damages public confidence.
- A licensee with a past impaired-driving conviction who's **charged again** may raise fitness concerns. Remember **RESA s.10** (good reputation) and **Rules s.23** (report charges and convictions).

### Trust, honesty and integrity

**Trust** is a profession's most valuable and most fragile asset, especially as consumers can increasingly **do it themselves** with technology. Trust rests on **honesty** and **integrity**.

**Honesty** (Rules **s.33**; RESA s.35 deceptive dealing; s.118 false statements) means:

- Transparency (within confidentiality)
- Clear information
- Not withholding to mislead
- No deception
- **Realistic expectations** (price, rent, time to sell)
- **Following through on promises** (document inducements under s.49)
- Being truthful about your **expertise**: careful with "expert" or "specialist", and honest about unfamiliar areas or property types

**Integrity** means doing the right thing **whether or not anyone's watching**:

- Professional behaviour; don't disparage colleagues or the regulator
- **Objectivity**: no bias, conflicts or pressure clouding judgment
- **Fair dealing**: no bullying or manipulation; **no discrimination**
- Competence, and knowing your limits
- Confidentiality
- Due care
- **Accountability**: own your mistakes, tell your managing broker, and **never** pay or pressure someone to drop a BCFSA complaint

> 💡 **The headline test.** Would you be comfortable seeing this decision as a news headline or a viral post? If not, don't do it.

> 🚨 **Discriminatory instructions.** Sellers who want to sell only to buyers "like us" are asking you to break the ***Human Rights Code***. Explain you can't screen offers or showings that way, that you must **present every offer**, and that narrowing the buyer pool can cost them money. Ask your managing broker for help with the conversation.

### Where your duties to clients come from

| Source | Duties |
|---|---|
| **Contract** | Do what your service agreement says |
| **Tort** | Reasonable care and skill (don't be negligent) |
| **Agency / fiduciary** | Perform personally (don't delegate without permission); loyalty and the other fiduciary duties |
| **Rules s.30** | Client duties (below) |
| **Rules ss.33–34** | Honesty and reasonable care, owed to **clients and non-clients** |
| **RESA s.35** | Professional misconduct and conduct unbecoming |

Breaking one source often breaks several: **discipline** from BCFSA and a **lawsuit** from the client.

### Rules s.30: your duties to clients

| Section | Duty | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **30(a)** | Act in the client's **best interests** | The most important. Doesn't require unlawful acts or conflicts (e.g. lending a client money). |
| **30(b)** | Follow **lawful** instructions | Get instructions in writing, or confirm verbal ones by email or text. |
| **30(c)** | Act only within your **authority** | You need **written authority to sign** for a client (Rules **s.45**). |
| **30(d)** | Advise **independent professional advice** on matters outside your expertise | Tax (especially **GST**) has caused several discipline cases. |
| **30(e)** | **Confidentiality** | Lasts **forever**, even after the relationship ends. |
| **30(f)** | Disclose all known **material information** | Anything that might affect value or the client's decision. **You** don't decide what's relevant. |
| **30(g)** | Communicate **all offers** promptly and objectively | Unless the client instructs otherwise. Get that instruction in writing and re-confirm it. |
| **30(h)** | **Reasonable efforts to discover relevant facts** | About property the client is considering buying. A frequent discipline issue. |
| **30(i)–(j)** | **Avoid** conflicts; if one exists, disclose it **promptly, fully, in writing**, **separately** from any service or trade agreement | |

**Confidentiality traps:**

- **Former client:** you resigned because a seller wouldn't let you disclose mould. Later, a buyer client likes that house. You **can't** reveal the mould, and you shouldn't represent that buyer on that property.
- **Good-faith breach:** an estranged daughter begs for her elderly mother's new address. Not your call. Offer to pass the **daughter's** contact details to the mother instead.

### Conflicts of interest

A conflict = a **substantial risk** that your representation will be harmed by **your own interest** or your **duties to someone else** (another current client, a former client, a third party). Having a conflict isn't automatically wrong. Failing to **avoid or manage** it is.

**Examples:**

- Rushing a sale before your vacation
- A renovation company offering you **referral fees** for your commercial buyers
- Two buyer clients wanting the same house
- Your buyer client liking your seller client's listing
- A friend wanting preferential lease terms on your listing
- Commission itself: you only get paid if it closes

**Case (2024 BCSRE 1):** a licensee **lent a client $50,000** to complete a purchase and didn't disclose the conflict. Result: **$5,000** administrative penalty, **$22,958.18** in enforcement expenses, and two courses.

**Managing conflicts:**

- Anticipate them, especially **potential** conflicts.
- Talk with clients **early and often**.
- Use the **reasonable, informed third party** test.
- Remember **disclosure and consent don't end your duties**. If you can't serve the client loyally, you **can't continue**.

> 🏢 **The warehouse dilemma (commercial).** You're a buyer's agent and part-own a warehouse that fits the brief. Option 1: never mention it. That avoids the conflict but may not serve the client. Option 2: tell the client; if they're interested, **end the agency** and have them hire a new agent. You might lose the client, but that's the price of integrity. Either way, the Rules' **disclosure requirements** for a licensee's own property apply.

### s.34 and "reasonableness"

Rules **s.34** imports the negligence standard: **reasonable care and skill**, to clients and non-clients. "Reasonable" means what a **competent real estate professional** would do. Two self-checks:

- What would a **reasonable, informed third party** think of my effort?
- Would a **panel of five experienced managing brokers** say I acted appropriately?

Not knowing enough, or not being careful enough, **isn't an excuse**. Falling short of your duties **is** an ethical lapse.

### Duties to third parties

You don't owe **fiduciary** duties to non-clients, but you still owe them **honesty (s.33)**, **reasonable care (s.34)**, and conduct that isn't "unbecoming". Never sabotage the other side.

### Disclosure: early, continuous, full, written

| | |
|---|---|
| **Early** | As soon as practicable, so decisions are well informed |
| **Continuous** | Update as things change |
| **Full** | Don't summarize away key facts. **When in doubt, disclose.** Explain again if it isn't understood. |
| **Written** | Use the prescribed forms; follow verbal disclosures with written confirmation |

### The duty to report misconduct

**Why licensees must report:**

1. You're the **boots on the ground**.
2. Many consumers **never complain**.
3. Complaints come **after the harm**.
4. **Peer pressure** works.

Lawyers, accountants and health professionals all have similar duties.

**Under the Rules (s.29):** representatives and associate brokers must **promptly** tell their **managing broker** about conduct they think may be **professional misconduct**, **conduct unbecoming**, or **improper or negligent** conduct. This covers your **own** conduct, people acting for you, and **anyone in your brokerage**, including unlicensed staff and breaches of **other laws** (e.g. anti-spam rules).

**Outside your brokerage:** there's no explicit Rule, but there's an **ethical duty** to report misconduct by any licensee or brokerage employee. Start with your managing broker.

**Not sure? Ask:**

- Has a consumer been harmed?
- Is harm likely?
- Is the licensee benefiting without proper disclosure?
- Do they seem unaware of the problem, or unwilling to fix it?

Any "yes" means **report**. When in doubt, **report**.

**Go straight to BCFSA when:**

- There's an urgent risk (e.g. theft of client money).
- Your managing broker is unavailable and it's serious.
- You're concerned about how your managing broker is handling it.
- The **managing broker** is the problem.
- You'd simply be more comfortable doing so.

BCFSA's **anonymous tip line** works too, but give enough detail, because they can't follow up with you.

> 🚨 **Never weaponize reporting.** Threatening to report someone to gain an advantage may be **extortion**. And **retaliating** against someone who reported you is itself conduct unbecoming: a licensee who left a voicemail threatening never to process a colleague's offers again after she complained was disciplined.

**Managing brokers (Rules s.28)** must take reasonable steps to deal with misconduct. Options include:

- Getting BCFSA guidance
- Talking with those involved, affected consumers or legal counsel
- Reporting to BCFSA
- Ending the licensee's relationship with the brokerage
- Updating policies or training
- Offering compensation

**Compensating the client doesn't cancel the duty to report.**

### Other codes

- The **REALTOR® Code** (CREA): Preamble, Code of Ethics (ending with the **Golden Rule**), and **29 articles** of Standards of Business Practice. It can demand **more** than the law, but **the law wins** if they conflict.
- Others: the **REIBC** and **REIC** codes, **PAMA**, and the **Strata Property Agents of BC**.
- Courts may use these codes to help set the **standard of care**.

### The five-step ethical decision framework

1. **Consider the situation**: facts, ethical issues, legal and ethical obligations.
2. **Identify stakeholders**: your client and your duties to them, others you owe duties to, who's affected, who to consult.
3. **List feasible alternatives**, short- and long-term.
4. **Evaluate each one**: Rules compliance, broader ethics, and what if the facts change?
5. **Choose and justify**: can you defend it? Who needs to know? Any reporting duty? **Document your reasoning.**

## Two alerts

> 🚨 **ALERT 1: Second-hand facts need a source and a disclaimer.** If you didn't verify it yourself (square footage, zoning, rents, building condition), say where it came from, in writing, with a **specific** disclaimer, and send the client to an expert.

> 🚨 **ALERT 2: Reasonable grounds to suspect = file.** You don't need proof. Don't tip off the client. Talk to your compliance officer, and file the STR **as soon as practicable**.

## Trap list: things the exam likes to twist

1. **Negligence** needs **all three**: duty, breach, damage not too remote.
2. **Misrepresentation** is the most common basis of liability against licensees.
3. Negligent misrepresentation has **five** elements (*Cognos*), starting with a **special relationship**.
4. **Deceit** needs knowledge or **recklessness**, and **anyone** can commit it. Negligent misrepresentation generally involves someone with **special skill**.
5. **General** disclaimers may fail (*Koerber*). **No** disclaimer protects a licensee who **knows** the information is false.
6. **Vicarious liability** applies only to acts in the **ordinary course** of work.
7. In a fiduciary claim, the **agent** must prove they fulfilled their duties.
8. *Ocean City Realty*: undisclosed commission split = **entire commission forfeited**, even with no personal benefit.
9. **Rules ss.49–50**: inducement promises need a **signed written statement** with all details.
10. Real estate boards can **expel** (no MLS®) but **can't suspend your licence**.
11. Only licensees **acting in purchases and sales** are FINTRAC reporting entities, not pure rental or strata managers.
12. Compliance program: **5** elements; reviewed every **2 years**.
13. **Large cash/virtual currency** = **$10,000+** in one transaction or within **24 hours**. Large Cash Transaction Report within **15 calendar days**.
14. STRs: **no dollar threshold**, filed **as soon as practicable**, include **attempted** transactions.
15. "Reasonable grounds to **suspect**" = a **possibility**. "Reasonable grounds to **believe**" = a **probability**.
16. Photo ID can't be checked by **video call** or from a **photocopy**. Credit file must be **3+ years** old. Dual process = **two** sources.
17. FINTRAC beneficial owner = **25%**. LOTA interest holder (corporations) = **10%**.
18. PEP/HIO check also triggered by **$100,000+** cash or virtual currency.
19. Records: producible within **30 days**; kept **5 years**.
20. FINTRAC AMPs: **$100K** individual / **$500K** entity per violation, **always published**. Criminal: **$2M** and/or **5 years**.
21. LOTR **doesn't confirm ownership**. **Lawyers/notaries**, not licensees, file LOTA documents.
22. **Confidentiality lasts forever**; most other fiduciary duties end with the relationship.
23. Conflicts must be disclosed **in writing**, **separately** from service and trade agreements.
24. **Disclosure and consent don't cure** a conflict you can't manage.
25. The Rules' duty to report (s.29) covers your **brokerage** and goes to your **managing broker**. Outside the brokerage it's an **ethical** duty.
26. **Compensating** a harmed client **doesn't remove** the duty to report.
27. If the REALTOR® Code and the law conflict, **the law wins**.

## Drill

**Q1.** A seller tells you their office building is "about 22,000 sq ft". You put that in the listing with "information believed accurate but not guaranteed". A buyer pays $300 per sq ft and later finds it's 19,800. What's your exposure, and what should you have done?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Exposure to a **negligent misrepresentation** claim: you're a skilled professional, the figure was inaccurate, you didn't verify it, the buyer reasonably relied on it, and they lost about 2,200 × $300 = **$660,000** in value. A **general** disclaimer like yours was found ineffective in *Koerber v. Salmon*. You also risk BCFSA discipline under **s.34** (reasonable care).

Better: measure or get a professional measurement, or state the **source** in writing with a **specific** disclaimer ("measurements provided by the seller; buyer to verify independently"), and suggest the buyer's agent add a subject for verification.

</details>

**Q2.** Name the three elements of negligence, and the five elements of negligent misrepresentation.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Negligence:** (1) a duty of care, (2) breach of the standard of care, (3) damage that isn't too remote (it was reasonably foreseeable).

**Negligent misrepresentation** (*Cognos*): (1) a special relationship creating a duty of care, (2) an untrue, inaccurate or misleading statement, (3) made negligently, (4) reasonable reliance, (5) resulting damage.

</details>

**Q3.** Your buyer client's deposit of $250,000 arrives as a wire from a company in another country that has nothing to do with the buyer. The buyer won't explain. What are your FINTRAC obligations?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Receipt of funds**: verify the identity of whoever paid the funds and keep a **receipt of funds record**.
- **Third party determination**: take reasonable measures to find out whether the buyer is acting for someone else, and document them.
- If the buyer or payer is an entity: get and confirm **beneficial ownership** (25%+), tracing through the layers.
- An unrelated third-party payer plus refusal to explain are **red flags**. If, with the context, you have **reasonable grounds to suspect**, file an **STR as soon as practicable**. No proof needed, no dollar threshold.
- **Don't tip off** the client. Talk to your compliance officer or managing broker.
- Keep all records for **5 years**.

</details>

**Q4.** A client pays you $12,000 in cash: $6,000 in the morning and $6,000 that evening. What's triggered?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Two cash payments from the same person within **24 hours** totalling **$10,000+** = a **large cash transaction**. You must verify their identity, do a **third party determination**, keep a **large cash transaction record**, and file a **Large Cash Transaction Report** within **15 calendar days**. (Many brokerages simply refuse cash.) Splitting payments to stay under $10,000 could itself be a red flag.

</details>

**Q5.** You're acting for a buyer. Your own seller client's listing is perfect for them. What must you do?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

This is a **conflict of interest**: you owe loyalty to two clients on opposite sides. Under Rules **s.30(i)–(j)**, take reasonable steps to avoid it, and if it exists, disclose it **promptly, fully and in writing**, **separately** from any service or purchase agreement. Then follow the agency rules on how (or whether) you can continue (covered in Lesson 9). Remember that disclosure doesn't relieve you of your duties: if you can't act in each client's best interests, you can't continue for both. Keep your managing broker informed.

</details>

**Q6.** A colleague at another brokerage tells you they're "holding" a buyer's deposit in their own bank account until subjects are removed. What should you do?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

That's a serious trust breach and a risk to the public. There's no explicit **Rules s.29** duty for licensees at another brokerage, but you have an **ethical duty** to report. Start with your **managing broker**. Given the risk to client money, it's reasonable to report **directly to BCFSA** (or through the anonymous tip line, with enough detail) if it can't wait. Don't threaten the colleague, and don't use the information for advantage.

</details>

**Q7.** Your seller client says: "I've decided I'll only accept offers from buyers with no kids. Don't show it to families." Respond using the ethical framework.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

1. **Situation:** the instruction is likely discriminatory (family status) under the *Human Rights Code*, so it isn't a **lawful** instruction (s.30(b)), and following it would be conduct unbecoming.
2. **Stakeholders:** the seller (your client), prospective buyers, your brokerage, the profession.
3. **Alternatives:** comply (not an option); refuse and explain; withdraw if the client insists.
4. **Evaluate:** only refusing and explaining meets the Rules and ethics. You can also point out that limiting buyers may cost the seller money.
5. **Choose and justify:** explain the law, confirm you'll show to everyone and **present every offer**, document the conversation, and involve your managing broker. If the client insists, consider ending the relationship.

</details>

## Mindset: you're a gatekeeper, not an order-taker

Every rule in this lesson comes down to one idea: **people rely on you**. Buyers rely on what you tell them, clients rely on your loyalty, and the public relies on you not to let criminals use the system.

The professionals who stay out of trouble share a few habits:

- They **verify or attribute** every fact.
- They **put promises in writing**.
- They **ask the natural follow-up question** when something feels off.
- They **disclose early**, in writing, and **when in doubt, disclose**.
- They **talk to their managing broker** before, not after.

> 🏢 **For commercial specifically.** Commercial is where these risks concentrate. Deals involve **numbered companies, holding structures and trusts** (so beneficial ownership tracing is routine), **big wires from many sources**, **rent rolls and square footage** that drive price, and clients who expect you to "just get it done". Learn to say, calmly and early: *"Before we go further, I need to identify who ultimately owns the purchasing company. It's a legal requirement for every deal."* Sophisticated clients expect it, and the ones who resist are telling you something.

> **This week's habit.** Look up FINTRAC's published **administrative monetary penalties** and read one involving a real estate brokerage. Then write your own 3-line script for asking a corporate client about beneficial ownership, so it comes out naturally when you need it.
