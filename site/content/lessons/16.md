## The business side of being a licensee

> **Scenario.** A client owns a small bakery in a building they also own, held in a corporation. They want to sell "the whole thing" and buy a duplex to live in.
>
> - Do you need your licence to sell the bakery? It depends on whether the **real estate** is included and how the **shares** are sold.
> - The buyer asks for the **financial statements**. The balance sheet says the building is worth $410,000, but it would sell for $1.2 million. Which is right?
> - On the duplex purchase, your client is shocked to learn they owe **$1,480** to the seller for property taxes "on top of the price."
> - You want to email 300 past clients about your new listing. Can you, under **anti-spam** law?
> - You post on Instagram that you're "#1 in BC." Is that a problem?
>
> All of these are in this final lesson.

This lesson covers the business and practice skills that wrap around every deal:

1. **Ch 8, Financial statements:** forms of business, the balance sheet, income statement and tax return.
2. **Ch 24, From contract to completion:** what happens after subjects are removed, and **statements of adjustments**.
3. **Ch 25, Marketing:** target markets, the marketing mix, advertising rules and listing presentations.
4. **Ch 26, Technology:** tools, websites, social media, anti-spam, e-signatures, privacy and security.

## Vocabulary first

| Word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| **Sole proprietorship** | A business owned by one person, with **unlimited** personal liability. |
| **General partnership** | Two or more people carrying on business together for profit; each partner is liable for all the debts. |
| **Limited partnership** | General partners (run it, unlimited liability) + limited partners (passive, liability limited to their investment). |
| **Corporation** | A separate legal entity owned by shareholders, who are liable only for what they paid for their shares. |
| **PREC** | Personal real estate corporation: a corporation through which a licensee can earn commission. |

| **Income statement** | Revenue and expenses over a period; shows net income or loss. |
| **Depreciation (accounting)** | Spreading the cost of an asset over its useful life in the financial statements. |
| **Capital cost allowance (CCA)** | Depreciation for **tax** purposes, at CRA's set rates. |
| **Goodwill** | The amount paid for a business above the market value of its net assets. |
| **Conveyancer** | The lawyer or notary who completes the transfer of title. |
| **Completion / possession / adjustment dates** | When title transfers and money is paid / when the buyer gets the keys / when taxes, rent and other costs switch to the buyer. |

| **Undertaking** | A lawyer's or notary's binding professional promise. |
| **Target market** | A segment of the market with a common need that you choose to serve. |

## Forms of business organization

| Form | Separate legal entity? | Who pays the income tax? | Owners' liability |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sole proprietorship** | No | The owner, on their personal return | **Unlimited** |
| **General partnership** | Debatable; can do business in its own name | Each partner, on their share (income flows through) | **Unlimited**, for all debts incurred in the ordinary course, even ones they didn't approve |
| **Limited partnership** | Same as partnership | The partners | General partners unlimited; **limited partners** only to the amount they put in (if they stay passive) |
| **Limited liability partnership (LLP)** | Same as partnership | The partners | Each partner is liable for **their own** wrongdoing, not other partners' (unless they knew and didn't act) |
| **Corporation** | **Yes** | The **corporation** (usually at a lower rate than personal rates) | Shareholders lose only what they paid for **shares** |

Key points:

- In a partnership, **every partner is an agent** of the others. A junior partner who signs a contract binds all the general partners, even if the partnership agreement says only the senior partner can sign. (The others can then go after the junior partner.)
- A **limited partner** who starts taking part in day-to-day management risks being treated as a **general partner**, with unlimited liability.
- **LLPs** are mostly used by law, notary and accounting firms in BC.
- A corporation **never dies**: it carries on as shares change hands. A partnership may end when a partner dies, unless the agreement says otherwise.
- **Shareholders don't own the company's assets.** They own shares, and earn through **dividends**, **capital gains** on selling shares, or a share of the surplus if the company is wound up.
- Lenders to small corporations often demand **personal guarantees** from the owners, which undoes the limited liability for that loan.

> **Worked example: who pays?** Four people each put $25,000 into a business ($100,000 total). One of them defrauds a client, who wins a **$250,000** judgment. That leaves a **$150,000** shortfall.
>
> - **General partnership:** any or all four partners can be made to pay the $150,000.
> - **Limited partnership** (one general partner, three limited): the three limited partners lose their $25,000 each, no more. The **general partner** owes the $150,000.
> - **LLP:** once the partnership's assets are used up, the partner **who committed the fraud** owes the $150,000. The others lose their investment but nothing more, as long as they didn't know and took reasonable steps to prevent it.

### Other ways to invest together

- **Trust:** a **trustee** holds assets for **beneficiaries**. A **family trust** is a common example.
- **REIT:** investors buy **units** of a trust that owns real estate or mortgages. If it meets CRA's rules, the trust itself isn't taxed; unitholders pay tax on what they receive. REITs offer **limited liability** (like a corporation), partnership-like tax treatment and **liquidity** (listed on stock exchanges). Canada has 40+ REITs.
- **Joint venture:** two or more established entities team up for one project (e.g. a developer and a landowner). Its liability and taxation depend on whether it's set up as a partnership or a corporation.

## Personal real estate corporations

The Real Estate Services Regulation lets licensees earn commission through a **PREC**. Benefits:

- **lower corporate tax rate** on business income (up to a threshold)
- **tax deferral**: leave income in the company; pay personal tax only when dividends are paid
- **income splitting** in limited cases (non-voting shares to a spouse or adult children)
- more deductible business expenses

The catch:

- **No liability shield for your real estate work.** The licensee stays **personally liable** for real estate services (that's what **errors and omissions insurance** is for).
- **Double licensing costs**: both the licensee and the PREC must be licensed and carry E&O insurance.
- Setup and accounting costs. Get **legal and accounting advice** first.

> 🔑 **PREC advertising.** Advertise in the PREC's full licensed name including the words "**Personal Real Estate Corporation**" (e.g. "Jordan Lee Personal Real Estate Corporation"). The acronym **"PREC" is not allowed** in ads.

## Accounting principles (GAAP)

Financial statements follow **generally accepted accounting principles**. Since 2011, Canada has two sets:

- **IFRS** (International Financial Reporting Standards): required for **public** companies. Allows real estate to be shown at **fair (market) value**.
- **ASPE** (Accounting Standards for Private Enterprises): chosen by most **private** companies because IFRS is costly. Real estate stays at **cost less depreciation**.

The principles you need:

| Principle | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Cost** | Assets are recorded at what was paid for them (plus costs to get them working, but not recoverable GST). |
| **Revenue recognition** | Record revenue when it's **earned**, not when cash arrives (the **accrual** basis). A commission is usually recognized when **title transfers**, though it may be at subject removal depending on the agent contract. |
| **Matching** | Record expenses in the same period as the revenue they helped earn. A year of insurance is spread over 12 months. |
| **Materiality** | Small items (stationery) can be expensed right away; an exception to matching. |
| **Objectivity** | Use verifiable data, like the actual price paid. |
| **Consistency** | Once you pick a method, stick with it year to year. |

A **fiscal year** can't be longer than **53 weeks**. Individuals use the calendar year; corporations can pick any year-end but need CRA approval to change it.

## The balance sheet

The balance sheet is a **snapshot** on one date:

> **Assets = Liabilities + Owner's equity**
>
> (or: Assets − Liabilities = Owner's equity)

| Section | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Current assets** (turned into cash within a year) | Cash, accounts and rent receivable, inventory, prepaid expenses |
| **Non-current assets** | Land, buildings, equipment (at **cost less accumulated depreciation** = **net book value**) |
| **Current liabilities** (due within a year) | Accounts payable, wages payable, property taxes payable, **tenants' deposits**, income tax payable (corporations), the **current portion** of the mortgage |
| **Non-current liabilities** | The rest of the mortgage |
| **Equity** | Proprietorship: owner's capital. Partnership: each partner's capital. Corporation: **share capital** + **retained earnings**. |

> **Worked example: day one.** Priya invests **$150,000** of her own money. She buys a rental building for **$600,000** (land $250,000, building $350,000), paying $120,000 down and taking a **$480,000** mortgage.
>
> | Assets | | Liabilities and equity | |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Cash | $30,000 | Mortgage | $480,000 |
> | Land | $250,000 | Priya, capital | $150,000 |
> | Building | $350,000 | | |
> | **Total** | **$630,000** | **Total** | **$630,000** |

> 🚨 **A balance sheet doesn't show what a business is worth.** Long-term assets sit at **historical cost less depreciation**. A building bought 20 years ago for $350,000 may be on the books at $200,000 and sell for $1.5 million. Buyers look at **market value**, not book value.

In a corporation, the owners' names don't appear on the statements, only shares. That's the **corporate veil**.

## The income statement

The income statement covers a **period** (usually a year): **Revenue − Expenses = Net income** (or net loss).

- Uses the **accrual** basis: record rent **earned**, even if not yet collected; record expenses **incurred**, even if not yet paid.
- **Mortgage principal** repayments are **not** an expense (they reduce a liability). Mortgage **interest** is.
- **Cost of goods sold** = opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory. Example: $50,000 + $200,000 − $60,000 = **$190,000**.
- **Depreciation** spreads a building's cost over its life. **Land is never depreciated.**

> **Straight-line depreciation** = (cost − salvage value) ÷ useful life
>
> Building cost $350,000, salvage value $50,000, life 25 years: ($350,000 − $50,000) ÷ 25 = **$12,000 a year**.

> **Worked example: Priya's first year.**
>
> | | Amount |
> |---|---|
> | Rent **earned** (only $44,000 collected) | $48,000 |
> | Mortgage **interest** (of $30,000 paid; $6,000 was principal) | −$24,000 |
> | Operating expenses incurred | −$6,500 |
> | Property taxes | −$3,500 |
> | Depreciation | −$12,000 |
> | **Net income** | **$2,000** |
>
> The unpaid $4,000 of rent becomes **rent receivable** on the balance sheet. Net income flows into **owner's equity** (or **retained earnings** for a corporation): it's the link between the two statements.

A proprietorship's income statement shows **no income tax**: the owner pays it personally. A corporation's statement deducts income tax.

A **cash flow statement** shows where cash actually came from and went. A business can show a profit and still run out of cash, or look cash-rich only because it borrowed or sold assets.

## Goodwill

**Goodwill** is what a buyer pays **above** the market value of a business's net assets (assets − liabilities), for things like reputation, customer lists, location or great staff.

> **Example.** A business's assets are worth $650,000 at market and its debts are $250,000: net assets **$400,000**. A buyer pays **$520,000**. Goodwill = **$120,000**.

## The tax return and capital cost allowance

Only **corporations** file a business tax return. Proprietors and partners report their share on personal returns.

CRA doesn't accept accounting depreciation. For tax, you must use **capital cost allowance (CCA)**:

- Assets go into **classes** with set rates (e.g. many buildings are **Class 1, 4%**).
- CCA is calculated on a **declining balance**: the rate × the **undepreciated capital cost (UCC)**.
- **Half-year rule:** in the year you buy, you can claim only **50%** of the normal rate.
- **No CCA in the year you sell** the asset.
- CCA is **optional**: claim any amount up to the maximum, but unused CCA **can't be doubled up** next year.
- **Recapture:** if you sell for more than the UCC, the CCA you claimed beyond the real loss in value is added back to **taxable income**.

> **Example: CCA on Priya's building ($350,000, Class 1, 4%)**
>
> | Year | Calculation | Maximum CCA |
> |---|---|---|
> | 1 | $350,000 × 4% × 50% | $7,000 |
> | 2 | ($350,000 − $7,000) × 4% | $13,720 |
>
> Taxable income in year 1 = net income $2,000 + depreciation $12,000 − CCA $7,000 = **$9,000**. Taxable income and accounting net income are different numbers.

> 🏢 **Commercial angle: why investors love CCA.** CCA is a **non-cash** deduction: the owner deducts it from taxable income without spending any money, even while the building is **rising** in value. Recapture claws some back on sale, but the owner has **deferred** tax for years and pays it later in **cheaper future dollars**. On a large office or apartment building, that's a major tax shelter, and government can change CCA rates to encourage investment in, say, rental housing.

## Selling a business: do you need a licence?

| Situation | Licence needed? |
|---|---|
| The business owns **no** real estate | **No** |
| The business is sold as **assets** that include real estate (a sale, a lease, or an **assignment of a lease**) | **Yes** |
| A corporation that holds real estate is sold by **100%** share purchase | **Yes** |
| **Less than 100%** of the shares of a company holding real estate | **No**: it's a **securities** trade (may need *Securities Act* registration) |

At minimum, a buyer of a business should get and review:

1. an **income statement** for **12 months** ending no more than **120 days** before the agreement is signed
2. the **balance sheet**
3. a list of **fixtures, chattels, rights and other assets** that are **not** included

If statements aren't available, the buyer should see an **accountant**.

> 💡 **Watch the inventory.** Inventory is often priced separately and can be full of junk: last season's shoes, expiring groceries, wilting flowers, parts for obsolete engines. Get an expert to value it. And remember, in business sales the buyer and seller often know the business far better than you do. Your job is often to put their deal into a clear, complete contract.

## After the subjects come off

Once the contract is signed and subjects are removed, the licensee's main work is done, but the deal isn't. The **buyer's conveyancer** (lawyer or notary) takes over:

1. **Searches title** through **myLTSA** to check the contract matches reality: are all registered owners named as sellers? Are all encumbrances dealt with?
2. **Prepares documents**: the **Form A** (transfer), the **property transfer tax** return, both **statements of adjustments**, and others (e.g. a **Form F** for a strata lot).
3. Arranges **execution**: the seller signs the Form A; the buyer signs the PTT form, the buyer's statement and any mortgage, and pays the **balance due to complete** into the conveyancer's **trust account**.
4. **Registers**: re-checks title on completion day, then files electronically.
5. **Pays out** once the documents show as **pending applications** on title.

Only a **lawyer or notary** can do conveyancing for a fee. The **buyer** usually pays for the conveyance, so the **buyer chooses** the conveyancer. Lenders usually use their own lawyers for the mortgage.

### Your contract has to be right

The standard contract's title clause promises title free of encumbrances **except** a short list (Crown grant reservations, registered restrictive covenants and rights-of-way **in favour of utilities and public authorities**, and listed tenancies). Easements, building schemes and private covenants are **not** on that list.

> 🚨 **JBP Developments v. Li (BCSC 2018).** A restrictive covenant on title (needing a third party's consent to build or alter) wasn't listed as an exception. The seller couldn't give title as promised, the **buyer walked away**, and the **deposit went back to the buyer**. If a charge will stay on title, **add it to the title clause**.

Title terms are **conditions** (essential terms). If title isn't as described, the buyer can usually **terminate**. A buyer with cold feet in a falling market will use exactly that. The seller may then blame the licensee, and the brokerage may lose its **commission** because there was no "legally enforceable contract."

### Three dates

| Date | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Completion** | Seller transfers title; buyer pays. |
| **Possession** | Buyer gets the keys (at the time stated in the contract). |
| **Adjustment** | Taxes, rent and other costs switch to the buyer. Usually the same as possession. |

- Don't set possession on the **same day** as completion, especially not at noon: closing can take hours. Possession the **day after** completion is safer.
- Don't schedule a client's **sale and purchase** to complete on the same day.
- Leave enough time between acceptance and completion for **mortgage approval**.

> 🚨 **Possession means possession.** A buyer's licensee picked up the keys a day early and handed them over; the buyers walked in and set off the alarm. The licensee was **reprimanded** and paid **$1,500** in costs for failing to use reasonable care. Hand over keys **on** the possession date, not before.

### Survey and title insurance

- Lenders may require a **survey** to check the buildings sit within the boundaries. An **encroachment** (e.g. the house over the lot line) can stop the mortgage.
- **Title insurance** protects an owner or lender against certain title problems (fraud, forgery, encroachments, liens, bylaw violations). It can **replace** a survey or let a lender proceed **despite** an encroachment. There are **owner's** and **lender's** policies.

### Undertakings

The seller hands over the signed Form A **before** being paid, relying on the buyer's conveyancer's **undertaking** (a solemn professional promise) to pay once registration is pending. The seller's lawyer gives an undertaking to **discharge** the seller's mortgage from the proceeds.

### Costs that surprise buyers

Warn your buyers (especially first-timers) about:

1. **property tax adjustments** (repaying the seller for prepaid taxes)
2. **mortgage interest adjustments** (interest from the advance date to the start of the first regular payment period)
3. **PST on mortgage default insurance** premiums: must be paid in **cash at closing**, can't be added to the mortgage

Plus PTT, legal fees and possibly the **foreign buyers' tax**.

## Statements of adjustments

There are **two** statements:

- **Buyer's statement:** shows the **balance due to complete** (cash the buyer must bring).
- **Seller's statement:** shows the **cash proceeds of sale** (cash the seller will receive).

They're **not mirror images**. Each item appears on one or both.

> 🔑 **Debit and credit (closing-statement meaning, not accounting).**
>
> - **Credit** = already paid by, or owed **to**, that party. It reduces what the buyer owes / increases what the seller gets.
> - **Debit** = an amount that party must **pay**. It increases what the buyer owes / reduces what the seller gets.
>
> If an item appears on **both** statements, it's on **opposite sides**.

### Where each item goes

| Item | Buyer's statement | Seller's statement |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase price | Debit | Credit |
| Deposit (held by brokerage) | **Credit** | Not shown |
| Commission | Not shown | **Debit** |
| New mortgage from a lender | Credit | Not shown |
| Interest adjustment on new mortgage | Debit | Not shown |
| **Vendor take-back** mortgage | Credit | **Debit** |
| Existing mortgage **assumed** by buyer | Credit | Debit |
| Existing mortgage **paid off** by seller | Not shown | Debit |
| Taxes **prepaid** by seller (buyer's share) | Debit | Credit |
| Taxes **not yet paid** (seller's share) | Credit | Debit |
| Rent already collected by seller (buyer's share) | Credit | Debit |
| Tenants' security deposits + interest | Credit | Debit |
| Property transfer tax | Debit | Not shown |
| Each party's own legal fees | Debit (buyer's) | Debit (seller's) |

### The deposit and the commission are separate

The deposit is **part of the price** the buyer has already paid, held by the brokerage as **stakeholder**. The commission is the **seller's** debt to the brokerage. On completion, the deposit becomes the seller's money, and (as authorized in the listing contract) the brokerage keeps the commission from it. If the deposit is more than the commission, the brokerage sends the excess to the conveyancer; if less, the conveyancer pays the brokerage the difference.

### Property tax adjustments

BC property taxes cover the **calendar year**, usually due early **July**. The **buyer pays from and including the adjustment date**.

> **Taxes prepaid by the seller.** Annual taxes $3,650, already paid. Adjustment date **September 1**. Buyer's share: Sept 1 to Dec 31 = 30 + 31 + 30 + 31 = **122 days**. $3,650 × 122 ÷ 365 = **$1,220**. **Debit buyer, credit seller.**

> **Taxes not yet paid.** Estimated annual taxes $3,650. Adjustment date **March 1** (non-leap year). Seller's share: Jan 1 to Feb 28 = **59 days**. $3,650 × 59 ÷ 365 = **$590**. **Credit buyer, debit seller**: the seller pays the buyer now for their part, and the buyer pays the whole bill in July.

**Taxes due but unpaid:** charge the whole year's taxes and any **penalty** to the seller, then adjust as if prepaid. The **penalty isn't shared**: it's the seller's alone. The conveyancer pays the city from the proceeds.

**Home owner grant:** if either party isn't eligible, adjust the **gross** taxes; if both are eligible for the basic grant, adjust taxes **net** of it.

### Rent

Rent is paid **in advance** on the 1st. If the seller collected the month's rent, the buyer gets a credit from **and including** the adjustment date to month-end.

> **Example.** A suite rents for $2,400 a month, paid to the seller on September 1. Adjustment date **September 21**. Buyer's share: Sept 21–30 = **10 days** of 30. $2,400 × 10 ÷ 30 = **$800**. **Credit buyer, debit seller.**

Tenants' **security deposits** (plus interest) also go to the buyer, because the new owner must repay them by law.

### Worked example: both statements

> **Facts.** Price **$900,000**. Deposit **$45,000** held by the buyer's brokerage. Buyer's new mortgage **$720,000**. Buyer's legal fees **$1,200**. PTT: 1% × $200,000 + 2% × $700,000 = **$16,000**. Seller paid the year's taxes of $3,650; adjustment date Sept 1 → buyer owes **$1,220**. Commission **$31,500**. Seller's mortgage payout **$400,000**. Seller's legal fees **$900**.
>
> **Buyer's statement**
>
> | Item | Debit | Credit |
> |---|---|---|
> | Purchase price | $900,000 | |
> | Deposit | | $45,000 |
> | New mortgage | | $720,000 |
> | Tax adjustment (Sept 1–Dec 31) | $1,220 | |
> | Property transfer tax | $16,000 | |
> | Legal fees | $1,200 | |
> | **Totals** | **$918,420** | **$765,000** |
> | **Balance due to complete** | | **$153,420** |
>
> **Seller's statement**
>
> | Item | Debit | Credit |
> |---|---|---|
> | Purchase price | | $900,000 |
> | Tax adjustment | | $1,220 |
> | Commission | $31,500 | |
> | Mortgage payout | $400,000 | |
> | Legal fees | $900 | |
> | **Totals** | **$432,400** | **$901,220** |
> | **Cash proceeds of sale** | **$468,820** | |
>
> **Check (trust reconciliation):** money in = $153,420 (buyer) + $720,000 (lender) + $13,500 (deposit left after commission) = **$886,920**. Money out = $400,000 + $468,820 + $900 + $16,000 + $1,200 = **$886,920**. ✔

> 🚨 **Read the statements.** In one case, a listing licensee was found **15% liable** to the seller for missing a mortgage fraud that was visible in the statements of adjustments: they showed **cash going to the buyers**, and the seller's take-back mortgage had become a **second** mortgage instead of a first. If a statement doesn't match the contract, raise it.

Items like heating oil left in the tank are usually settled **outside** the statements.

## The marketing concept

**Marketing** is communicating the value of a service to consumers. It's much more than advertising: it includes pricing, place, relationships and how you deliver your service.

The **marketing concept** starts with the **client's needs**. Every action should help you:

- get a **new** client
- **keep** a client (repeat business)
- get **referrals**

Long term, aim for about **two-thirds** of your business from **repeat clients and referrals** and **one-third** from new clients.

You'll run three kinds of marketing: a **brokerage** marketing to licensees, **your personal** marketing program, and a separate **listing marketing program** for each property you list.

## Target markets and positioning

You can't serve everyone well. **Segment** the market and choose a **target market**: a group with a common need. Segment by:

- **property type** (condos, townhouses, retail, industrial, office)
- **location** (a town, a neighbourhood)
- **consumer type** (families, luxury buyers, institutional investors)

Choose based on your resources, profit and growth potential, competition, your **affinity** for the market, and whether you can reach it profitably.

| Strategy | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Undifferentiated** | One service, one program, for everyone. Vulnerable to specialists. |
| **Differentiated** | Several programs for several segments (e.g. a brokerage with branch offices). |
| **Niche** | Focus everything on one small, active, growing segment. **Best fit for individual licensees.** |

**Product positioning** is how your service sits in clients' minds. A **positioning map** plots competitors on two attributes (e.g. price vs location) to spot gaps.

> 🚨 **Don't take listings outside your competence.** A residential licensee tempted by a commercial listing should think hard. Without the knowledge to market it properly, the client is poorly served and you may breach your **duty of competence**. Better: **refer** it to a commercial specialist for a referral fee.

## Understanding buyers and sellers

A licensee meets three basic needs: **information**, **knowledge and skills**, and **trust and confidentiality** (especially important to commercial clients).

### The shopping behaviour model

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| **Convenience goods** | Groceries: bought often, little effort |
| **Shopping goods** | Homes, cars, **real estate services**: compared before buying |
| **Specialty goods** | A Ferrari, a famous specialist: wanted with no substitute |
| **Unsought goods** | Life insurance, fire extinguishers: not sought until prompted |

### The process model of buying

1. **Felt need**: a trigger (new baby, divorce, job change, retirement, lease expiry, rising rents or rates).
2. **Pre-purchase activity**: research (open houses, websites, interviewing licensees). Sellers typically interview about **three** licensees.
3. **Purchase activity**: signing the listing. Most decide within **one week** of finishing their research, so stay in touch.
4. **Post-purchase activity**: doubts and regret, called **cognitive dissonance** ("buyer's remorse"). Reassure clients at each stage (listing, accepting an offer, removing subjects, moving).

In residential, consumers choose the **licensee**. In commercial, the **brokerage's** name and reputation matter more.

## The marketing mix

The **4 Ps**:

- **Product**: your service. It's **intangible**: clients can't test-drive it, so you must make its value visible.
- **Place**: where clients reach you (office, website, email, mobile).
- **Price**: your fee. Methods include value, prestige, cost-plus, mark-up, penetration (low to win share), skimming (high early), meet-or-beat, tiered and promotional pricing. Be ready to **explain the value** behind your fee.
- **Promotion**: the **promotional mix**:

| Element | Example |
|---|---|
| **Personal selling** | Face-to-face presentations, door-knocking about a new listing |
| **Advertising** | Paid, non-personal: signs, print, online ads |
| **Sales promotion** | Pens, calendars, notepads, contests |
| **Public relations** | Coaching a team, joining a service club, sponsoring events |
| **Publicity** | Media coverage you don't pay for or control; seen as more believable |

## SMART goals and budgets

Goals should be **SMART**: **S**pecific, **M**easurable, **A**chievable, **R**elevant, **T**imely.

> **Not SMART:** "Get more listings."
>
> **SMART:** "Get 6 new listings from a postcard mailing to 400 homes in Westridge by June 30."

Ways to set a marketing budget:

| Method | Weakness |
|---|---|
| **Percentage of sales** | Backwards: advertising should drive sales, not follow them. Spending drops just when sales drop. |
| **Follow the competition** | No reason to assume competitors are right. |
| **Objective/task** | Set objectives, cost the tasks. The most logical, as long as you also ask whether each objective is **worth** its cost. |

## Messages, brands and ads

- Your **personal brand** is how people perceive your business: keep it **simple, consistent, integrated** and **different**. Use a current professional photo.
- Every campaign needs one **theme** (e.g. "luxury condo specialist").
- Build messages with **AIDA**: **A**ttention, **I**nterest, **D**esire, **A**ction.
- Appeals can be **rational** (value, security) or **emotional** (pride, joy, fear).
- **Informal** sources (friends, family, word of mouth) are more **credible** than **formal** ones (ads, direct mail).
- Build a **feedback** system: track every call, email and lead back to its source.

### Designing an ad

1. **Copy strategy platform**: objectives, target audience, main selling idea, secondary features.
2. **Layout**: headline, copy, visuals, signature.

- Readers' eyes land first at about the **10 o'clock** position, then move up and right. Put the headline there. Readers look for the **logo at the bottom**.
- Use **white space**, balance, unity and flow; avoid clutter.
- **Headlines**: upper and lower case; benefit, news, curiosity or targeted ("Young and Single?").
- **Copy**: short, clear, uses "you," sells the **lifestyle**, avoids clichés, and ends with a **call to action**.

### Ad types

Brochures, **direct mail** and postcards, classified ads, newspaper and magazine ads, online ads (e.g. **Google Ads**: paid placements, not organic search results), property **signs**, radio and TV, billboards and bus benches.

**Telemarketing**: check the National **Do Not Call List** (exceptions include existing business relationships, consent and business numbers). **Unlicensed assistants may not telemarket.**

## The advertising rules

| Rule | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **s.37 Personal offices** | No sign showing real estate services are offered there; don't answer the phone in the brokerage's name; don't put the personal office address in ads. |
| **s.40 Advertising generally** | The **brokerage's licensed name** must be **prominent and easily readable**. Use your **licensee name** (or PREC name). Teams must show the **team name**. Any office address must be the **brokerage's**. |
| **s.41 False or misleading** | No ads you know, or ought to know, are false or misleading. |
| **s.42 Owner's consent** | Don't advertise specific property for sale without the owner's (or their agent's) consent. |

The three most common violations BCFSA penalizes:

1. **Missing or tiny brokerage name** (a logo or franchise name isn't enough).
2. **Unregistered team names**.
3. **Personal office** signage or addresses.

**Team names** must be approved by BCFSA, must sound like a team ("Team," "Group," "Associates," "Network"), can't use "Realty" or "Real Estate" (except "Real Estate Team" or "Real Estate Group" at the end), and can't be confused with a brokerage.

> 🚨 **Back up every claim.** In April 2025, BCFSA fined four licensees **$4,000 to $6,250** for claims like "#1 in BC" with no qualifying information, and for missing brokerage and PREC names on social media. Any ranking, award or sales volume needs its **source, date and basis** stated.

The *Competition Act* (Lesson 8) also applies: **s.52** makes knowingly or recklessly misleading advertising a **criminal** offence, **s.74.01** creates **civil** liability for the same thing, and **s.74.05** bans charging **more than the advertised price**.

## The listing presentation

Most sellers choose based on **referrals, reputation or past dealings**. Your listing presentation turns an **intangible** service into **tangible** evidence.

**Written presentation:** personal and team introduction, about the brokerage, past sales (similar properties), the **listing marketing plan** with a timeline, the **CMA** (current listings, recent sales, expired listings), your strengths (true, verifiable statistics), **testimonials**, sample brochures and the listing forms.

**In-person presentation:** don't read the document aloud. **Create a dialogue**, **listen**, avoid jargon, present to **all** the sellers (don't assume who decides), and stay professional.

### The listing marketing program

- Set **SMART** listing goals (e.g. "10 calls in the first week," "10 showings").
- Pick one clear **listing message**: ask the sellers what made them fall in love with the property.
- Set a **budget**. New licensees often pay up front and recover it from commission; luxury sellers sometimes fund it.
- **Track and report** on a fixed schedule (e.g. weekly), and use showing feedback to discuss price or fixes.

## Tools of the trade

- **Mobile office:** work from anywhere, but stay connected to your brokerage, follow procedures, and know when shortcuts aren't allowed.
- **Forms software** (e.g. WEBForms® on REALTORLink®): always use the **current** forms.
- **MLS®**: the listing licensee is responsible for the **accuracy** of the data. CREA owns the MLS® and REALTOR® trademarks. Public sites (realtor.ca) show only part of the data.
- **CRM** (customer relationship management) and **PIM** (email, calendar, contacts) software keep you in touch with your database.
- **GIS mapping** layers lot size, zoning, sales and more onto maps (e.g. Vancouver's **VanMap**).
- **Research sites**: **myLTSA** (titles), **BC Registry** (companies, Personal Property Registry, Manufactured Homes Registry), municipal sites (zoning, permits).
- **Video calls and virtual tours**: use unique meeting IDs, passwords and waiting rooms to protect client privacy.

### Photos and drones

> 🚨 **Don't misrepresent with editing.** Removing a garbage can or a parked car is fine. Removing **power lines** or changing the property's physical features is **misleading** (Rules s.41). Label **virtual staging** and AI-enhanced images.

**Drones** between **250 g and 25 kg** need a Transport Canada **pilot certificate** and **registration**; flying near or over bystanders or in controlled airspace needs more. Neighbours can sue under BC's ***Privacy Act***, so ask them first.

## Websites, SEO and social media

- Register a simple, memorable **domain name**; make the site **mobile-friendly**; write **original** content (not generic filler).
- **SEO** (search engine optimization) helps your site rank in **organic** (unpaid) search results.
- **Web analytics** (e.g. Google Analytics) show who visits and how. Share listing page stats with sellers.
- Blogs, podcasts and newsletters keep you **top of mind**. Send newsletters at most **weekly** and at least **quarterly**; use **BCC** so you never expose clients' addresses.

### Social media rules

- Keep **personal and professional** accounts separate.
- The **brokerage name** (and team and PREC name, if any) must be on your **profile**. It doesn't have to be in every post.
- On **YouTube**, put the brokerage name in the **title or description** (or the video intro).
- On **Craigslist and Kijiji**, every posting needs the brokerage name.
- **Don't translate** the brokerage name into another language.
- Never post **client confidential** information, even in private groups. You're accountable for what you **repost** too.
- Ask: would I say this in person in a public forum?
- Don't advertise **other licensees' listings** without permission, and remove **expired** listings promptly.

## Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation (CASL)

CASL covers **commercial electronic messages (CEMs)**: emails, texts and other messages that encourage commercial activity (no profit motive needed). Every CEM needs:

1. **Consent**:
   - **express** (a clear request with purpose and sender identity; **no pre-checked boxes**), or
   - **implied** (a business or non-business relationship within the last **2 years**, or an address published without a "no spam" notice)
2. **Identification**: who it's from, a mailing address and another contact method
3. A free, easy **unsubscribe** that you honour promptly

**Referral exception:** you can message someone you were referred to, if the referrer knows both of you and the message names the referrer and has an unsubscribe.

Penalties: up to **$1 million** for individuals and **$10 million** for businesses. Keep a **log** of how and when you got each consent.

## E-signatures and records

- BC's ***Electronic Transactions Act*** (2001) lets electronic documents and signatures satisfy legal requirements for writing and signatures, **if the parties intend it**.
- It **doesn't** cover documents that create or transfer an interest in land and need **registration** (e.g. the Form A). It **does** cover listing agreements and contracts of purchase and sale.
- BCFSA: tablet and electronic signatures can create enforceable contracts. But **a name in an email is not a signature**.
- Keep the **DocuSign Certificate of Completion** with the transaction file: it proves who signed and when.
- Brokerages must keep records **in BC** (RESA s.25), may store them electronically but must print them promptly on BCFSA's request (Rules s.91), and must keep them **7 years** (Rules s.92). US-based cloud services may be subject to US laws like the *Patriot Act*.
- **Copyright** protects **expression**, not facts or ideas. **Fair dealing** allows small quotes for research, criticism or private study, not commercial use. Software is **licensed**, not owned.

## Security and AI

- **Back up early, back up often**, in **two physical locations** (e.g. computer + cloud or external drive).
- **Passwords**: at least **8** characters, mixed types, no dictionary words, no "qwerty" or "123456," no obvious swaps like $ for s, different for every account. A **password manager** helps.
- **Viruses and malware**: use anti-virus software and keep it updated.
- **Phishing**: fake messages pretending to be trusted senders. Verify before clicking or sending money.
- **Firewalls**: your router is the first line of defence.
- Don't leave laptops unattended. E&O offers **Cyber Guard** for BC licensees.

> 💡 **BCFSA's AI tips.**
>
> - Don't put client or sensitive information into AI tools.
> - **Fact-check** every output: you're responsible for your ads and representations.
> - Watch for **bias** (e.g. in tenant screening).
> - Don't use AI to **draft contracts** or give legal advice.
> - **Label** AI-enhanced images and virtual staging.
> - Follow your brokerage's AI policy.

## Privacy: PIPA

BC's ***Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA)*** governs how private organizations **collect, use, disclose and protect** personal information. (Federal **PIPEDA** applies elsewhere; BC, Alberta and Quebec have their own laws.)

- **Personal information** = anything about an identifiable person: name, birth date, income, physical characteristics, home address. **Business contact information** and work product aren't covered.
- **Tell people why** you're collecting it, on or before collecting it, and generally get **consent**.
- Use it only for purposes a **reasonable person** would consider appropriate.
- **Destroy or anonymize** it once it's no longer needed for its purpose or for legal and business reasons.
- Keep confidential comments (e.g. why a seller is moving) **off the internet** unless authorized. Get the seller's **written consent** before posting listing details online.

## Two alerts

> 🚨 **ALERT 1: Write the contract the conveyancer can close.** Search title before you write; list every charge that will stay; set realistic completion, possession and adjustment dates (possession the day **after** completion); and hand over keys only on the possession date. A title error lets the buyer walk and can cost the commission.

> 🚨 **ALERT 2: Every ad, post and email has rules.** Brokerage name prominent (s.40), nothing misleading (s.41), owner's consent (s.42), registered team names, PREC name in full, qualified claims, CASL consent and unsubscribe, PIPA consent, and honest photos. Fines start in the thousands.

## Trap list: things the exam likes to twist

1. **Proprietorships and partnerships don't pay income tax**; owners and partners do. **Corporations** do.
2. General partners have **unlimited** liability for all ordinary-course debts, even ones they didn't approve.
3. A **limited partner** who manages the business risks becoming a **general partner**.
4. **LLP**: each partner liable for **their own** wrongs, not other partners' (unless they knew and did nothing).
5. Shareholders own **shares**, not the company's assets.
6. **PREC**: licensee stays **personally liable** for real estate services; both licensee and PREC need licences and E&O. Advertise "Personal Real Estate Corporation" in full, never "PREC."
7. **REIT**: units trade publicly; trust not taxed if it meets CRA rules; unitholders taxed.
8. **IFRS** (public companies) allows **fair value**; **ASPE** (most private companies) keeps **cost less depreciation**.
9. **Accrual** basis: revenue when **earned**, expenses when **incurred**.
10. **Materiality** is an exception to **matching**.
11. Fiscal year ≤ **53 weeks**.
12. **Assets = Liabilities + Equity.** Balance sheet = a date; income statement = a period.
13. Balance sheet **doesn't show market value**.
14. Mortgage **principal** isn't an expense; **interest** is. **Land** isn't depreciated.
15. Straight-line depreciation = (cost − salvage) ÷ life.
16. **CCA**, not accounting depreciation, for tax. **Half-year rule**: 50% in year one. **No CCA in the year of sale.** Unused CCA can't be doubled up. **Recapture** on sale.
17. **Goodwill** = price − market value of net assets.
18. Selling a business: licence needed if real estate is included as **assets** (including a **lease assignment**) or **100%** of shares of a real-estate-holding company. **Under 100%** of shares = securities trade.
19. Business sale package: income statement for **12 months** ending ≤ **120 days** before signing, balance sheet, list of **excluded** assets.
20. Only **lawyers or notaries** convey for a fee. The **buyer** usually chooses and pays the conveyancer.
21. Standard title clause exceptions don't include **easements or private covenants**; add them (*JBP v. Li*).
22. Title terms are **conditions**: breach lets the buyer **terminate**.
23. Possession **after** completion; never noon on completion day.
24. Early key handover → reprimand + **$1,500**.
25. Payment is made once the transfer shows as a **pending application**, not after final registration.
26. Surprise costs: **tax adjustment**, **interest adjustment**, **PST on default insurance** (cash, not added to the mortgage).
27. **Deposit**: credit to buyer, **not** on seller's statement. **Commission**: debit to seller only.
28. Lender mortgage: credit buyer only. **VTB**: credit buyer **and** debit seller. Seller's payout: debit seller only.
29. Buyer pays taxes **from and including** the adjustment date. Prepaid → debit buyer/credit seller. Unpaid → credit buyer/debit seller. **Penalty**: seller only.
30. Rent collected by seller → **credit buyer, debit seller**. Security deposits + interest go to the buyer.
31. **PTT** is a debit on the buyer's statement only.
32. Listing licensee **15% liable** for missing fraud visible in the statements.
33. Business mix goal: **⅔ repeat/referral, ⅓ new**.
34. Real estate services are **shopping goods**.
35. Process model: felt need → pre-purchase → purchase (**within one week**) → post-purchase (**cognitive dissonance**).
36. **Niche marketing** suits individual licensees.
37. **4 Ps**: product, place, price, promotion. Promotional mix: personal selling, advertising, sales promotion, PR, publicity.
38. **SMART**: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, timely.
39. **Percentage-of-sales** budgeting is backwards.
40. **AIDA**. Eyes start at **10 o'clock**; logo at the bottom.
41. Rules **s.37** personal office, **s.40** brokerage name, **s.41** misleading, **s.42** owner consent.
42. Team names: no "Realty"/"Real Estate" (except "Real Estate Team/Group" at the end).
43. Unlicensed assistants **can't telemarket**. Check the **DNCL**.
44. Social media: brokerage name on the **profile**, not every post; YouTube in **title or description**; no **translated** brokerage names.
45. **CASL**: consent (express or implied within **2 years**), identification, unsubscribe; penalties to **$1M / $10M**.
46. ***Electronic Transactions Act*** excludes registrable land transfer documents; covers listings and contracts. An email name isn't a signature.
47. Records: keep in BC, **7 years**; print on BCFSA request.
48. Drones **250 g–25 kg**: licence and registration.
49. **PIPA** excludes **business contact information**.

## Drill

**Q1.** Four friends want to buy a small strip mall together. Two will manage it; two just want to invest and don't want to risk their homes. What structure fits, and what should the passive investors avoid?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- A **limited partnership**: the two managers are **general partners** (unlimited liability), the two investors are **limited partners** (liability limited to their contribution).
- The limited partners must **stay out of day-to-day management**, or they risk being treated as general partners with unlimited liability.
- Alternatives: a **corporation** (all four limited to their shares, but lenders may demand **personal guarantees**, and income is taxed in the company) or a **joint venture**.
- Recommend they get **legal and tax advice**. That's not your call to make for them.

</details>

**Q2.** A seller's balance sheet shows their building at $420,000. A buyer's broker says it's worth $1.3 million. Who's right, and why the gap?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- The balance sheet shows **historical cost less accumulated depreciation** (net book value) under ASPE. It **doesn't** show market value.
- The building may have been bought decades ago, and depreciation has reduced its book value while its market value has risen.
- For pricing, use an **appraisal** or the **income approach**, not the balance sheet. Any amount paid above the market value of the net assets would be **goodwill**.

</details>

**Q3.** You're asked to sell 60% of the shares of a company whose main asset is a warehouse. Do you need your real estate licence? What if it were 100%?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **60%**: no real estate licence. Selling less than 100% of the shares is a **securities** trade, which may need registration under the *Securities Act*.
- **100%**: yes. Selling all the shares of a company holding real estate is a trade in real estate.
- If the business sold its **assets** instead (including the warehouse or a lease assignment), a licence would also be needed.

</details>

**Q4.** Taxes of $4,380 for the year were paid by the seller on July 2. The adjustment date is November 1. What's the adjustment, and on which side of each statement?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- Buyer's share: Nov 1 to Dec 31 = 30 + 31 = **61 days**.
- $4,380 × 61 ÷ 365 = **$732**.
- **Debit** on the buyer's statement (they repay the seller); **credit** on the seller's statement.

</details>

**Q5.** Price $650,000; deposit $30,000; new bank mortgage $500,000; PTT $11,000; buyer's legal fees $1,000; buyer owes $400 for prepaid taxes. What's the balance due to complete?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- Debits: $650,000 + $11,000 + $1,000 + $400 = **$662,400**.
- Credits: $30,000 + $500,000 = **$530,000**.
- Balance due to complete: $662,400 − $530,000 = **$132,400**.
- (Check the PTT: 1% × $200,000 + 2% × $450,000 = $2,000 + $9,000 = $11,000 ✔.)

</details>

**Q6.** You want to email a "Just Listed" flyer to 300 past clients, some you last dealt with 4 years ago. You also want your Instagram bio to say "Top agent in town." What do you need?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **CASL**: you need **consent**. Clients from the last **2 years** give **implied** consent; for the 4-year-old ones, you need **express** consent (and you can't email them just to ask unless you have consent). Every email must **identify** you and have a working **unsubscribe**.
- **Advertising Rules**: your **brokerage name** (plus team/PREC name) must be on the Instagram **profile**. "Top agent in town" must be **qualified** (source, date, basis) or it's misleading (s.41). BCFSA has fined licensees $4,000–$6,250 for unqualified "#1" claims.
- **Privacy**: use **BCC** or mailing software so no client sees another's address (PIPA).

</details>

## Mindset: run your practice like a business

You're about to become a **business owner**. Your clients will judge you by your contracts, your closing day, your ads and how you treat their information, long before they judge your negotiating skills.

The habits that matter: **write clean contracts** so closings are boring, **check the statements** against the deal, **pick a niche** and become its expert, **track** what your marketing actually produces, and **protect** client data like it's your own.

> 🏢 **For commercial specifically.**
>
> - **Business sales and share deals** are common. Know when a share sale needs a licence (**100%**) and when it's a securities trade. Insist on proper **financial statements** and an **excluded-assets** list, and bring in accountants.
> - **Read financials fluently.** Owners hold property through **corporations**, **partnerships** and **REITs**. Know that book value isn't market value, that **CCA** and **recapture** drive after-tax returns, and that a **non-resident** or corporate seller changes the closing.
> - **Closings are bigger and slower**: rent rolls, **security deposits**, **assumed mortgages**, GST self-assessment and estoppel certificates all flow through the statements of adjustments. Read them line by line.
> - **Commercial clients choose brokerages**, not just individuals. Your brokerage's brand, research and **confidentiality** matter more. Never let deal details leak online.
> - **Marketing is targeted**: offering memorandums, broker email lists, LinkedIn and direct outreach to owners. **CASL** applies to business emails too, so log consent.

> **This week's habit.** Write your own SMART goal for your first year (number of deals, target market, date). Then draft a one-paragraph positioning statement: who you serve, what you specialize in, and why a client should choose you.
