## Why the financing can matter more than the price

> **Scenario.** You're listing a small retail building. Three things land on your desk in the same week:
>
> 1. An offer at **full price**, but the buyer wants the seller to **carry a big mortgage at a below-market rate**.
> 2. A second buyer who wants to **assume** the seller's existing cheap mortgage, and the seller assumes they'll be "off the hook" once it's assumed.
> 3. A notice that the bank holding a **neighbouring** property's mortgage has started **foreclosure**, and your investor client wants to buy it.
>
> Each one is a legal and financial trap if you don't know how mortgages work. The "full price" offer may be worth tens of thousands less than it looks. The seller may stay **personally liable** for years after an assumption. And the foreclosure purchase runs on **court rules**, not normal contract rules.

Lesson 12 covered the math. This lesson covers the **law** of mortgages (what a mortgage is, its key clauses, the federal and provincial rules, assignment, assumption, priorities, **foreclosure** and **agreements for sale**), how **financing changes what an offer is worth**, the **true cost** of bonused loans, **prepayment penalties**, and how lenders **underwrite** residential and commercial loans.

## What a mortgage is, legally

Financially, people say "I have a mortgage" meaning a loan. **Legally, a mortgage isn't the loan**: it's an **interest in land** given to the lender as **security** for the loan, plus evidence of the debt.

- The **mortgagor** (borrower) **grants** the mortgage and gets the money.
- The **mortgagee** (lender) **receives** the mortgage and gives the money.

In some provinces (e.g. Alberta) a mortgage is just a **charge** on title. In BC, the prevailing view is that it still works the old common-law way: a **transfer** of the borrower's interest to the lender as security, even though the borrower stays the **registered owner**.

### Where the equity of redemption came from

Under old **common law**, the borrower actually transferred the land to the lender, who promised to give it back if paid **exactly on time**. One day late and the land was gone, **and** the borrower still owed the debt.

The **courts of equity** found this unfair and gave borrowers an **equitable right to redeem** after the due date (traditionally about six months). That's the **equity of redemption**. A lender who wanted to end that right had to ask the court, which is the origin of **foreclosure**.

> 🔑 **Two meanings of "equity".** The **equity of redemption** is a legal *right* to redeem. Everyday **equity** is *value*: a $500,000 home with a $100,000 mortgage has $400,000 of equity. Each extra mortgage eats into that cushion, which is why **second and later mortgages are smaller and dearer**.

## Legal and equitable mortgages

**Legal mortgage.** In BC's Torrens system a mortgage must be **registered** under the *Land Title Act* to bind third parties. Once registered it ranks ahead of later charges and unregistered interests (subject to statutory priorities, below). The lender can foreclose; the borrower can redeem until **order absolute** or a court-approved sale.

**Equitable mortgages** bind the borrower but not third parties. They arise:

| How | Key points |
|---|---|
| **Deposit of the duplicate certificate of title** | Handing the lender the duplicate certificate, **with intent** to create security. The LTO won't register most dealings without it. Unclear intent → may just be safekeeping (*Royal Bank v. Mesa Estates*). Can't be protected by caveat (statute prohibits registering it). Rare today. |
| **Agreement to give a mortgage** | A promise of a future mortgage, or a mortgage that's **procedurally** unregistrable (wrong form). Lender should file a **caveat** until it's fixed. |
| **Mortgage disguised as a transfer** | A "sale" that's really security for a loan. The court looks at **substance** and restores the equity of redemption. |

## Implied terms

Courts read certain protections into every mortgage:

- **No clog on the equity of redemption.** When the loan is repaid, the borrower must get title back **free** of the mortgage's conditions.
  - An **option to purchase** given to the lender **as part of the loan** is void.
  - A redemption date so far off it's practically never (e.g. 100 years) is void.
  - But a term that merely **restricts prepayment** or **prohibits assumption** is **not** a clog.
  - An option granted **later**, as a separate deal, can be valid.
- **Collateral advantages** (extra benefits to the lender, like a gas station owner promising to buy only the lender's fuel): void if part of the mortgage **and** they outlast redemption; possibly valid if they're an **independent bargain**.
- **Good faith.** *Bhasin v. Hrynew* (SCC 2014) requires **honest performance** of contracts. In *1026238 B.C. Ltd. v. Pastula* (2016), borrowers said the lender had orally promised more time; the court found no new contract and no breach.

## Express terms: the Form B mortgage

Since 1990, BC's land title office accepts only the prescribed two-part **Form B** mortgage:

- **Part 1**: parties, legal description, signatures, key financial terms, and any **modifications** to standard terms.
- **Part 2**: everything else: the prescribed standard terms, the **lender's filed standard terms**, or express terms.

If the lender uses filed standard terms, the borrower must get an **exact copy** before or when signing, and **acknowledge** it. If not, the mortgage takes effect on the **prescribed** standard terms instead.

### Common clauses

| Clause | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Repayment** | Payment amounts and dates; balance due at the end of the term; payments go to **interest first**, then principal. Interest runs before and after default. |
| **Acceleration** | On default, the lender **may** demand everything. It's at the **lender's option** (it wouldn't want a high-rate loan forced to end). *Law and Equity Act* **s.25**: the court can relieve against acceleration, but (s.28) only **once** for the same party and covenant. |
| **Omnibus** | If the borrower doesn't pay something (taxes, strata fees), the lender can pay it and **add it to the mortgage**. An alternative to accelerating. |
| **Insurance** | Insure to **replacement value**; lender has a claim on the proceeds. |
| **Repairs / waste** | Keep the property in good repair; maybe no alterations without consent. |
| **Fixtures** | Lists items deemed **fixtures** (more security for the lender). |
| **Lender's remedies** | Lender can enforce this security without first going after other security. |
| **Advances** | Lender **isn't obliged** to advance funds. Lets it stop advances on construction loans or HELOCs (e.g. if a lien is filed). |
| **Costs** | Borrower pays legal and enforcement costs, but BC law limits what a lender actually recovers in foreclosure. |
| **Charges in priority** | Borrower must keep prior charges, taxes and insurance paid. |
| **Further charges** | No new mortgages without the lender's written consent. |
| **Strata property** | Obey the Act and bylaws, pay levies; assigns the borrower's **vote** to the lender on permitted matters. |
| **Guarantor** | Adds a third party's personal promise to pay. |
| **Due on sale** | Whole balance due if sold (at the lender's option). **Without it, the mortgage is assumable.** If enforced, the prepayment penalty is due too. |
| **Portability** | Borrower can move the mortgage to a new home (e.g. within 60 days), often with a **blended rate** for extra funds. |

### Covenantor vs guarantor

| | **Covenantor** | **Guarantor** |
|---|---|---|
| Liability | **Primary**, like the borrower | **Secondary** |
| When the lender can collect | Any time, even before chasing the borrower | After exhausting remedies against the borrower |
| Easier for the lender? | **Yes** | Less so |

Documents sometimes mix the two labels. Courts look at what the **wording actually requires**, not the title on the signature line.

## Special types of mortgages

- **Interim blanket mortgage**: one mortgage over a whole development, with a **partial discharge** clause so each lot or strata unit is released as it's sold (the lender gets the sale price, or a set amount per square foot, often with limits until the loan is repaid).
- **Vendor take-back (VTB)**: the **seller** "takes back" a mortgage for part of the price. Title passes to the buyer; the seller's mortgage is registered against it. No cash changes hands for that part; payment is deferred. No 80% statutory limit, fewer fees. The seller may earn more than in a savings account and sell faster, but buyers usually pay **more** for cheap financing.
- **Reverse annuity mortgage (RAM)**: the lender pays the (usually retired) borrower; repaid on sale, death or refinancing.
- **Bridge financing**: short-term, higher-rate loan while long-term financing is arranged (e.g. bought before selling).

> 🏢 **Commercial angle: the developer's toolkit.** Blanket mortgages with partial discharges, staged **construction advances**, and **priority agreements** with mezzanine lenders are routine in development. When you sell presale units, the buyer's title will be cleared of the blanket mortgage by **partial discharge** at completion: know how that works so you can answer buyer questions.

> ⚠️ **Licensee duty on a VTB.** When you act for a **seller** offered a below-market VTB, you must protect their interests: a fair price **and** adequate security. Calculate and **disclose** the offer's **cash-equivalent value** (below) and recommend **legal advice**.

## Federal law: the Interest Act and the Criminal Code

### Interest Act

| Section | Rule |
|---|---|
| **No rate cap** | The Act doesn't limit rates. (Provincial unconscionability law and the *Criminal Code* can.) |
| **s.3** | Interest required but **no rate stated** → **5%** a year. |
| **s.6** | **Blended-payment** mortgages must state the rate calculated **yearly or half-yearly, not in advance**. If missing: **no interest** can be charged. Courts read this liberally (all required info present is enough; payments aren't "blended" if principal and interest can be readily separated). A rate stated simply "per annum" is presumed **compounded yearly**. |
| **s.7** | If that statement shows a **lower** rate than the repayment clause, only the **lower** rate is collectable. |
| **s.8** | **No higher charge on arrears** than on money not in arrears. A "three months' interest on default" clause is unenforceable. *Krayzel Corp. v. Equitable Trust* (SCC 2016): a **discount lost on default** = higher rate on default = void. A rate rising just with **time** is fine. *Bankers Mortgage v. Plaza 500 Hotels* (BCCA 2017): a broker's exit fee unconnected to the mortgage didn't breach s.8. Compound interest is fine **if the mortgage expressly says so**. |
| **s.10** | Prepayment for **individuals** (see below). |

**Bonuses.** If the lender advances $90,000 on a $100,000 mortgage, the $10,000 **bonus** is treated as **principal**, not interest, under s.6. But it **is** interest for the *Criminal Code*.

#### Section 10: the five-year prepayment right

It applies only if:

1. the borrower is an **individual**, and
2. the mortgage isn't payable for **more than 5 years** from its date, and
3. **5 years** from the mortgage date have passed.

Then the borrower can pay everything owing **plus three months' interest**, and **no further interest** can be charged. Residential or not doesn't matter; being an **individual** does.

- **Not available** to corporations (ever), and since **January 1, 2012**, to partnerships, unlimited liability corporations and business trusts (older mortgages excepted).
- **Renewals**: if a renewal **re-dates** the mortgage, the 5-year clock restarts at the renewal date. If it just **extends** the term and keeps the original date, the clock runs from the original date.

> 🧮 **Re-dated vs extended.**
>
> - **Maya** signs a 5-year mortgage in 2016. In 2021 she renews for another 5 years and the renewal **re-dates** the mortgage to 2021. In 2023 she wants to pay off: **no s.10 right**. The new date is 2021, and the renewal term (5 years) isn't longer than 5 years.
> - **Raj** has the same facts, but his 2021 renewal **keeps the 2016 date**. In 2023, more than 5 years have passed since the mortgage date: he **can** tender payment plus 3 months' interest.

A lender **loses** its right to a prepayment penalty once it **accelerates** and demands the full balance (e.g. starts foreclosure).

### Criminal Code

- **Criminal rate**: from **January 1, 2025**, an **APR over 35%** (previously an effective rate over 60%). Loans made before 2025 aren't caught by the new rate.
- **Business exemption**: for commercial loans to borrowers that **aren't natural persons**: over $10,000 up to **$500,000** → up to **48% APR** allowed; over **$500,000** → **no** criminal rate.
- "Interest" here includes **fees, fines, penalties, bonuses, commissions** (and sometimes profit-sharing), so a criminal rate can be reached more easily than you'd think.
- **Mortgage fraud** offences include misusing mortgage proceeds under a power of attorney (s.331), an agent concealing title or mortgage information (s.385), and fraudulently selling while knowing of an unregistered mortgage (s.387).

> 🚨 ***R. v. Frebold*** **(BCCA 2001).** A licensee managing a commercial building for an overseas owner, under a power of attorney, borrowed **$1.725 million** against it, secretly lent **$672,000** of it to his own company, and **altered** the copy he faxed to the owner to hide it. Convicted of **fraud and forgery**: **30 months** in prison.

## Provincial law

### Business Practices and Consumer Protection Act (BPCPA)

**Unconscionable transactions (s.8).** A court considers all the circumstances, including:

- undue pressure;
- taking advantage of infirmity, ignorance, illiteracy, age or inability to understand;
- a price far out of line with similar deals;
- no reasonable chance the consumer could pay;
- terms so harsh as to be inequitable.

*Canmerica Mortgage v. Yu* (BCSC 2015) set out the common-law test: the borrower proves **inequality of bargaining power + substantial unfairness**; then the lender must prove the deal was **fair**. A high rate alone isn't enough: the court weighs the lender's **risk**, the borrower's alternatives and how desperate they were. If it intervenes, the court can **rewrite** the deal.

**Part 5 disclosure** (since **July 1, 2006**) applies to **individuals borrowing mainly for personal, family or household purposes**. A disclosure statement must be given:

| When | Trigger |
|---|---|
| **2 days** before the borrower is bound | Every credit agreement (waivable) |
| Every **12 months** | Floating rate |
| Within **30 days** | A **1%+** rise on a "fixed but changeable" rate; certain missed payments or default charges; any amendment |
| **21 days** before term ends | Renewal |
| **Monthly** | Open credit |

It must include, among other things, the term, amortization, advances, prepayment rights, **total cost of credit**, the annual rate and compounding, **APR**, other charges and default charges.

### Mortgage Brokers Act

A **mortgage broker** includes anyone who lends on mortgages as a business, holds out as a broker, trades mortgages or agreements for sale, earns **$1,000+ a year** in fees arranging mortgages, makes **10+ mortgage loans a year**, or collects mortgage payments as a business. Brokers and sub-mortgage brokers must be **registered** (exemptions include insurers, banks, credit unions, trust companies, lawyers in practice, government agents, trustees and court officers). The registrar can investigate, seize records, freeze trust funds, require bonds, and suspend or cancel registration.

### Environmental Management Act

A **secured lender** isn't liable for remediating a contaminated site just for protecting its security (financial involvement only; not causing contamination; environmental conditions in the loan; inspections). It **can** become liable if it **causes** contamination through control, or becomes the **registered owner** (e.g. by **order absolute**), until it sells.

> 🏢 **Commercial angle: environmental due diligence.** Industrial and commercial lenders will want **environmental site assessments** before lending, because foreclosing by order absolute could make them owners of a contaminated site. A contamination question can kill financing, so raise it early with industrial buyers.

## Assignment of a mortgage

The **lender** can sell (assign) the mortgage **without the borrower's consent**. Once the **assignee** gives the borrower **written notice**, the borrower must pay the assignee.

- The assignee takes **subject to the actual state of accounts** between the original parties, not what the registered mortgage says. If nothing was advanced, the assignee can't sue on the covenant.
- **Notice freezes the accounts.** Payments to the old lender **before** notice count; payments **after** notice don't.
- **Estoppel**: if the borrower **told** the assignee a balance, they can't later deny it.
- The assignee also takes subject to the borrower's **set-off** rights at the time of notice.
- The assigning lender isn't liable if the borrower defaults (unless it **guaranteed** the debt or committed fraud).

> 🧮 **Notice matters.** A lender assigns a $300,000 mortgage on March 1. On April 1 the borrower, not yet notified, pays the old lender $2,000. Notice arrives April 5. The borrower now owes the assignee **$298,000**; the assignee chases the old lender for the $2,000. If notice had arrived **before** April 1, the borrower would still owe the full $300,000 to the assignee.

## Assumption and novation

An **assumption** is when a **buyer takes over** the seller's mortgage. It can be attractive (a below-market rate, no new fees, avoids the seller's prepayment penalty). But:

> ⚠️ **The seller usually stays liable.** Unless released, the **original borrower** remains liable on the **personal covenant**. If the buyer defaults years later and the property sells for less than the debt, the lender can sue the original borrower for the **shortfall**. Be especially careful when the buyer is a **company with no assets**, or a property where the buyer has no other income.

- The buyer usually promises to pay and to **indemnify** the seller; the *Property Law Act* **implies** these promises unless excluded. That indemnity is worthless if the buyer is broke.
- **Direct action**: the *Property Law Act* lets a lender sue an **assuming buyer** directly, even though they never signed the original mortgage (privity is overridden). This applies to **all** lenders and purposes.

### Getting the seller released (Property Law Act)

For mortgages or agreements for sale for a **residential purpose** (to buy or improve the residence, household or family spending, or refinancing those):

| Route | How the seller's liability ends |
|---|---|
| **Lender approves the buyer in writing** | Request within **3 months** of the transfer. Lender can't **unreasonably** refuse; can ask for financial information and charge reasonable costs. If it refuses unreasonably, the seller can ask the **court**. |
| **Term expires** | Lender doesn't demand payment within **3 months** after maturity → seller released. |
| **Demand loan** | Lender doesn't demand payment within **3 months** of notice of the assumption → seller released. |

A **business** loan secured on a home doesn't qualify for this protection.

> 🎯 **What to put in the contract.** When a buyer assumes the seller's mortgage, use an assumption clause stating the approximate balance, rate and compounding, maturity date and payment, **subject to the lender approving the buyer in writing under s.24 of the *Property Law Act***, releasing the seller, by a set date. Make the condition for the benefit of **both** parties. You'll be the one drafting it, and liable if it's done carelessly.

### Novation

**Novation** replaces the old contract with a new one, releasing the original borrower (and guarantors). It applies to **any** mortgage or agreement for sale, not just residential. BC courts require:

1. the new borrower assumes **complete** liability;
2. the lender accepts the new borrower as **principal debtor** (not agent or guarantor);
3. the lender accepts the new contract in **full satisfaction** of the old.

A lender that approves an assumption and later **renews** with the buyer will often be found to have made a novation.

## Priorities

**Basic rule:** in BC, mortgages rank by **order of registration** at the land title office, not the date signed or funded.

- A mortgage that says it's **subject to** another ranks behind it even if registered first.
- Charge holders can rearrange order with registered **priority (postponement) agreements**.

### Redeem up, foreclose down

Picture the borrower at the **bottom** of the list. A lender can **foreclose** everyone **below** it and must **redeem** (pay off) anyone **above** it.

| Rank | Holder | Can foreclose | Must redeem |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st mortgage | $400,000 | 2nd, 3rd, borrower | nobody |
| 2nd mortgage | $100,000 | 3rd, borrower | 1st |
| 3rd mortgage | $50,000 | borrower | 2nd, then 1st |
| Borrower | owner | — | 3rd, 2nd, 1st |

If the 1st forecloses to order absolute, the 2nd and 3rd are wiped off title. If the 3rd forecloses, it becomes owner **subject to** the 1st and 2nd. Where there's equity, someone usually asks for a **sale** instead.

### Further advances

A first lender advancing funds in stages keeps priority over a later-registered second mortgage for those **later** advances only if:

1. it hasn't received **written notice** of the second mortgage in the prescribed way under the *Property Law Act* (oral notice doesn't count); **or**
2. its mortgage **obliges** it to make the advance (most say it doesn't have to); **or**
3. it has a **priority/postponement agreement** from the second lender (the most common fix).

### Statutory priorities: claims that can jump ahead of a registered mortgage

| Claim | Priority |
|---|---|
| **Builders lien** | Ahead of mortgage **advances made after the lien is filed**. |
| **Employment Standards** wage certificate | Ahead of nearly everything, **except** mortgage money advanced under a mortgage **registered before** the certificate. |
| **Property taxes** (*Local Government Act* / *Community Charter*; *Taxation (Rural Area) Act*) | Ahead of everything except the Crown, **without registration**. Unpaid **3 years** (2 rural) → **tax sale**; **1 year** to redeem, then title passes free of charges. |
| **Strata lien** | Ahead of mortgages; not ahead of Crown or builders liens; **fines excluded**. |
| **WorkSafeBC** (*Workers Compensation Act*) | Ahead of mortgages, except liens for workers' wages. |
| **CRA deemed trusts** (unremitted GST, source deductions, CPP/EI) | Ahead of security interests except the lender's "prescribed security interest". **No registry** to search; can be claimed **years later**. |

Lenders check title, tax status and strata accounts before every advance for exactly this reason.

## Foreclosure in BC

Foreclosure does two things: ends the borrower's **equitable right to redeem** and lets the lender **realize on its security**. There's no fixed timeline; it depends on serving respondents, court dates, the redemption period and how long a sale takes.

> 🚨 **Get help.** Foreclosures and court-ordered sales are complex. If you're inexperienced, talk to your **managing broker** and take a board course before taking one on.

### Step by step

1. **Demand letter** to borrowers and guarantors: default, **acceleration**, pay in full (usually a week or two).
   - Business or investment property of an insolvent borrower: ***Bankruptcy and Insolvency Act* s.244** notice, then wait **10 days**.
   - **Farmer** borrower: ***Farm Debt Mediation Act*** notice, then wait **15 business days**. Getting this wrong can void the whole foreclosure.
2. **Petition** filed in **BC Supreme Court** (usually the registry nearest the property), with a supporting **affidavit**. A **certificate of pending litigation** is registered on title.
3. **Respondents**: everyone with an interest registered **after** the lender (later mortgages, judgments) plus **residential tenants** if the lender wants vacant possession enforceable against them (they then must leave on the court's order, even mid-lease, without RTA notice).
4. **Relief sought**: declaration the mortgage is valid and in default; a **redemption period**; an **accounting** of what's owed (with a daily interest rate); **personal judgment** against borrower and guarantors (unless bankrupt); a **receiver** (abandoned or income-producing property); **possession**; **costs** (limited in BC).

### The court orders

| Order | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Order nisi** | Confirms the debt and sets the **redemption period**: usually **6 months** if there's equity; much shorter (even **one day**) if no equity, abandoned or being wasted. It's a **final** order (only appealable). **The owner can still list and sell** during this period. |
| **Conduct of sale** | Gives the lender the **exclusive** right to list (MLS) and market. The owner's right to sell, and any existing listing, **end**. Occupants must allow access. Every sale needs **court approval**. |
| **Order approving sale** | Court checks the price reflects **market value** (listing history, marketing, appraisal). Title passes **free and clear**; redemption rights end. |
| **Order absolute** | Lender takes **title**. Respondents' interests are extinguished. |

### Selling through the court

- The lender accepts an offer **subject to court approval**, then keeps marketing.
- At the hearing, anyone can submit **one sealed competing offer** that must beat the accepted one. The court usually approves the **highest**.
- Offers include the lender's **Schedule "A"** terms and are usually **"as is, where is"**.
- Proceeds go, in order: (1) property **taxes and utilities**, (2) **strata fees** with priority, (3) **commissions**, (4) the **petitioning lender** plus costs, (5) **into court** for later charge holders and the owner.
- After a **judicial sale**, the borrower remains liable for any **deficiency**.

### Order absolute vs judicial sale

| | **Judicial sale** | **Order absolute** |
|---|---|---|
| Who sells | Lender, under court supervision | Lender owns it outright; no supervision |
| Surplus | Paid into court for others | Lender keeps any profit; no accounting |
| Deficiency | Borrower and guarantors still **liable** | ***Property Law Act* s.32**: lender **loses** the right to sue on the covenant |
| Why choose it | Usual route | Big repairs needed; **insurer** requires title (bankrupt borrower); want to rent or wait for a better market; borrower is "judgment proof" anyway |

Property transfer tax is payable when title moves to the lender after order absolute.

> 🎯 **Licensee checklists for foreclosures.**
>
> - **Acting for an owner in foreclosure:** don't assume they can't sell. Call the **lender's lawyer**: what stage, what orders? Before conduct of sale or order absolute, they **can** sell; after order nisi, note the **redemption deadline**. Pull a **title search** for other mortgages, judgments and liens that must be cleared.
> - **Acting for the lender:** advise keeping **insurance** and **maintenance** going (reasonable costs can be added as "protective disbursements"). Expect to write a **marketing report** and possibly attend or testify at the approval hearing.
> - **Acting for a buyer:** find the **court dates yourself** (don't wait for the listing agent; buyers have missed their chance to bid). Explain **as is, where is**, subject to court approval, possible competing bids, and arrange **insurance** from the date the contract becomes unconditional. Amend standard clauses appropriately.

## Agreements for sale

An **agreement for sale** is a sale on credit: the buyer pays a down payment and instalments; gets **possession** now; and the seller signs over the **fee simple** when the price is fully paid.

| | **Vendor take-back mortgage** | **Agreement for sale** |
|---|---|---|
| Who's registered owner | **Buyer** | **Seller** (until paid in full) |
| Seller's interest | A **mortgage** charge | Buyer's interest registered as a **charge** ("right to purchase", **Form C**) |
| Financially | Same | Same |

**Remedies** mirror foreclosure: the seller petitions for **specific performance** or **cancellation** with forfeiture of payments; the court grants an **order nisi** with a redemption period (usually **6 months**); a **judicial sale** is possible; if not brought up to date, the agreement is **cancelled**.

> 🏢 **Commercial angle: creative financing closes deals.** In a slow market or with a hard-to-finance property (special-use building, a business owner without bank-friendly financials), seller financing through a **VTB** or **agreement for sale** can be the only way the deal happens. Your job is to make sure your client understands what it's **worth** and what **security** they're getting.

## How financing changes what an offer is worth

When the buyer pays all cash (with their own bank loan), financing doesn't affect the seller. But when the buyer **assumes** the seller's mortgage or the seller **lends** part of the price at a **non-market rate**, value shifts between the parties.

**Cash-equivalent price (market value of the offer):**

> **Cash down payment + market value of the mortgage = cash-equivalent price**

The **market value of the mortgage** = the present value of its payments (and any balance at the end of the term) **discounted at the market rate**. In other words: what would an investor pay today to buy that stream of payments?

> 🔑 **The see-saw.** Interest rates and present value move in **opposite** directions.
>
> - Contract rate **below** market → mortgage worth **less** than face → offer worth **less** than stated (a **discount**).
> - Contract rate **equal** to market → offer worth exactly what it says.
> - Contract rate **above** market → mortgage worth **more** than face → offer worth **more** than stated (a **bonus**).

### The three-step method

1. **Contract rate**: convert if needed, calculate the **payment** (round it, re-enter as negative) and, if partially amortized, the **balance at the end of the term** (enter as FV, negative).
2. **Market rate**: convert, set **N** to the number of payments the holder will actually receive, solve **PV** = market value of the mortgage.
3. Add the **cash down payment**.

> 🧮 **The scenario from Lesson 12.** Price **$900,000**: $300,000 down + **$600,000 VTB** at **j₂ = 3%**, 25-year amortization, monthly. Market rate **j₂ = 6%**.
>
> **Step 1 (contract):** convert 3% → j₁₂; 300 N, 600000 PV, 0 FV → PMT **$2,839.47**. (At market, the payment would be about $3,839, so the buyer saves roughly **$1,000 a month**.)
>
> **Fully amortized:**
>
> - Step 2: convert 6% → j₁₂ (5.926346%), keep N = 300, FV = 0 → **PV = $443,801.28**
> - Step 3: $300,000 + $443,801.28 = **$743,801.28**
>
> The "$900,000" offer is really about **$744,000 for the property** plus **$156,000 of cheap financing** handed to the buyer.
>
> **Counter with a 3-year term** (same payment, balance due after 36 months):
>
> - Step 1: 36 INPUT ■ AMORT → balance **$549,272.82**; enter as FV (negative).
> - Step 2: at the market rate, N = **36** → **PV = $553,446.07**
> - Step 3: $300,000 + $553,446.07 = **$853,446.07**
>
> Shortening the term limits the giveaway to 3 years, adding about **$110,000** of real value for the seller. (Strictly, use the market rate for a **3-year** term; we kept 6% for simplicity.)

Then the real question: **what is the property worth without the financing?** If it's worth $740,000, the fully amortized offer is fine. If it's worth $880,000, your seller is giving away a lot. Options: ask for more cash down, a higher rate or a **shorter term**.

> 🚨 **Discipline.** A licensee was reprimanded and ordered to take disciplinary education for **not explaining** to sellers the effect of financing terms well **below market** rates. You must explain it, or bring in someone who can.

### Above-market vendor financing

If the seller lends to a risky buyer at **above** market rates, the mortgage is worth **more** than face. The seller is being paid for the property **plus** a financing package (and for the risk and work of being a lender). The same three steps apply.

### Assumed mortgages

The same method, with two differences: **N** is the number of payments **remaining**, and the payment and end-of-term balance are usually already **known** from the existing loan.

> 🧮 **Assumption.** Three years ago the seller borrowed **$450,000** at **j₂ = 2.5%**, 25-year amortization, 5-year term. Payment **$2,015.85**; balance now **$409,556.81**; balance at maturity (in **24** months) **$380,870.58**. A buyer offers **$250,000 cash + assume the mortgage**. Market rate for a 2-year term: **j₂ = 5.5%**.
>
> - **Face value** of the offer: $250,000 + $409,556.81 = **$659,556.81** (what MLS and the LTO would show).
> - **Market value of the mortgage**: N = 24, PMT = −2,015.85, FV = −380,870.58, rate = j₂ 5.5% converted → **PV ≈ $387,448**
> - **Cash-equivalent price**: $250,000 + $387,448 ≈ **$637,448**
>
> The buyer is getting roughly **$22,000** of financing benefit. Is the seller being paid for it? Could the seller **port** the cheap mortgage to their next home and keep the benefit? Would assumption save the seller a **prepayment penalty**? Does the buyer qualify, and will the lender **release** the seller?

These calculations are a **starting point**; what the benefit is actually worth comes down to negotiation.

### Appraisers and non-market financing

A sale recorded at $659,557 that included $22,000 of financing benefit isn't a clean comparable. An appraiser must **adjust** for the financing (and ideally interview the parties), or leave the sale out.

### Portable mortgages

Most mortgages are **portable**, but only if the borrower **re-qualifies** under today's stress test, and within a limited window (often **30 to 120 days**). Most **variable** mortgages aren't portable. Extra money usually gets a **blended rate**. Portability makes sellers less likely to offer assumptions.

## Bonuses, brokerage fees and the true cost of borrowing

- A **lender bonus** is extra the borrower repays to boost the lender's yield.
- A **brokerage fee** pays a mortgage broker.
- Fees can be **deducted** from the advance (a **discount**: sign for $200,000, receive $190,000) or **added** to the face value (a **bonus**: need $190,000, sign for $200,000). Same effect: the borrower pays interest on more than they received. The amount received is the **net** amount; the face value is the **gross** amount.

Because the *Interest Act* treats bonuses as **principal**, the contract rate understates the true cost. So the **BPCPA** requires disclosure of the **APR** (contract rate plus non-interest finance charges) for consumer loans. The **cost of funds advanced (COFA)** is another measure (not legally required).

### Cost of funds advanced

1. Use the **gross** (larger) amount and contract rate to find the **payment** (and the **balance at the end of the term** if partially amortized).
2. Re-enter the **net** (smaller) amount actually received as **PV**; set **N** to the **term** (not the amortization) for a partially amortized loan.
3. Solve **I/YR**, then convert to the rate format the question asks for.

> 🧮 **A 5% fee on a $200,000 loan.** Face value **$200,000** at **j₂ = 8%**, 25-year amortization, monthly. A **$10,000** fee is deducted; the borrower receives **$190,000**.
>
> - Payment on $200,000: **$1,526.43**
> - **Fully amortized** (fee spread over 25 years): PV = 190,000, N = 300 → COFA ≈ **j₂ 8.62%**
> - **1-year term**: balance after 12 payments **$197,327.48**; PV = 190,000, N = 12, FV = −197,327.48 → COFA ≈ **j₂ 13.64%**
>
> The **shorter the term**, the **more** a fixed fee drives up the true rate. After one year the borrower owes more than they received. Prepaying early makes it worse.

> 💡 **Comparing loans with fees.** One loan may have a lower contract rate but a bigger fee; another a longer amortization. The only fair comparison is the **cost of funds advanced** (or APR) for each.

### Yield to the lender

Today most conventional brokerage fees are paid **by the lender**. Then the borrower's cost equals the contract rate, but the **lender's yield is lower**, because it paid out more than the loan amount.

> 🧮 A lender advances **$500,000** at **j₂ = 6%**, 25-year amortization, 5-year term, and pays the broker **1%** (**$5,000**).
>
> - Payment **$3,199.03**; balance after 60 months **$449,183.74**.
> - Lender's outlay = **$505,000** (enter PV as −505,000), N = 60 → yield ≈ **j₂ 5.75%**, not 6%.

### Investment yield

The same idea works for someone **buying** an existing mortgage at a discount (e.g. a developer selling its VTB loans to an investor): enter the **price paid** as PV, the remaining payments and balance, and solve for the yield. Buying at a **discount** pushes the yield **above** the contract rate.

> 🏢 **Commercial angle: always ask "all-in".** Private and MIC loans often come with lender fees, broker fees and both sides' legal costs. On a short bridge or construction loan, a 2% fee can add several points to the true annual rate. When your client compares lenders, compare **all-in costs over the realistic hold period**, not headline rates.

## Prepayment penalties

In a **closed** mortgage with a term of 5 years or less, the lender doesn't **have** to let the borrower prepay. Most will, for a penalty, typically the **greater** of:

- **three months' interest** on the amount prepaid (balance × monthly rate × 3), or
- the **interest rate differential (IRD)**: roughly the balance × (contract rate − current rate for the remaining term) × time left.

The IRD compensates the lender for lost interest when rates have **fallen**. Lenders calculate it differently (some use "posted" rates and discounts), so the penalty can be **far larger** than borrowers expect. You won't calculate penalties on the exam, but you must understand them.

| Category | Prepayment |
|---|---|
| **Open** | Any amount, any time, no penalty |
| **Closed** | Not allowed except by agreement (uncommon in pure form today) |
| **Partially open** | Allowed with a **penalty**; often **prepayment privileges** (e.g. 10% a year penalty-free) |

Product names don't decide the terms; the contract does.

> 🎯 **Licensee tip.** Before your seller signs a listing, have them ask their lender (or a mortgage broker) for the **payout figure and penalty**. A five-figure IRD penalty can wipe out the seller's expected net proceeds, and may make **porting** or a buyer **assumption** worth considering.

## Residential underwriting

For an owner-occupied home there's no rental income, so the lender looks at the **borrower's** income: **borrower qualification**.

### The five Cs of credit

| C | Question |
|---|---|
| **Character** | **Will** they repay? (job, history, stability) |
| **Capital** | How much of their own money is going in (down payment, savings habits)? |
| **Capacity** | **Can** they repay? (income, debt service ratios) |
| **Credit** | What's their repayment history (credit report)? |
| **Collateral** | What's the security (the property)? |

The lender's **first** concern is that the loan is **repaid as agreed**; the property is a **last resort**. Underwriting is about the default risk of **this** borrower and **this** property, not lending risk in general.

### Pre-approval

A **pre-approved** mortgage sets a maximum loan and usually **holds the rate for 60 to 120 days** (with the lower rate if rates fall). It's still subject to the **property** (appraisal) and final terms.

### Risk classes

- **Prime ("A")**: most Canadian lending; qualifies on credit and income.
- **Alt-A ("A minus")**: good credit but non-standard situations (e.g. **self-employed**).
- **Subprime ("B")**: poor or limited credit, non-verifiable income, past **consumer proposal** or **bankruptcy**. Higher rates and fees, lower LTVs, maybe guarantors. Canada's subprime market is far smaller and more conservative than the US's was before 2007–08.

### The application and credit analysis

- **Application**: personal, employment and income details; assets and liabilities; the financing needed; the property; a **subsequent costs** clause (borrower pays underwriting, legal, appraisal and survey costs if accepted); permission to check references and credit; signature.
- **Credit report** (Equifax, TransUnion): scores **300 to 900**; high 600s is good; **750+** gets the best terms; CMHC requires at least one borrower at **680**. A score of **0** may just mean no credit history. **R1–R9** (revolving) and **I1–I9** (instalment): 1 = paid on time, 9 = sent to collections. Discuss the report with the applicant; it doesn't show the story behind it.
- **Gross income**: definitions vary by lender and tighten when money is scarce (a form of **non-rate rationing**). Verified with employment letters, T4s, pay stubs, financial statements.
- **Self-employed**: OSFI requires a **35%** minimum down payment at federally regulated lenders. Help by having clean, current tax filings and statements.
- **Rental income**: insured loans may add **100%** of rent to income (CMHC won't count an **illegal** suite). Some conventional lenders use a **rental offset** (up to 90% of rent subtracted from the payment).
- **Interview**: will they pay, and will they look after the property?

> 🚨 **Mortgage fraud** means any material misstatement or omission a lender or insurer relies on: fake down payments, forged employment letters, forged title documents. Red flags: inconsistent documents, missing support, identity doubts. Verify, document and meet in person.

### Four different "values"

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Purchase price** | What the parties agreed. |
| **Purchase cost** | Price **plus** legal, appraisal, credit and other fees and any bonus (always higher than price). |
| **Market value** | Likely price between an informed, willing buyer and seller at arm's length, after reasonable exposure. |
| **Lending value** | A **long-term, conservative** value that ignores short-run or speculative factors. Usually **less** than market value, sometimes equal, almost never more. |

Lenders use the **lower** of market value and lending value, and if two appraisals differ slightly, the **lower** one.

### Loan-to-value

> **Maximum loan = LTV ratio × lending value**

- **Conventional**: up to **80%** at federally regulated lenders.
- **Insured**: up to **95%**.
- **Private lenders**: typically **75% to 90%**.

Lenders don't set LTVs very low, because competitors would win the business and borrowers would turn to costlier **second mortgages**, raising default risk. A **second-mortgage** lender cares about the **combined** LTV.

### Debt service ratios

> **GDSR** = (annual principal and interest + property taxes) ÷ gross income
>
> **TDSR** = (annual payments on **all** debts, including housing) ÷ gross income

Some lenders add heating, half of strata fees and junior mortgage payments to the GDSR (this course uses **P + I + T**). The **insured** maximums under the stress test are **GDSR 39%** and **TDSR 44%**; many lenders use lower limits. The lender lends the **lowest** amount that **all** constraints allow.

### The stress test

For **all** mortgages from **federally regulated** lenders (banks, trust and loan companies, insurers), the borrower must qualify at the **greater of 5.25% or the contract rate + 2%** (since June 1, 2021). Credit unions and other provincially regulated lenders aren't bound unless they choose to apply it. No stress test for an uninsured **straight switch** (since November 2024).

> 🧮 **Putting it together.** A buyer earns **$120,000**, has a **$700/month** car loan, and is buying a home with **lending value $800,000** and taxes **$3,600/year**. Lender: **80%** LTV, **GDSR 39%**, **TDSR 44%**, contract rate **j₂ = 4.5%**, 25-year amortization, monthly.
>
> 1. **LTV**: 0.80 × $800,000 = **$640,000**
> 2. **Stress rate**: greater of 5.25% or 4.5% + 2% = **j₂ 6.5%** (convert to j₁₂ ≈ 6.413688%)
> 3. **GDSR**: 0.39 × $120,000 = $46,800 − $3,600 taxes = $43,200 a year = **$3,600/month** → PV at 6.5% over 300 months ≈ **$537,455**
> 4. **TDSR**: 0.44 × $120,000 = $52,800 − $3,600 − $8,400 car = $40,800 = **$3,400/month** → PV ≈ **$507,596**
> 5. **Maximum loan = the lowest = $507,596** (TDSR binds).
>
> At a credit union not applying the stress test (j₂ 4.5%), the TDSR limit would be about **$614,303**. Paying off the car loan would raise the limit to the GDSR figure.

### Ways to borrow more (and their trade-offs)

| Change | Effect | Catch |
|---|---|---|
| **Longer amortization** | Lower payment → bigger loan | Slower principal repayment; more interest; more lender risk |
| **Shorter term at a lower rate** | Bigger loan for the same payment | Renewal risk if rates rise; repeat transaction costs |
| **Second mortgage** | More total debt | Higher rate, shorter amortization; limited by **combined LTV** and TDSR |

### Mortgage loan insurance

The **lender** is insured; the **borrower** pays a one-time premium (usually **added to the loan**). No annual or renewal fees. Since 2006, no application fees.

| LTV | CMHC premium (on total loan) |
|---|---|
| Up to 65% | 0.60% |
| 65.01–75% | 1.70% |
| 75.01–80% | 2.40% |
| 80.01–85% | 2.80% |
| 85.01–90% | 3.10% |
| 90.01–95% | 4.00% |
| 90.01–95%, non-traditional down payment | 4.50% |

- The premium added to the loan **isn't counted** in the LTV, but **is** included in the payment used for the **GDSR**.
- **Minimum down payment**: **5%** of the first **$500,000**, **10%** of the part from $500,000 to **$1.5 million**. **$1.5 million+** → **20%**, and **no** insured financing.
- **Low-ratio insurance** (20%+ down) only if: amortization generally ≤ **25 years** (30 for first-time buyers/new builds), price under **$1.5 million**, credit score **600+**, **owner-occupied**.
- On default, after the property is sold, the insurer pays the lender's **shortfall** and takes over the claim against the borrower.

> 🧮 **Premium example.** A $450,000 home with 5% down: loan = **$427,500** (95%). Premium 4% = **$17,100**, added → face value **$444,600**. Payments (and the GDSR) are based on $444,600.
>
> **Minimum down payment on $750,000**: 5% × $500,000 + 10% × $250,000 = **$50,000**.

### After approval

- **Covenants**: pay **property taxes** (many lenders collect them monthly in a tax account, because taxes rank ahead of the mortgage), keep **insurance** for **full insurable value** with the lender named (not just the loan amount, or the lender ends up sharing the payout with a second insurer), and **maintain** the property.
- **Cannabis**: insurers may refuse, surcharge or exclude grow-related damage, and lenders may decline properties used for growing (mould, electrical tampering). Ask whether cannabis has been grown there.
- **Interest adjustment**: interest from the **funding date** to the start of the first payment period, charged at the daily equivalent rate. Understand it; no calculation needed.
- **Arrears**: the lender must judge whether to give time, adjust terms or go to court.

## Commercial underwriting

**Commercial** underwriting covers **income-producing** property: rentals that aren't owner-occupied, apartments, retail, office, industrial, mixed-use, plus land and development loans. The focus shifts from the borrower's salary to the **property's net operating income (NOI)**. Lenders also weigh the borrower's **track record**, market conditions, the property's type, age, condition and location, and the **strength of the tenants' covenants**.

Two constraints again, and the **lower** wins.

### 1. Loan-to-value

Up to about **80%** in strong times; **60–65%** is typical in uncertain times.

### 2. Income: safety margin or debt coverage ratio

> **Safety margin**: payments may use only part of NOI. A 20% safety margin → maximum annual payments = **80% of NOI**.
>
> **DCR = NOI ÷ annual debt service**, so **maximum annual debt service = NOI ÷ DCR**.

They're two ways of saying the same thing: **DCR = 1 ÷ (1 − safety margin)**. A 20% margin = DCR **1.25**; 25% = **1.33**.

Typical DCRs: around **1.2** for apartments historically (1.3–1.35 in volatile times); **CMHC-insured** buildings higher; small (under 5 units) buildings lower; warehouses and industrial **1.4–1.5**.

> 🧮 **A small industrial building.** NOI **$240,000**. Lending value **$4,000,000**. Lender: **65%** LTV, **DCR 1.25**, **j₂ = 6%**, 25-year amortization, monthly.
>
> 1. **LTV**: 0.65 × $4,000,000 = **$2,600,000**
> 2. **DCR**: $240,000 ÷ 1.25 = **$192,000** a year = **$16,000/month** → at j₁₂ 5.926346% over 300 months, PV ≈ **$2,500,756**
> 3. **Maximum loan ≈ $2,500,756** (DCR binds; about 62.5% of value).
>
> If the buyer needs more, options include more equity, a lower rate, a longer amortization, a **mezzanine** loan, **vendor financing**, or raising NOI (better leases) **before** refinancing.

> 🏢 **Commercial angle: income is the loan.** For investment property, **NOI drives the loan amount**. That's why lenders scrutinize **rent rolls, lease terms, tenant quality, vacancy and expenses**. A building with short leases or weak tenants may appraise well and still get a small loan. When you list or buy commercial property, prepare clean income and expense statements early. They're the lender's underwriting file.

## Two alerts

> 🚨 **ALERT 1: Explain non-market financing, in dollars.** Whenever an offer involves a VTB, an assumption or an agreement for sale at a rate different from the market, your client must understand the **cash-equivalent value** and the **risks** (being a lender, continuing liability). Recommend **legal advice**. Failing to explain it has led to discipline.

> 🚨 **ALERT 2: Foreclosures run on court rules.** Find out what orders exist and the **court dates**, check title for other charges, and remember the owner's right to sell ends with **conduct of sale** or **order absolute**. Court-ordered sales are **as is, where is** and **subject to court approval** with possible sealed competing bids. Ask your managing broker if you're unsure.

## Trap list: things the exam likes to twist

1. A mortgage is an **interest in land as security**, not the loan itself. In BC it's treated as a **transfer** of the borrower's interest, though the borrower stays registered owner.
2. **Equity of redemption** = right to redeem after default; created by **equity**; ended by **foreclosure**.
3. Equitable mortgages bind the **borrower**, not third parties. Deposit of the duplicate certificate needs **intent**; can't be protected by caveat.
4. **Option to purchase** given to the lender with the loan = **clog** = void. Prepayment restrictions and no-assumption clauses are **not** clogs.
5. **Acceleration** is at the **lender's** option. Court relief under *Law and Equity Act* s.25, **once** per party and covenant.
6. **Omnibus** clause: lender pays and **adds to the mortgage**.

8. **Covenantor** = primary debtor; **guarantor** = secondary.

10. s.10 not for **corporations**; not for partnerships/ULCs/business trusts since **Jan 1, 2012**. Re-dating a renewal **restarts** the clock.
11. Bonus = **principal** under the Interest Act but **interest** under the Criminal Code.
12. Criminal rate: **APR over 35%** from **Jan 1, 2025**; business loans to non-individuals: **48%** cap for $10K–$500K; none over $500K.

14. Mortgage broker triggers include **$1,000+** fees a year or **10+** loans a year.
15. **Assignment**: no borrower consent needed; **notice** freezes accounts; assignee takes subject to the **actual** balance and set-off.
16. On **assumption**, the original borrower **stays liable** unless released. Lender can sue the assuming buyer **directly** (*Property Law Act*).
17. PLA release (residential purpose only): lender approves in writing (request within **3 months**; can't unreasonably refuse), or no demand within **3 months** after maturity (or of notice for a demand loan).
18. **Novation**: new borrower fully liable + accepted as principal debtor + new contract in full satisfaction.
19. Priority = **order of registration**, unless "subject to" or a priority agreement.
20. **Redeem up, foreclose down.**
21. Further advances keep priority if: **no written notice**, **obliged** to advance, or **priority agreement**.
22. Property taxes beat mortgages **without registration**; tax sale after **3 years** (2 rural); **1-year** redemption.
23. **Builders lien** beats advances made after filing; **strata lien** beats mortgages (not fines).
24. *BIA* s.244: **10 days**; *Farm Debt Mediation Act*: **15 business days** (defect can void the foreclosure).
25. **Order nisi**: redemption period, usually **6 months**; owner **can still sell**.
26. **Conduct of sale**: owner's right to list **ends**; existing listing terminated; sale needs **court approval**.
27. Sealed bid at the approval hearing must be **higher**; court usually takes the **highest**.
28. **Order absolute**: lender takes title, keeps any surplus, but **loses the covenant** (PLA s.32). Judicial sale: borrower liable for **deficiency**.
29. **Agreement for sale**: seller **stays on title**; buyer's interest registered (Form C, "right to purchase").
30. Cash-equivalent price = **cash down + market value of mortgage** (PV at **market** rate).
31. Contract rate **below** market → offer worth **less** than stated. **Above** → worth **more**.
32. Shorter term on a below-market VTB → **higher** cash-equivalent value for the seller.
33. Assumption: N = payments **remaining**.
34. COFA: payment from the **gross**, PV = **net**, N = **term**. Shorter term → **higher** COFA.
35. Lender pays the broker → **lender's yield falls**; borrower's cost unchanged.
36. Prepayment penalty: greater of **3 months' interest** or **IRD**.
37. Five Cs: **character, capital, capacity, credit, collateral**.
38. Credit scores **300–900**; **680** CMHC minimum (one borrower).
39. **Lending value** ≤ market value; lenders use the **lower**.
40. Conventional **80%**, insured **95%**.
41. **GDSR** = P + I + T; **TDSR** = all debts. Insured max **39% / 44%**. Lend the **lowest** result.
42. Stress test: greater of **5.25%** or **contract + 2%**; federally regulated lenders only.
43. Self-employed at federally regulated lenders: **35%** minimum down.
44. CMHC premium **4%** at 90.01–95%; premium excluded from LTV but included in GDSR.
45. Min down: **5%** to $500K, **10%** to $1.5M; **$1.5M+** = 20% and uninsurable.
46. Insure for **full insurable value**, not the loan amount.
47. Commercial: **NOI**; LTV 60–80%; **DCR = NOI ÷ debt service**; DCR **1.25** = 20% safety margin.

## Drill

**Q1.** A buyer will assume your seller's mortgage. The seller says, "Great, I'm off the hook." What do you tell them, and what do you put in the contract?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Not automatically. The seller stays liable on the **personal covenant** unless released. If the buyer defaults and a sale doesn't cover the debt, the lender can pursue the **seller** for the shortfall (and can also sue the buyer directly under the *Property Law Act*).

If the loan was for a **residential purpose**, the seller can be released if the lender **approves the buyer in writing** (request within **3 months** of the transfer; the lender can't unreasonably refuse). Use an assumption clause **subject to the lender approving the buyer in writing under s.24 of the *Property Law Act***, by a set date, for the benefit of both parties. Otherwise, aim for a **novation**. Recommend **legal advice**.

</details>

**Q2.** List the three ways a first-mortgage lender keeps priority for a construction advance made after a second mortgage is registered.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

1. It hasn't received **written notice** of the second mortgage in the prescribed way (oral notice doesn't count).
2. Its mortgage **obliges** it to make that advance.
3. It has a **priority (postponement) agreement** from the second lender. This is the usual solution.

</details>

**Q3.** Your seller's house is under foreclosure. An order nisi was granted two months ago with a six-month redemption period. Can you list it? What should you do first?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Yes**, the owner can still list and sell during the redemption period, as long as there's no **order for conduct of sale** or **order absolute**. First, contact the **lender's lawyer** to confirm what orders exist, the redemption deadline and the payout. Pull a **title search** for other mortgages, judgments and liens that must be paid to give clear title. Move fast: if the lender gets conduct of sale, your listing ends. Talk to your **managing broker** if you're new to foreclosures.

</details>

**Q4.** Offer: $200,000 cash + a $500,000 VTB at j₂ = 2%, 25-year amortization, monthly, **fully amortized**. Market rate j₂ = 6%. Without calculating, is the cash-equivalent price above or below $700,000? Then describe how you'd calculate it.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Below** $700,000: the contract rate is **below** market, so the mortgage is worth less than its face value (the see-saw).

1. Convert 2% → j₁₂; N = 300, PV = 500,000, FV = 0 → solve and round **PMT**; re-enter it as negative.
2. Convert 6% → j₁₂; keep N = 300, FV = 0 → solve **PV** = market value of the mortgage.
3. Add $200,000 cash = cash-equivalent price.

Then compare with what the **property alone** is worth, and consider countering with a **shorter term**, a higher rate or more cash.

</details>

**Q5.** A borrower needs $150,000. A private lender offers it at j₂ = 9%, but adds a $7,500 fee to the loan (25-year amortization, 1-year term). Another offers j₂ = 11% with no fee, on the same amortization and term. Which is cheaper, and how would you show it?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Calculate the **cost of funds advanced** for the first loan: payment on the **gross** $157,500 at 9% (converted to j₁₂, over the amortization), then the balance after 12 payments; then PV = **$150,000** (net), N = **12**, FV = −balance → solve I/YR and convert to j₂. A 5% fee squeezed into **one year** adds roughly **5 percentage points**, giving a true rate of about **j₂ 14.4%**. So the **11% no-fee loan is cheaper**. The shorter the term, the more a fee hurts.

</details>

**Q6.** A lender quotes your commercial buyer: 70% LTV, DCR 1.30. The building's NOI is $390,000 and its lending value $6,000,000. Which constraint is likely to bind, and why?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **LTV**: 0.70 × $6,000,000 = **$4,200,000**.
- **DCR**: maximum annual debt service = $390,000 ÷ 1.30 = **$300,000** ($25,000 a month). At typical rates (e.g. j₂ 6%, 25 years) that supports only about **$3.9 million**.

So the **DCR binds**. The lender lends the **lower** amount. To borrow more, the buyer needs more NOI, a lower rate, a longer amortization or a lower DCR (or extra equity or mezzanine money).

</details>

## Mindset: you're protecting people from expensive surprises

The biggest financial mistakes in real estate rarely show up in the price. They hide in the **financing**: a cheap VTB that quietly gives away six figures, an assumption that leaves the seller liable for years, a prepayment penalty that eats the profit, a short-term loan with fees that triple the real rate, a foreclosure sale that slips by because no one checked the court date.

You don't have to be a lawyer or a mortgage broker. You **do** have to recognize these situations, explain them in dollars, write protective clauses, and know when to send your client to a professional.

> 🏢 **For commercial specifically.**
>
> - **Underwriting is NOI-driven.** Know DCR, LTV and how lenders read rent rolls. You'll use these every day.
> - **Creative financing** (VTBs, assumptions, agreements for sale, mezzanine debt) closes commercial deals. Always calculate what it's worth.
> - **Foreclosures and receiverships** are a real source of commercial opportunities. Learn the court process, timelines and how sealed-bid hearings work.
> - **Environmental and statutory priorities** (tax liens, builders liens, CRA deemed trusts) can surprise both lenders and buyers. Search title and ask questions early.
> - **Prepayment penalties and yield maintenance** on commercial loans can be very large. Find out before listing.

> **This week's habit.** Take any listing with a mortgage on title (or make one up). Ask: if a buyer assumed it, or the seller carried part of the price at 2% below market, what would the offer really be worth? Run the three-step calculation until you can do it in under five minutes.
