## Why contract law is your toolkit

> **Scenario.** Your buyer offers **$2.4M** for a small industrial building, open until 6 p.m. Friday.
>
> - At 2 p.m. the seller's agent calls: "My client accepts, but wants completion moved up two weeks." Your buyer says, "Fine, I'll think about it," and goes to the gym.
> - At 4 p.m. a second buyer offers **$2.5M**. The seller now says, "Forget the first buyer. Their offer's dead anyway."
> - Your buyer gets back at 5 p.m., says "I accept the original deal at $2.4M," and asks if they have a contract.
>
> They **don't**. The seller's "accept, but change the date" was a **counter-offer**, which **killed** the original offer. Your buyer never accepted the counter-offer, so there was never a contract.

Every listing, offer, amendment and subject removal you'll ever handle is a **contract** question. Licensees get sued, and lose commissions, because they misunderstand basic contract rules. This lesson is the foundation; Lesson 8 applies it to the actual BC real estate forms.

## Vocabulary first

| Word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| **Contract** | A promise (or set of promises) the courts will enforce. |
| **Promisor / promisee** | The one making a promise / the one who can enforce it. In most real estate deals, each side is both. |
| **Offer / offeror / offeree** | A proposal ready to be accepted / the person making it / the person it's made to. |
| **Acceptance** | Unconditional "yes" to the offer as it stands. |
| **Counter-offer** | A reply that changes the offer. It **rejects** the original and becomes a new offer. |
| **Revocation** | The offeror withdrawing their offer before it's accepted. |
| **Consideration** | Something of value each side gives in the bargain (money, a promise, giving something up). |
| **Seal** | A formal mark that makes a promise binding even **without** consideration. |
| **Capacity** | The legal ability to make a contract. |
| **Void / voidable / unenforceable / illegal** | Four different ways a contract can fail (see below). |
| **Condition precedent** | A "subject to" clause: something that must happen before the main obligations kick in. |
| **Frustration** | An outside event after signing that destroys or fundamentally changes the deal. |
| **Breach** | Failing, without legal excuse, to do what you promised. |
| **Condition vs warranty** | A fundamental term vs a minor one. Breaching a condition lets the other side end the contract. |
| **Damages** | Money to compensate for a loss. |
| **Specific performance** | A court order to actually carry out the contract (e.g. transfer the property). |
| **Quantum meruit** | "As much as deserved": payment of a reasonable amount when none was agreed. |
| **Assignment** | Transferring your **benefits** under a contract to someone else. |
| **Privity of contract** | Only the parties to a contract can sue or be sued on it. |

## The seven essentials

A binding contract needs **all seven**:

1. **Offer**
2. **Acceptance**
3. **Consideration**
4. **Legal intention**
5. **Capacity**
6. **Legal object**
7. **Genuine consent**

Contracts can be oral, written, or a mix, **but** most contracts about **land** must be in **writing** to be enforced (*Law and Equity Act* **s.59**, Lesson 8).

> 🧠 **Memory aid:** "**O**nly **A** **C**areful **L**icensee **C**an **L**ock **G**ood deals": Offer, Acceptance, Consideration, Legal intention, Capacity, Legal object, Genuine consent.

## Four ways a contract can fail

| Type | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Void** | Never existed. Nobody can enforce it. Money paid is returned. | Both sides mistakenly think the cabin exists, but it burned down (**common mistake**) |
| **Illegal** | Against a statute or public policy. Void, and the court may refuse to help **either** side recover money. | A contract to commit a crime |
| **Voidable** | Valid **until** the party with the right cancels (rescinds) it | A minor's contract; one induced by misrepresentation, duress or undue influence |
| **Unenforceable** | All essentials present, but a court won't enforce it for a **procedural** reason | An **oral** contract for land (no writing under s.59) |

## 1. Offer

- Can be written, oral or by conduct, but must be **clear and unambiguous**. If a term has two reasonable meanings, the court enforces neither.
- Usually made to a **specific person**. Someone who overhears it can't accept it.
- **Standing offers** (like a public reward) can be accepted by anyone who meets the terms.
- **Invitation to treat**: most **ads, listings and shop displays** aren't offers. They invite people to make offers. An ad for a house at $700,000 doesn't bind the seller to sell to the first person who says "yes".

> 💡 **In practice:** the contract of purchase and sale usually starts as the **buyer's signed offer**, drafted by the buyer's licensee. Under Rules **s.30**, you must promptly communicate every signed offer you receive (unless your client instructs otherwise).

### How an offer ends

An offer ends when:

- A **stated time limit** runs out
- A **reasonable time** passes (if no limit was set)
- It's **revoked** before acceptance
- Either party **dies or becomes mentally incapable** before acceptance
- A **counter-offer** is made
- It's **rejected**

**Revocation can happen before the deadline.** "Open until noon tomorrow" doesn't mean the offeror has to wait. They can withdraw any time **before acceptance**, as long as the revocation is **actually communicated** (*Byrne v. Van Tienhoven*). It doesn't have to be written, but writing is smart.

> 🔎 **Two acceptances.** Val offers her property to Pete, open until noon. At 11 a.m., without revoking, she accepts Tom's offer. At 11:30 Pete accepts. Val now has **two contracts**. Under "first in time, first in right", Tom likely gets the property and Val owes Pete **damages**.

### Option agreements: keeping an offer open

To stop the offeror from revoking, the other side pays **separate consideration** to keep the offer open. That's an **option**: a separate contract. Example: the buyer pays $1,000 for a 30-day option at $750,000. If exercised, the $1,000 usually goes toward the price; if not, the seller keeps it.

> 🏢 **Commercial use:** developers **assemble land** with options on several neighbouring lots, exercising them only once they're sure they can get every parcel. Have a **lawyer** review any option you draft.

### Counter-offers vs inquiries

Changing **any** term creates a counter-offer, which **kills** the original offer. You **can't** go back and accept the original later.

An **inquiry** ("Is the fridge included?") doesn't kill the offer. The line can be thin.

> 🔎 ***Livingstone v. Evans* (Alberta, 1925).** Evans offered land at $1,800. Livingstone replied, "Send lowest cash price. Will give $1,600 cash." That was a **counter-offer**, so it ended the $1,800 offer. But Evans answered, "Cannot reduce price", which the court read as **renewing** the $1,800 offer. Livingstone then accepted, and won. Lesson: say clearly what you mean.

> 🚨 **ALERT:** before a client changes even one small term in an offer, make sure they understand they're making a **counter-offer** and may **lose** the deal entirely if the other side walks away.

## 2. Acceptance

- Must be **clear** and a **positive act**. Silence isn't acceptance ("if I don't hear from you, I'll assume yes" doesn't work).
- Use the **method the offer specifies**, or if none, the **same method** the offer used.
- Rules **s.47**: after getting a signed acceptance, promptly give a copy to **each party** (each co-buyer individually) and to your brokerage.

### When does acceptance take effect?

| Method | Effective when |
|---|---|
| **Instantaneous** (phone, and probably fax) | **Received** by the offeror. If the line drops, no contract. |
| **Non-instantaneous** (mail) | **Posted**: the **postal acceptance rule**. Lost in the mail? Still a contract (if you can prove you mailed it). |

- Use a **slower** method than expected? You take the risk: it counts only if it arrives before the offer lapses.
- Ignore a **specified** method? The offeror can probably reject it.
- **Revocation has no postal rule.** It must always be **actually received**.

## 3. Consideration

Each side must give something of value: money, a promise, a benefit, or giving up a right. In a sale, the seller's promise to transfer title and the buyer's promise to pay are consideration for each other; nothing has to change hands at signing.

- Courts **don't judge adequacy**. **$1** works as well as $100,000 (unless there's fraud, duress, undue influence or misrepresentation).
- **Past consideration doesn't count.** Jane paints Tom's fence as a favour; afterwards Tom promises $50. No contract: the painting was done before the promise.
- **Gratuitous promises** (no consideration) generally aren't enforceable, **unless under seal**.

### Seals

A seal makes a promise binding without consideration. Today it can be a red sticker or even "SEAL" in a circle, **but** the signer must **know** they're sealing it and **mean** to.

> 🔎 ***Pooni v. Storsley* (2022 BCSC).** The standard BC contract's pre-printed black "SEAL" circles weren't consciously applied in an e-signed deal, so the contract **wasn't under seal**. Since there was no consideration, there was **no enforceable contract** yet. The standard form was then changed to add **initial boxes** next to the seals, confirming the signer understands and intends the seal.

**Corporate seals** just show a company approved the document. They **don't** replace consideration.

### Amendments

An amendment is a contract to change a contract, so traditionally it needed **fresh consideration** (often **$1**) or a seal. *Rosas v. Toca* (2018 BCCA) says agreed variations can be enforced **without** fresh consideration (absent duress, unconscionability or public policy problems). **Safest practice:** amend **in writing**, with **$1** or **under seal**.

### Quantum meruit

If someone requests services with no agreed price, the law implies a promise to pay a **reasonable** amount. It also applies to partial performance after a breach, work done under a void contract, and substituted performance.

> 🔑 ***Tapestry Realty v. Power Peak* (2009 BCSC).** The seller didn't **initial** page 1 of the listing contract showing a **10%** commission. The licensee couldn't prove the rate, so the court awarded **quantum meruit** of **7%**, costing them about **$30,000**. Fill out and initial **every** part of your service agreements.

## 4. Legal intention

The parties must intend to create legal obligations. Courts look at it **objectively** ("would a reasonable person think they meant to be bound?").

- **Commercial deals and strangers:** presumed **to intend** legal relations.
- **Family and close friends:** presumed **not** to.
- Either presumption can be rebutted with evidence.

## 5. Capacity

### Minors ("infants")

In BC, anyone **under 19**. Their contracts are generally **unenforceable against them** but **enforceable by them** (*Infants Act* s.19), even if the adult didn't know their age.

A contract **becomes enforceable** against them if:

- Another statute says so
- They **affirm** it after turning 19
- They **perform** it (even partly) within **1 year** of turning 19
- They **don't repudiate** it within **1 year** of turning 19

Courts have broad discretion over remedies (restitution, compensation, release).

### Mental incapacity

Everyone is **presumed capable**. Incapacity (from dementia, some mental illnesses, substance use) matters if the person can't understand the contract's terms **and** how it affects them, **and** the other party **knew**.

Under *Adult Guardianship Act* **s.60.2**, a transfer by an incapable adult stands only if the buyer paid **full and fair value**, or had **no reasonable way of knowing**.

> 🚨 **ALERT:** a licensee was fined **$1,000** and suspended **30 days** after a 90-year-old client with dementia sold her house to a builder **below value** and bought a condo from the same builder **above value**. He should have made sure she got **independent legal advice**.

**When a client seems confused**, take these steps:

1. Check for a **power of attorney**, **enduring POA** or **committee** (adult guardian).
2. If there is one, advise **legal advice**. The original document will be needed at the land title office.
3. Talk to your **managing broker** and get legal advice when dealing with attorneys or guardians.
4. If there's none, insist on **independent legal advice** (and a **Certificate of Independent Legal Advice**) before any listing or deal.
5. **Never accept** a POA yourself without legal advice.

### Powers of attorney and related documents

| Document | Key points |
|---|---|
| **Power of attorney (POA)** | Lets an attorney make financial/legal decisions for the donor. **Ends** on the donor's death, **incapacity** or bankruptcy. A POA dealing with **land** is valid only **3 years** from signing (*LTA* s.56). |
| **Enduring POA** | **Survives incapacity**; not limited to 3 years. May only start when a trigger event happens. |
| **Committee (adult guardian)** | Court-appointed when there's no enduring POA. Once appointed, the adult **can't contract**, even on a good day. Sometimes the **Public Guardian and Trustee**. |
| **Representation agreement** (since Sept 1, 2011) | **Can't** authorize buying or selling real estate. |
| **Advance directive** | Health care only. No real estate. |

**Why the land title office rejects POAs:** the name doesn't exactly match the title; alternate names ("aka"); wrong property or missing power to sell or mortgage; out-of-province POA with no solicitor's certificate; **expired**; donor dead or incapable; enduring POA not yet triggered.

**When a POA is involved:** get a **true copy** and read it all, consider asking family whether anyone else holds one, check lender policies, consult your brokerage, and send everyone for **legal advice**.

> 🚨 A licensee was fined **$750** and suspended **7 days** for ignoring the **Public Guardian and Trustee**, who was committee for an owner and had registered a **caveat**.

### People who can't read the language (or can't see)

They're bound if they knew the **general nature** of the document. They must ask for it to be read and explained. If it was **fraudulently misrepresented** to them, they may plead ***non est factum*** ("not my deed").

### Corporations

Make sure the company **actually exists**. At common law, a company can't adopt a contract made **before** it was incorporated. BC's *Business Corporations Act* now allows BC companies to ratify pre-incorporation contracts, but federal and other-province companies follow different rules. **Search the registry, and get legal advice.**

> 🏢 **Commercial angle:** buyers often sign "**ABC Holdings Ltd. or nominee**" or for a company "**to be incorporated**". Confirm who the legal buyer is, that it exists, and who can sign for it, before you rely on the contract.

### Estate sales

1. **Search title.** If there's a surviving **joint tenant**, the property passes to them and **isn't** part of the estate.
2. **With a will:** the **executor** needs a **Grant of Probate** (often 3–6 months). They can list and sign a contract **subject to probate**.
3. **No will:** an **administrator** needs **Letters of Administration** and has **no authority** until then, so **don't** sign contracts with them before it's granted.
4. Signature format: "*[Name], Executor (or Administrator) of the Estate of [Deceased]*".

## 6. Legal object

Contracts to break a statute or public policy are void, and courts may refuse to help a party who knew about the illegality.

> 🔎 ***Shafazand v. Whitestone* (2014 BCSC).** A builder and owner agreed to build a house with a **hidden illegal suite** (concealed until after final inspection). When they sued each other, the court refused to let the owner collect the **$42,000** balance it was otherwise owed, partly because it had already profited by selling the house. Both claims were dismissed.

## 7. Genuine consent

Five things can destroy consent: **misrepresentation**, **mistake**, **duress**, **undue influence** and **unconscionability**.

### Misrepresentation

A false **statement of fact** that **induced** the contract, of a kind that would induce a **reasonable person**.

- **Fact vs opinion:** "We replaced the roof last year" is fact. "I don't think the roof needs replacing for a few years" is opinion and usually not actionable, **except** an **expert's** opinion, which is treated as fact. **A licensee is an expert.**

| | **Innocent misrepresentation** | **Fraudulent misrepresentation** |
|---|---|---|
| Knew it was false? | No | Yes, or **reckless** |
| Rescission | Only **before completion** (not after land is transferred) | **Any time** |
| Damages | **No** | **Yes** (in tort) |

**"Entire agreement" clauses** ("no representations except those in this contract") protect a **seller** against **innocent** misrepresentations, but **never** against **fraud**, and **never protect the licensee**, who isn't a party to the contract.

> 🚨 ***Betker v. Williams* (1991 BCCA).** Both licensees advertised a lot as a **building lot** when zoning didn't allow a house. Despite the exclusion clause, they were **negligent**, and the seller was **vicariously liable**. A misrepresentation that goes to the **whole purpose** of the purchase beats an exclusion clause the buyer's attention wasn't drawn to.

### Defects: caveat emptor and its limits

**Caveat emptor** ("let the buyer beware") is the starting point. Buyers must inspect and ask.

| | **Patent defect** | **Latent defect** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Visible, or findable through reasonable inspection and inquiry | **Not** discoverable through reasonable inspection |
| Examples | Broken windows, water stains, restrictive covenants on title | Hidden mould, contaminated soil, structural problems |
| Seller must disclose? | **No.** Buyer's job (with experts) | Only if **known** (or should have been known) **and** it makes the property **dangerous or unfit to live in** |

- Sellers can't use **wilful blindness** (deliberately not checking a suspicion).
- *Gibb v. Sprague* (Alberta): **ants in the roof** = latent but **unknown**, so no liability. **Basement mould** = known (past floods, air fresheners hiding the smell), so liable. **Electrical** problems = **patent** (the buyer's friend noticed the panel issues), so no liability.
- Conditions **off** the property (e.g. landslide risk from next door, radioactive soil nearby) can count if known and dangerous.

> 💡 **Always advise an inspection.** Home inspectors in BC must be **licensed** (by Consumer Protection BC). Inspections don't cover environmental issues, so suggest **specialists** where needed (e.g. old oil tanks). If the buyer declines an inspection, **document** your advice.

### The licensee's wider duty: Rules s.59

When you act for a **seller** (or landlord), you must disclose any **material latent defect** you **know** about to **all other parties**, **in writing**, **before any agreement**, and **not** in the contract itself. It's **broader** than a seller's common law duty:

A **material latent defect** is a defect not discernible by reasonable inspection that:

- (a) makes the property **dangerous** or potentially dangerous, **unfit to live in**, or **unfit for a purpose** the buyer told you about (or you otherwise know)
- (b) would be **very expensive** to fix
- (c) is the subject of a **local government notice** saying it must or should be fixed
- (d) **lack of required permits**

So an **illegal suite** may not be a common law latent defect for the seller (easy to check with the city), but under (d) the **listing licensee must disclose it**.

> 🚨 **If your client tells you not to disclose a material latent defect, you must stop acting for them** on that trade.

**Specific hazards:**

- **Underground oil tanks:** regulated by the BC Fire Code and local bylaws (e.g. Vancouver requires removal or a permit after **2 years** out of service). If you suspect one, **ask**; once you know, **disclose** and flag the environmental concern.
- **Radon** above **200 Bq/m³**: BCFSA treats it as a **material latent defect**. The Property Disclosure Statement asks about it.

### Defects vs stigmas

A **stigma** affects value for **subjective** reasons: a past death or crime, rumoured hauntings, a notorious former occupant. *Summach v. Allen* (2003 BCCA): a nearby **nude beach** wasn't a defect, because whether it's a problem depends on personal taste.

- **Sellers generally needn't disclose stigmas.** Buyers must ask.
- If a buyer **asks** you (as seller's agent), **answer truthfully or decline** to answer. **Never lie.**
- Advise sellers that disclosing is usually safer than a collapsed deal or lawsuit.
- **For buyers:** ask what matters to them, ask the seller's side and neighbours, search online, and add a **seller warranty clause** about specific stigmas.

### Mistake

Must be about a **fact** that goes to the **root** of the contract.

| Type | What happens | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Common** | Both make the **same** mistake (the cabin had already burned down) | **Void** |
| **Mutual** | Each makes a **different** mistake. Two ships named "Peerless" (*Raffles v. Wichelhaus*) | **Void** if no reasonable interpretation wins; otherwise the court picks the more reasonable one |
| **Unilateral** | One side is mistaken and the **other knows** and stays quiet | Court can correct it (*Beverly Motel*: the buyer "snapped at" a transfer that wrongly included the seller's **second lot** and had to give it back) |

***Non est factum*** is a special unilateral mistake: signing a document of a **completely different nature** from what you were told (an illiterate owner who thinks she's signing a will but is actually signing a deed).

Courts **presume you read what you sign**. Most mistakes won't get you out of a contract.

### Duress, undue influence and unconscionability

All three make a contract **voidable**:

| | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Duress** | Forced by actual or threatened **violence** or imprisonment (to you or those close to you), or **economic duress** (unlawful financial threats that take away free will) |
| **Undue influence** | Someone in a **dominant** position (doctor, lawyer, adviser) uses it to push the other into a deal they wouldn't otherwise make |
| **Unconscionability** | **Inequality** (ignorance, need, distress) **plus** a **substantially unfair** bargain. The stronger party must then prove the deal was fair. |

## How contracts end

| Way | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Performance** | Everyone does what they promised. **Substantial** performance counts; refusing it is a breach. |
| **Agreement** | Parties agree to waive the rest or replace the contract (needs consideration or seal unless both still have obligations left) |
| **Condition precedent not met** | A "subject to" clause fails, and the contract ends |
| **Frustration** | An outside event **after** signing destroys or fundamentally changes the deal |
| **Breach** (accepted) | A **fundamental** breach the innocent party accepts as ending the contract |

Some buyers also have **statutory rescission rights**: REDMA's 7-day right (Lesson 5) and the **Home Buyer Rescission Period** under the *Property Law Act* (3 business days, 0.25% fee, Lesson 8).

### Conditions precedent ("subjects")

A **condition precedent** is an event (other than just time passing) that must happen before the parties must perform: subject to financing, inspection, sale of the buyer's home, etc. Until subjects are removed, the seller can only accept **backup offers**.

**Waiver** (*Law and Equity Act* **s.54**): a party can waive a condition precedent if:

1. It's for **their sole benefit**
2. The contract can be performed without it
3. They waive it **before** the deadline (or within a reasonable time if there isn't one)

If it benefits **both** parties, **both** must agree to waive it. So always write: ***"This condition is for the sole benefit of the [Buyer/Seller]."***

### Frustration

An event **outside the parties' control**, after the contract is made, destroys the subject or makes performance **fundamentally different**, and the contract didn't provide for it. Examples: the house burns down before completion (unless the contract covers it); an unexpected **rezoning** wipes out the buyer's intended use (*Capital Quality Homes v. Calwyn*, Ontario: deposit returned). You **can't frustrate a contract yourself**: selling your own equipment to dodge a job is a breach.

> 🧠 **Common mistake vs frustration:** same idea, different timing. Destroyed **before** the contract = common mistake (void). Destroyed **after** = frustration.

### Breach

A breach can happen three ways:

1. **Anticipatory breach**: announcing in advance you won't perform. The innocent party can **sue now**, or wait until the performance date and then sue.
2. Making performance **impossible** (e.g. selling the property to someone else).
3. **Failing to perform** on the due date.

**Condition vs warranty:**

| | **Condition** (fundamental) | **Warranty** (minor) |
|---|---|---|
| Example | Completing on the completion date ("**time is of the essence**"); refusing to complete | Painting the bedroom before possession |
| Innocent party can | **(1)** End the contract and sue for damages, **(2)** continue and sue for damages, or **(3)** continue and sue for **specific performance** | Sue for **damages** only, and must still perform |

> 🔎 ***Gill v. Bal* (2017 BCSC).** "Time is of the essence" and documents due by **4:00 p.m.** The buyers' mortgage funds weren't confirmed until **5:36 p.m.** That was a breach of a condition; the contract ended and the sellers got damages. Courts **enforce deadlines strictly**. Be careful with specific **clock times** that depend on lenders or other people.

**Good faith** (*Bhasin v. Hrynew*, SCC 2014): parties must perform contracts **honestly**, not capriciously, and must not knowingly mislead each other about performance. It doesn't mean putting the other side first.

> 🚨 Rules **s.48**: **never** persuade a party to break an existing contract so they can sign a different one with you.

## Assignment and privity

**Privity:** only the parties can sue or be sued on a contract. Exceptions include interests that **run with the land** (Lesson 2) and **assignment**.

You can assign **benefits**, not **burdens**. If A owes B $1,000, B can assign the right to collect to C (C must agree; A's consent isn't needed). A can't assign the debt.

**Statutory assignment** (*Law and Equity Act* **s.36**) needs **three** things:

1. **In writing**
2. **Absolute** (the whole thing) and **unconditional**
3. **Written notice** to the original promisor

Then the assignee can sue directly. If any is missing, it may still be an **equitable** assignment, but all three people must be parties to any lawsuit.

> 🏢 **Assignment of real estate contracts (Regulation s.8.2).** Offers drafted by licensees must include the **standard assignment terms**, unless the buyer instructs otherwise:
>
> 1. No assignment without the **seller's written consent**
> 2. The **seller gets any profit** from an assignment
>
> If they're left out, the buyer's licensee must deliver a **prescribed notice** to the seller's side recommending professional advice. (REDMA pre-sales are handled differently.)

**Vicarious performance** isn't assignment. You can have someone else **do** the work (a subcontractor), but you **stay liable**. Not allowed for **personal** contracts (like commissioning a portrait).

## Remedies

| Remedy | Type | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Damages** | **Common law**, available **as of right** | Put the innocent party where they'd be if the contract had been performed |
| **Specific performance** | **Equitable**, discretionary | Only where **damages aren't adequate**, e.g. the property is genuinely **unique** |
| **Injunction** | **Equitable**, discretionary | Stop someone doing something (or, as a **mandatory** injunction, make them do it). Disobeying = contempt. |
| **Quantum meruit** | Restitution | Reasonable payment where no price was fixed |

**Damages details:**

- *Hadley v. Baxendale*: you recover losses that **flow naturally** from the breach or were **foreseeable** when the contract was made.
- A seller whose buyer walks can recover resale costs and any **price drop** on resale, if they made reasonable efforts.
- The innocent party must **mitigate** (keep losses reasonable).
- A **liquidated damages** clause is enforceable if it's a **genuine pre-estimate** of loss; otherwise it's an unenforceable **penalty**.

**Specific performance and land:** historically every property was "unique", so buyers usually got it. The SCC has said that's **no longer automatic** for mass-produced homes and business properties. The buyer must show **no reasonable substitute exists**. *Sihota v. Soo* (2010 BCSC): granted, because of a 2-year search, a corner lot for a planned house, a school behind it, and ill parents within walking distance.

> 💡 **Commission and quantum meruit:** if a seller verbally promised a commission and then refuses to pay, you **might** recover a reasonable amount (*Banfield v. Hoffer*, Manitoba), but **proving** the promise is the hard part. Get it in writing.

## Two alerts

> 🚨 **ALERT 1: Any change is a counter-offer.** One altered term kills the original offer. Make sure your client understands before they change anything.

> 🚨 **ALERT 2: Fill in, sign and initial everything.** Missing initials cost one licensee about $30,000 (*Tapestry*). Missing seal acknowledgements left a buyer with no contract (*Pooni*). Missed deadlines ended a deal (*Gill v. Bal*).

## Trap list: things the exam likes to twist

1. **Seven** essentials: offer, acceptance, consideration, legal intention, capacity, legal object, genuine consent.
2. **Void** = never existed. **Voidable** = valid until rescinded. **Unenforceable** = valid but procedurally barred. **Illegal** = void and courts may refuse help.
3. An **ad or listing** is usually an **invitation to treat**, not an offer.
4. An offer "open until Friday" **can still be revoked** before acceptance, and revocation must be **communicated**.
5. To keep an offer open, you need an **option** with **separate consideration**.
6. A **counter-offer kills** the original offer. An **inquiry** doesn't.
7. **Silence** is never acceptance.
8. **Postal acceptance rule**: mailed acceptance is effective when **posted**. **Revocation** has no such rule.
9. Courts **don't weigh adequacy** of consideration; **$1** is enough. **Past consideration** doesn't count.
10. A **seal** replaces consideration only if the signer **knowingly** applied it (*Pooni*). **Corporate seals** don't replace consideration.
11. Family/friends: presumed **no** legal intention. Commercial: presumed **yes**.
12. Minors (under **19**): contract generally **unenforceable against** them, **enforceable by** them.
13. POA over land: valid **3 years** (*LTA* s.56); ends on death, **incapacity** or bankruptcy. **Enduring** POA survives incapacity.
14. **Representation agreements** (since **Sept 1, 2011**) **can't** authorize real estate sales.
15. Administrator: **no authority** until **Letters of Administration**. Executor may list **subject to probate**.
16. **Innocent** misrepresentation: rescission only **before completion**, **no damages**. **Fraudulent**: rescission any time **and** damages.
17. An **expert's opinion** is treated as **fact**, and licensees are experts.
18. "**Entire agreement**" clauses **never protect the licensee**, and never protect anyone from **fraud**.
19. Sellers must disclose only **known latent** defects that make a property **dangerous or unfit to live in**.
20. **Rules s.59** is **broader**: includes great expense, local government notices and **missing permits**; must be **in writing**, **before** agreement, **not in the contract**.
21. Client says "don't disclose" a material latent defect → you must **stop acting**.
22. **Stigmas** generally needn't be disclosed, but **never lie** when asked.
23. **Common** mistake = same mistake (**void**). **Mutual** = different mistakes. **Unilateral** = other side knew.
24. Waiving a subject (*LEA* s.54): **sole benefit**, contract still workable, **before the deadline**.
25. **Frustration** = after the contract; **common mistake** = before. You can't frustrate a contract yourself.
26. Breach of **condition** → three choices. Breach of **warranty** → damages only.
27. Statutory assignment: **written**, **absolute**, **written notice** to the promisor. Assign **benefits**, not burdens.
28. **Damages** are a **right**; **specific performance** and **injunctions** are **discretionary** (equitable).
29. **Liquidated damages** OK if a **genuine pre-estimate**; otherwise an unenforceable **penalty**.

## Drill

**Q1.** Your seller client receives an offer at $1.8M, open until 5 p.m. They want to "accept, but with possession two weeks later". What do you tell them?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Changing possession is a **counter-offer**, not an acceptance. It **ends** the buyer's original offer. If the buyer rejects the counter-offer, the seller **can't** go back and accept the $1.8M offer as it was. The buyer becomes free to walk away. Make sure the seller understands that risk before changing anything, and that only the buyer can now accept (or counter).

</details>

**Q2.** A buyer's offer says it's open until noon tomorrow. At 10 a.m., before the seller accepts, the buyer's agent phones to withdraw it. The seller says, "They can't, it's open until noon." Who's right?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

The **buyer**. An offer can be **revoked any time before acceptance**, even if it says it's open until a later time, as long as the revocation is **communicated** to the offeree (it was, by phone). The only way to force an offer to stay open is an **option** backed by **separate consideration**. Best practice: confirm the revocation in writing.

</details>

**Q3.** Explain the difference between void, voidable and unenforceable, with a real estate example of each.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Void**: never existed. Example: both parties contract to sell a cottage that had already burned down (**common mistake**).
- **Voidable**: valid until the entitled party rescinds. Example: a buyer induced by the seller's **misrepresentation** about the roof can choose to rescind (before completion, if innocent; any time, if fraudulent).
- **Unenforceable**: all essentials present but a court won't enforce it. Example: an **oral** agreement to sell land (no writing under *Law and Equity Act* s.59).

</details>

**Q4.** Your buyer's contract has "subject to the buyer obtaining financing by June 1. This condition is for the sole benefit of the Buyer." On May 25, the buyer inherits money and wants to firm up without a mortgage. Can they?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Yes. Under *Law and Equity Act* **s.54**, a party can waive a condition precedent if it's for their **sole benefit** (stated expressly here), the contract can be performed without it (they can pay cash), and the waiver is made **before the deadline** (May 25 < June 1). They give written notice of waiver (subject removal), and the contract becomes firm.

</details>

**Q5.** You're listing a building. The seller tells you the upper floor was converted to offices **without permits**, but says "don't mention it, the city never checks". What do you do?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Lack of required permits is a **material latent defect** under **Rules s.59(d)**. You must disclose it **in writing** to all other parties **before** any agreement (not in the contract). Explain this to the seller. If they still instruct you not to disclose, you **must stop acting** for them on this trade. Talk to your managing broker. Staying quiet would also expose you to misrepresentation claims and discipline.

</details>

**Q6.** An elderly owner wants to list her home. Her son says he has power of attorney and will sign everything. The POA was signed four years ago. What should you check?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- Get a **true copy** and read it all.
- An ordinary POA dealing with land is valid only **3 years** from signing (*LTA* s.56), so a 4-year-old one has **expired** unless it's an **enduring** POA.
- Check whether it's enduring, whether it's actually **in effect** (some only start on incapacity), whether it covers **this property** and the power to **sell**, and that names match title exactly.
- Ask whether the mother is capable; if she is, consider having her sign or confirm directly.
- Ask whether anyone else holds a POA or a committee has been appointed.
- Talk to your managing broker, and advise everyone to get **legal advice** before listing.

</details>

**Q7.** Completion is at 4 p.m. with "time is of the essence". The buyer's lender funds at 4:45 p.m. The seller refuses to complete. Who's in breach, and what can the seller do?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

The **buyer** breached a **condition** (time of the essence makes the deadline fundamental), as in *Gill v. Bal*. The seller can **end the contract and sue for damages** (resale costs and any price drop, subject to the duty to **mitigate**). The seller may also keep the deposit, depending on the contract terms (Lesson 8). Lesson for licensees: build in buffer time, and be careful promising clock times that depend on third parties.

</details>

## Mindset: write it down, clearly, every time

Almost every case in this lesson turned on something **small**: one changed term, one missing initial, one unticked seal box, one ambiguous sentence, 45 minutes late. Contract law rewards people who are **precise** and **boring** in the best sense.

Build these habits now:

- **Read every word** before anyone signs.
- **Fill every blank** (or strike it), and get **every initial**.
- Say **who benefits** from every subject.
- **Confirm in writing** anything said out loud.
- When something unusual comes up (POA, estate, company not yet formed, illegal suite, assignment), **slow down and get legal advice**.

> 🏢 **For commercial specifically.** Commercial deals are almost all **custom contracts**, drafted and redrafted by lawyers, with options, assignments, nominee buyers, long subject periods and complicated conditions. You won't be the lawyer, but the best commercial agents **understand contract mechanics well enough to spot problems early**: an offer that's really a counter-offer, a subject that benefits both sides, a buyer company that doesn't exist yet, a deadline nobody can meet. That's what makes lawyers and clients trust you.

> **This week's habit.** Find a copy of BCREA's standard **Contract of Purchase and Sale** (your course materials include it) and, for each section, label which of the **seven essentials** it supports. You'll be ready for Lesson 8.
