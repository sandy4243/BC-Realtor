## Why a real estate course starts with law

Start with the problem before the rules.

> **Scenario.** You help Priya buy a small retail building in Surrey for $2.4 million. During a showing you mention, casually, "the roof was redone about five years ago." You heard that from the seller and didn't check. Eight months after Priya moves in, the roof fails. It was actually redone eleven years ago. Repair cost: $180,000.
>
> Priya is furious. Who pays?

Notice what happened. **You didn't sell her a building.** You gave her *information and advice* about a legal transfer of rights, and she relied on it. That reliance is where your career lives, and where it can end.

Every question Priya's lawyer will ask is a legal one:

| Question | Where it's covered |
|---|---|
| Was the statement a *misrepresentation*? | Contract and tort law (Lessons 6–8) |
| Did you have a duty to verify it? | Agency law (Lesson 9) |
| Did you break your regulator's rules? | RESA (Lessons 4–5) |
| Is she too late to sue? | **Limitation periods (this lesson)** |
| Which court or tribunal does she go to? | **The court system (this lesson)** |
| If she wins and you don't pay, how does she collect? | **Enforcing a judgment (this lesson)** |

This lesson is the map of the room everything else happens in. You're not learning to be a lawyer. You're learning to recognize when you're standing on a legal landmine.

## Vocabulary first

Read these once. They come up constantly.

| Word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| **Law** | The *enforceable* set of rules that governs a society. "Enforceable" is the key word: someone can make you comply. |
| **Statute** | A written law passed by a government: Parliament, a provincial legislature, or a city council. Also called *legislation*. RESA is a statute. |
| **Bylaw** | A law passed by a *municipality*. Zoning rules are bylaws. |
| **Regulations / Rules** | Detailed rules made *under* a statute by a body the statute authorizes. The statute is the frame; regulations are the fine print. |
| **Case / decision / judgment** | A judge's written ruling in a specific dispute. |
| **Precedent** | An earlier decision that later courts are expected to follow. |
| **Contract** | An agreement between two or more people that creates a legal obligation to do, or not do, something. |
| **Tort** | A private wrong that injures someone, *other than* breaking a contract, for which a court can award money. Negligence is the big one. |
| **Damages** | Money a court orders one party to pay another as compensation. |
| **Remedy** | Whatever the court gives you to fix the wrong. Usually money; sometimes an order to *do* something. |
| **Liable** | Legally responsible. "Found liable" means you have to pay or comply. |
| **Plaintiff / Defendant** | The person who sues / the person being sued. |
| **Jurisdiction** | The *power* a court, tribunal or government has: over what subjects, what geography, what dollar amounts. No jurisdiction, no power to deal with your problem. |
| **Licensee** | You, once licensed. The official BC term for a real estate agent. |

> 💡 **Contract vs tort: the most useful distinction in this course.**
> A **contract** wrong: you broke a *promise you made*.
> A **tort**: you injured someone without ever promising them anything. You owed them a duty just by being in the situation.
> In Priya's case both could apply. That's normal.

## Where laws come from

Think of BC law as water coming from **two taps**. Everything you'll deal with came out of one of them.

### Tap 1: Common law, made by judges one case at a time

Nobody sat down and wrote it. It **accumulated**, decision by decision, over about a thousand years.

After the **Norman Conquest of England in 1066**, English judges began settling disputes according to local custom. Over the following three centuries their written decisions hardened into a body of rules, "common" because it became common to the whole country. England brought the system to what became BC during colonization, and it is still developing today.

**The engine: *stare decisis*** — Latin for "let the former decision stand."

> **Analogy.** Picture a workplace with no employee handbook. Whenever a dispute comes up, the manager decides it and writes the decision down. Over the years, that pile of decisions *becomes* the handbook. New disputes are settled by finding the closest earlier decision and following it.

**Why bother?** Predictability and certainty. If every case were decided from scratch, nobody could plan anything, and you could never tell a client what a contract means.

**The catch, and it's exam material.** Two cases are almost never identical, so a judge:

1. reads the earlier decision,
2. works out which facts were **crucial** to its outcome,
3. compares those crucial facts with the case in front of them, and
4. if a crucial fact is different, **"distinguishes"** the earlier case — declares that it doesn't bind them here.

> ⚠️ **"Distinguish" is a technical verb.** It doesn't mean "point out a difference". It means "treat this precedent as inapplicable because a material fact differs".

**Which courts bind which?**

```
Supreme Court of Canada        ← binds every court in Canada; most weight
        ↑
Provincial Courts of Appeal    ← bind lower courts IN THAT PROVINCE,
                                  merely persuasive in other provinces
        ↑
Provincial trial courts        ← may govern later decisions on the same point
```

If no Canadian court has decided the point, a judge may look at **English or other Commonwealth** decisions. These are persuasive only, and **not binding until a Canadian court adopts them.**

### Tap 1½: Equity, the fairness patch

Equity is a branch of Tap 1, and it exists because Tap 1 broke.

By the time of the Tudors and Stuarts, *stare decisis* worked too well. The rules became **rigid**, and judges followed precedent even when the result was plainly **unfair**. So people went over the judges' heads and petitioned the **King**, seen as the source of all legal rights. The King began deciding cases on **fairness**. The job passed to his **chancellor**, and a whole parallel system grew up: the **courts of chancery**, or **courts of equity**.

The key framing: equity was meant to **correct** the common law and soften its harshness, not to overrule it. It was **discretionary**, fitted to individual circumstances.

**Why you care: equity created remedies that money can't replace.**

| Remedy | What it does | Origin |
|---|---|---|
| **Damages** | Payment of money | Common law |
| **Specific performance** | Orders the party in default to **actually carry out the contract** | ⭐ Equity |
| **Injunction** | Orders someone to **stop** doing something | ⭐ Equity |
| **Mandatory injunction** | Orders someone to **do** a particular thing | ⭐ Equity |

> **The course's own real estate example.** George agrees to sell his house to Kevin. On completion day George refuses to sign the transfer; he wants more money. Kevin sues.
>
> At **common law**, Kevin gets **damages**: enough money to put him where he'd have been if the deal had closed.
>
> But Kevin doesn't want money. **He wants the house.** Equity can order **specific performance**, forcing George to transfer it.

This matters especially in real estate because land is treated as **unique**. Another house isn't a substitute, so specific performance is far more common in property disputes than in ordinary commercial ones.

> ⚠️ **Equity is discretionary.** Kevin could be **refused** specific performance even if he's right, for example **if he waited too long** to bring his case. An equitable remedy is a privilege, not an entitlement.

### The fusion

For centuries the two court systems ran separately. Then:

- In the **1870s**, the English **Judicature Acts** "fused" them into one system.
- Today in Canada, judges in every province **except Quebec** (which uses a French-derived civil code system) can apply **both** common law and equitable principles.

> 🔴 **Memorize: where common law and equity conflict, equity wins.**

**A vocabulary trap.** "Common law" has two meanings:

1. **Narrow:** the original judge-made rules, as distinct from equity.
2. **Broad (more common):** *all* judge-made law, common law and equity together, as opposed to **statute law**.

### Tap 2: Statute law, written by governments

Elected representatives write rules down and pass them. **Canada's Constitution** divides that power between federal and provincial governments.

| Level | Power comes from | Controls | Real estate relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Federal** (Ottawa) | The Constitution | Banking, bankruptcy, currency, postal service, marriage and divorce, **criminal law**, patents, copyright, shipping, fisheries, defence | Money-laundering law is criminal (Lesson 6); the *Interest Act* is federal (Lesson 12) |
| **Provincial** (Victoria) | The Constitution | ⭐ **Property and civil rights**, municipal institutions, administration of justice, education, local matters | ⭐ **Almost all real estate law**: RESA, *Land Title Act*, *Strata Property Act*, *Residential Tenancy Act* |
| **Municipal** (council) | ❗ **Not the Constitution.** Powers are **carved out of provincial power** and handed down | Zoning, building bylaws, streets and traffic, business licensing | Zoning decides what can be built (Lesson 14) |

Two rules to lock down:

- **Residual power**, anything the Constitution didn't specifically assign, belongs to the **federal** government.
- ⚠️ **Municipalities are creatures of the province.** They have no constitutional standing of their own. The province granted their powers and can change them.

**What is statute law for?** Building new rules case by case is very slow, so legislation's usual job is **to change the common law**.

> The course's own example: at common law, **you didn't need a licence to act as a real estate agent.** Anyone could. The requirement was **created by statute, the *Real Estate Services Act*.** Your whole profession, as a regulated occupation, is a statutory override of the common law. That's why Lessons 4 and 5 matter so much.

### How a judge answers a legal question

```
1. Is there a STATUTE that answers it?
       yes → interpret it the way earlier courts have (stare decisis)
       no, or only partly
2. Turn to CASE LAW (common law and equity)
```

Statute first, then cases.

## Public law vs private law

Two questions: *who is in the dispute, and does the state have a stake?*

| | **Private law** (also "civil law") | **Public law** |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Individual vs individual | Individual vs the state, or how government itself works |
| State's direct interest? | No | Yes |
| Examples | **Breach of contract**, **torts**, divorce | **Criminal**, **tax**, **constitutional** law |

**For you, private law is the job**: real property, contracts, agency and torts.

> ⚠️ "Civil law" has a second, unrelated meaning: the code-based legal system used in Quebec and much of Europe. **In this course it almost always means private, non-criminal law.**

## Why you don't actually own your land

This looks like history. It's actually the foundation of Lesson 2.

After 1066, **William the Conqueror** declared himself owner of all England, then handed out land as **deals, not gifts**:

- Barons and knights held land **in exchange for services** to the King: military, agricultural or personal.
- Keep performing and you kept the land. **Stop, and it went back to the Crown.**
- These **tenants-in-chief** passed rights down to middle lords, and finally to the peasants who worked the land, each layer on the same terms.
- Later the services were swapped for a **money payment** called **scutage**.

That was the **feudal system**, and the rights each person held were called **estates**. Nobody *owned* land the way you own a phone; they **held** it for a period, in return for something.

> 🔑 **BC still works this way in theory. The Crown holds the underlying title to all land.**

| Feudal England | British Columbia today |
|---|---|
| The King owns all land | ⭐ The Crown holds underlying title to all land |
| You hold an *estate*, not ownership | You hold a **fee simple estate**, the largest bundle of rights available |
| You render **service** to keep it | You pay **property tax** |
| Stop serving → land reverts | Stop paying → **tax sale** |
| The King can take it back | The Crown can **expropriate** |

So "selling a property" means transferring a **bundle of rights carved out of the Crown's title**, subject to expropriation, tax sale, zoning, building regulation and other people's legal rights.

> 🏢 **Commercial angle.** This stops being philosophy the moment a client asks about a **99-year leasehold**, an **air space parcel** above a transit station, or a **ground lease** under a big-box store. Each is a different-sized slice of the same Crown-derived bundle. Lesson 2 gives you the full menu.

Because property law is **provincial**, it varies across Canada. BC and the other western provinces use the **Torrens** title system (Lesson 3); most eastern provinces use a less comprehensive one.

## The BC court system

If something goes wrong, *where do you go?* BC cases split into **criminal** and **civil**. Your career is about civil.

### Administrative tribunals, which are not courts

A **tribunal** is a specialized decision-maker created by statute to handle disputes in **one area**: strata disputes, residential tenancies, professional discipline. Less formal, faster and cheaper than court, and you often don't need a lawyer.

> **Analogy:** courts are general hospitals. Tribunals are clinics that treat one thing, quickly.

Tribunals are **outside the court system**, but courts can **review** their decisions when:

1. the rules of **natural justice** were breached (roughly: the process was unfair — no chance to be heard, or a biased decision-maker), or
2. someone challenges whether the tribunal had the **power or jurisdiction** to decide.

That's called **judicial review**.

### The Civil Resolution Tribunal (CRT)

Created by the *Civil Resolution Tribunal Act*. A fully online tribunal you'll meet constantly if you work with stratas. Its advantages: **online, faster, cheaper**.

| Dispute type | Monetary limit |
|---|---|
| ⭐ **Strata disputes** (unpaid fees, bylaw breaches) | ⭐ **None** |
| **Small claims** (debt, damages, recovering personal property, etc.) | **$5,000** |
| **Motor vehicle accidents** (injury, vehicle damage, lost business income) | **$50,000** |
| **Housing co-operatives** (records access, meetings, voting, bylaws) | — |

> ⚠️ **Two traps.**
> 1. "No limit" applies to **strata** disputes only. A $200,000 unpaid-levy claim can go to the CRT.
> 2. You *can* file a small claim over $5,000 at the CRT, but **you give up everything above $5,000 permanently**. It can't be claimed anywhere else.

For co-op claims, the co-op must name a **contact person** who is a director, officer or authorized employee.

**The CRT cannot decide:** libel, slander, malicious prosecution, claims **by or against government**, or **constitutional** questions. And only disputes arising **in BC**.

**Four stages:**

```
1. APPLICATION    claimant files; waits for a response
                  (none by the deadline → possible default decision)
2. NEGOTIATION    parties try to settle online themselves
3. FACILITATION   a CRT case manager helps them reach agreement
4. DECISION       a tribunal member decides — binding and enforceable
```

Parties can settle at **any** stage. Disagree with the decision? Apply to the **BC Supreme Court for judicial review within 60 days**.

### Trial courts

"Courts of original jurisdiction" means **where a case starts**. They hear the **evidence**: witnesses, documents, exhibits. The judge decides what to believe and applies the law.

| | **Small Claims Court** | **BC Supreme Court** |
|---|---|---|
| Money | **$5,001 – $35,000** | ⭐ **No limit** |
| Geography | ❗ **Limited:** the cause of action arose in that court's area, **or** the defendant lives or does business there | No territorial limit within BC |
| Party names | **Claimant** and defendant | **Plaintiff** and defendant |
| Rank | — | ⭐ Top trial court in BC |

A **cause of action** is the set of facts that gives you the right to sue. Over $35,000? **Reduce the claim** to fit, or **go to Supreme Court**.

> 🧮 **The money ladder (a near-certain exam question)**
> ```
> $0 – $5,000         → CRT (small claims)
> $5,001 – $35,000    → Small Claims Court
> Over $35,000        → BC Supreme Court
> Strata dispute      → CRT, any amount
> Vehicle accident    → CRT up to $50,000
> ```

### Appeals

The person appealing is the **appellant**.

| | Trial court | Appeal court |
|---|---|---|
| Hears live evidence? | ✅ Yes | ❌ Generally no |
| Looks at | The **facts** | The **legal principles** the trial judge applied |
| Judges | One | ⭐ A panel |

**An appeal is not a do-over.** You have to point to a **legal error**.

**① Small Claims → BC Supreme Court.** Not automatically a new trial. You first argue there were legal or factual errors; if the appeal is allowed, a **new trial may be ordered** and the evidence is heard again.

**② BC Supreme Court → BC Court of Appeal.** A panel decides by majority (with 3 judges, 2–1 for the appellant overturns the decision). ⭐ **Usually 3 judges, but 5 where an important precedent may be overturned.**

**③ BC Court of Appeal → Supreme Court of Canada.** You need **leave** (permission). ⭐ **The SCC can refuse to hear it**, in which case the Court of Appeal's decision stands. Panels have **at least 5** judges, more often **7 or 9**.

> ⚠️ The BC Court of Appeal must hear a properly filed appeal. The Supreme Court of Canada chooses.

### Your own appeal path as a licensee

```
You allegedly break RESA, its regulations or the Rules
        ↓
BC Financial Services Authority (BCFSA) issues a disciplinary order
        ↓ appeal
Financial Services Tribunal
        ↓ judicial review
BC Supreme Court
```

## How a lawsuit unfolds

Canada's system is **adversarial**: each side presents its own case as persuasively as it can, and a neutral judge (sometimes with a jury) finds the facts, applies the law and makes a binding decision.

> *Context:* some European systems are *inquisitorial*, where the judge investigates. Ours isn't. **The judge only sees what the parties put in front of them**, which is why documentation is everything. A fact nobody can prove effectively doesn't exist.

Follow Priya's $180,000 claim through it.

### Stage 1 — Cause of action arises

A civil wrong happens: a breach of contract or a tort.

### Stage 2 — Pleadings

**Pleadings** are the formal documents where each side states its position (BC Supreme Court Civil Rules):

| Document | Filed by | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Notice of civil claim | Plaintiff | Starts the case; sets out the claim |
| Response to civil claim | Defendant | Denies some or all of it |
| Reply | Plaintiff (sometimes) | Answers the response |
| Counterclaim | Defendant | Defendant's own claim against the plaintiff |
| Response to counterclaim | Plaintiff | Contests the counterclaim |

Each must be **served** (formally delivered) on the other side.

> 🎯 **Purpose of pleadings: narrow and define the issues the court actually needs to decide.**

### Stage 3 — Discovery

Forcing the relevant evidence into the open **before** trial:

- **Examination for discovery**: a "mini-trial" with the parties and lawyers but **no judge**. Each side questions the other **under oath**, recorded by a **court reporter**. The transcripts can be used at trial (with exceptions such as hearsay), often in cross-examination.
- **Discovery of documents**: each party hands over **all relevant documents**.

> 🔑 Discovery shows each side how strong or weak its position really is, and **most cases settle after it.** For you, it means **everything you write is discoverable**: emails, texts, notes, listing remarks. There's no "off the record".

### Stage 4 — Trial

Two separate ideas:

- **Burden of proof** — whose job is it to prove the case? **The plaintiff's.**
- **Standard of proof** — how convincingly? ⭐ **On a balance of probabilities**: more likely than not.

> Criminal cases need proof **beyond a reasonable doubt**, a much higher bar. In civil cases, **just over 50% is enough.**

⚠️ If the court finds the defendant's version **equally likely or more likely**, the claim is **dismissed**. A tie goes to the defendant.

Order: the plaintiff's witnesses (each cross-examined by the defence), then the defendant's witnesses (each cross-examined by the plaintiff), then each side sums up.

### Stage 5 — Judgment

The court decides what each party owes the other and may order a specific remedy.

### Court costs are not legal fees

> 🔴 A favourite exam trap.

| | **Legal fees** | **Court costs** |
|---|---|---|
| What | What *your own* lawyer charges you | An amount the court orders the **loser** to pay the **winner** |
| Set by | Your agreement with your lawyer | ⭐ A fixed **tariff** in the court rules, per step |
| Covers | Everything | ⭐ **Only a fairly small share** of real expenses |
| Guaranteed? | — | ❗ **No — it's at the court's discretion** |

The tariff prices each step (filing documents, letters, conferences, discovery, trial), so **the longer a case runs, the bigger the loser's bill**. Costs do two things: **partly reimburse the winner** and **discourage weak claims and defences**.

> **Example.** Alana sues Bob for **$60,000** and wins, with costs of **$3,500**, so Bob owes **$63,500**. If disputed, the cost list goes to the **Registrar**. Had Bob settled earlier he'd likely have paid much less. If the decision is **reversed on appeal**, the costs are **usually reversed too**, and Bob may get costs in both courts.

### Why most cases settle

1. The pre-trial process **narrows the dispute** and shows who's likely to lose.
2. **Costs risk**: pushing a weak case means paying the other side.
3. **Delay**: waiting months or years makes "a smaller amount now" attractive.

## Winning isn't getting paid

> Priya wins $180,000. Nothing happens. Nobody arrives with a cheque.

A judgment is **a piece of paper declaring a debt**. Collecting is a separate fight. The loser who hasn't paid is the **judgment debtor**; the winner is the **judgment creditor**.

### Step 1 — Find the assets

Search the **land title** records (real estate), **ICBC vehicle registrations**, and the **personal property registry** (cars, boats, machinery). If that's not enough, **examine the debtor under oath** about their assets, income, debts and expenses. Depending on the court, the creditor may also get an order for **instalment payments**.

### Step 2 — Use a collection tool

| Tool | How it works | Key detail |
|---|---|---|
| **Execution** | A **writ of execution** directs the **sheriff** to seize and sell enough assets to pay the judgment plus seizure costs | No assets? The writ comes back marked ***nulla bona***, "no goods" |
| **Charge against land** | Register the judgment against the debtor's land at the **land title office** | ⭐ Must be paid before anyone can take the land free of it. ❗ **Renew the registration every 2 years** |
| **Judicial sale** | Court-ordered sale of the land | **Earlier financial charges (e.g. the mortgage) are paid first**; the creditor gets the balance |
| **Garnishing order** | Served on a **third party who owes the debtor money** (employer, bank), ordering them to pay **into court** | ❗ A third party who ignores it and pays the debtor becomes **personally liable** |

> 🔑 A judgment registered on title **blocks a clean sale**. It's exactly the kind of thing that turns up on a title search mid-deal and threatens a completion date. Lesson 3 teaches you to read a title.

**Wage garnishment limits:**

- ⭐ **70% of wages are exempt** from seizure.
- Never less than **$100/month for a single person** or **$200/month for a married person with dependants**.
- ❗ The exemption **doesn't apply** to debts for **lodging**, or where a judge decides it isn't needed for the family's support.

And you **can't be jailed simply for not paying a debt**. It takes something more, such as contempt of court.

## Staying out of court

Court is **risky, expensive and slow**, so BC pushes people toward **alternative dispute resolution**.

| | 🤝 **Mediation** | ⚖️ **Arbitration** |
|---|---|---|
| Third party's role | ⭐ **Helps** the parties reach their own deal | ⭐ **Decides**, like a judge |
| Gives a decision? | ❌ No | ✅ Yes, a **binding award** |
| Ends when | The parties agree | The arbitrator rules |
| Strengths | Cheap, private, informal, high satisfaction | **Flexible, private, fast**, and you can **pick an expert decision-maker** |

> 🔑 **Memory hook: a mediator helps you agree; an arbitrator tells you the answer.**

### Mediation can be forced on you

It usually starts voluntarily, but:

| Route | Who can trigger it | Applies to |
|---|---|---|
| **Notice to Mediate (General) Regulation**, under the ***Law and Equity Act*** | ⭐ **Any one party** can require all the others to mediate | BC Supreme Court cases |
| **Small Claims Rule 7.3** | Any party files and delivers a Notice to Mediate | ⭐ Claims of **$10,000–$35,000** |

The parties pick a mediator together; if they can't agree in time, any party can ask the **Alternative Dispute Resolution Institute of BC (ADRBC)** to appoint one. Attendance is generally **mandatory**, and a party who refuses can face **court sanctions**.

### Arbitration

Governed by the ***Arbitration Act*** (formerly the *Commercial Arbitration Act*) unless the parties agree otherwise. They can agree how arbitrators are appointed, what procedure applies and who pays. The award is **private, final and usually binding**, with appeals only in limited cases.

> 🏢 **The most commercially relevant point in this lesson.** Commercial leases often include an **arbitration clause** saying which disputes go to arbitration.
>
> *Why, in practice:* commercial leases run 5, 10 or 20 years and contain built-in sources of disagreement: rent reviews on renewal, annual operating-cost reconciliations, arguments over what counts as a capital repair. Landlords and tenants don't want each one to become a public, multi-year lawsuit, so they agree in advance on arbitration: private, faster, and decided by someone who understands commercial property.
>
> *For you:* the dispute-resolution clause in a lease is not boilerplate to skim. It tells your client how a future fight will actually go, and roughly what it will cost.

## Limitation periods: the clock that kills claims

The sharpest point in the lesson. Read it twice.

> Once you have the right to sue, you must do it **within a time limit set by legislation**. Miss it and **the right to sue simply ends**.

Not "gets harder". **Gone.** A perfect case with perfect evidence becomes worthless.

> *Why it exists:* evidence decays (memories fade, documents disappear), people deserve to eventually move on, and the system needs finality.

**First, a structural point.** The ***Limitation Act*** is a **default statute**: it doesn't apply where another statute sets its own time limit. Always check.

### Two clocks

| | **Basic limitation period** | **Ultimate limitation period** |
|---|---|---|
| Length | ⭐ **2 years** | ⭐ **15 years** (cut from 30) |
| Starts | ⭐ When the claim is **discovered** | Usually when the **act or omission happened** |
| Covers | Most civil claims; ⭐ the old tort/contract distinction is gone | Everything, as a hard outer limit |

> *Why two?* The 2-year clock is fair to the claimant: it doesn't start until they could reasonably know they were harmed. On its own, though, that could leave a defendant exposed forever. The 15-year clock is the **absolute outer wall**, whether or not the harm was ever discovered.

❗ **Whichever clock runs out first ends the claim.**

### When is a claim "discovered"?

The 2 years starts on the day the claimant **knew, or reasonably ought to have known**, all three of:

1. that there was **injury, loss or damage**;
2. that it was **caused by something the defendant did or failed to do**; and
3. that **going to court would be an appropriate way to fix it**.

"Reasonably ought to have known" means you can't stop the clock by refusing to look.

### Acknowledgement resets everything

> 🔴 **If a potential defendant acknowledges liability, BOTH clocks restart from the date of the acknowledgement.**

That can be a **partial payment** on a claim for a fixed sum, or a **written confirmation of liability**. A claim about to expire can be revived by a defendant trying to be helpful. An email saying *"you're right, we got that wrong"* can restart a 15-year clock.

### Equity has a second, softer clock

An equitable claim must be brought **within the legal limitation period** *and* **within a reasonable time**. Wait too long and you can lose equitable relief entirely (think of Kevin losing specific performance). A litigant in equity needs **"fast feet"**.

## Two alerts that are about you

### 🚨 Alert 1 — Registered mail and court documents

Court and CRT documents can be served by **registered mail**, and the signature is **proof of receipt**.

> ⭐ **Nobody should sign for registered mail except the person it's addressed to.**

Once someone signs, the clock is running against you whether or not the document reaches your desk.

If you receive court documents:

1. **Tell your managing broker immediately**, so a response can be prepared **and an insurance claim filed**.
2. Know your **brokerage's policy** on receiving court documents.

If you don't: missing the court's deadline can mean a **default judgment** against you (you lose without a hearing), and your brokerage's policy may make you appeal **at your own expense**.

### 🚨 Alert 2 — Don't accidentally admit liability, and report early

**(a) Talk to a lawyer early** so you don't accidentally acknowledge liability and restart the clocks.

This is hard, so name it now. When a client is upset, a decent person's instinct is to say *"I'm so sorry, that's on me, I'll make it right."* Legally, that sentence can revive a dead claim. You're not being told to be dishonest. **Acknowledge the person; send the liability question to your broker and the insurer.**

**(b) The E&O reporting duty has a very low trigger.**

> *Context:* **Errors & Omissions (E&O) insurance** covers you when a professional mistake causes someone a financial loss. In BC it's run by the **Real Estate Errors and Omissions Insurance Corporation** (more in Lesson 4).

As a **condition of coverage**, you must give **written notice as soon as practicable** of **any error or circumstance that could reasonably lead to a claim, however unmeritorious you think it is**.

🔴 The duty is triggered by the **possibility** of a claim, not your opinion of it. The common failure: an agent decides a complaint is nonsense and doesn't report it, then finds that **not reporting**, rather than the original mistake, is what cost them their coverage.

> **Habit: when in doubt, report it.** Over-reporting costs you nothing. Under-reporting can ruin you.

## Trap list

1. **Distinguishing** a case = a **crucial fact differs**, so the precedent doesn't bind.
2. Common law vs equity conflict → **equity wins**.
3. **Municipal power comes from the province**, not the Constitution.
4. **Residual power is federal.**
5. **Court costs ≠ legal fees**: tariff-based, partial, discretionary.
6. **CRT strata disputes: no limit.** CRT small claims: **$5,000**, and **the excess is lost forever**.
7. Small Claims: **$5,001–$35,000 plus a territorial limit**. Supreme Court: neither.
8. **BC Court of Appeal must hear; the SCC can refuse**, and then the appeal court's decision stands.
9. Court of Appeal: **usually 3, five if an important precedent may be overturned**. SCC: at least 5, usually 7 or 9.
10. **Balance of probabilities**, and **a tie means dismissal**.
11. **2 years from discovery; 15 years ultimate; acknowledgement resets both.**
12. **E&O notice is required however unmeritorious the claim seems.**

## Drill: apply it

Write your answer before opening each model answer. Committing in writing is what makes it stick.

**Q1.** A BC Supreme Court judge has two earlier decisions: an **Ontario Court of Appeal** decision exactly on point, and a **BC Supreme Court** decision that's close but differs on one crucial fact. What does she do with each?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

The **Ontario Court of Appeal** decision binds lower courts in Ontario only. In BC it's **persuasive, not binding**: she can follow it, but doesn't have to.

The **BC Supreme Court** decision differs on a **crucial** fact, so she can **distinguish** it: declare it inapplicable to this case.

</details>

**Q2.** Your buyer sues a seller who refused to complete. She doesn't want money; she wants the building. (a) What remedy is she seeking? (b) Which historical system created it? (c) What could cost her that remedy even if she's completely right?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

(a) **Specific performance**: an order that the seller actually transfer the property.
(b) **Equity**, from the courts of chancery.
(c) **Delay.** Equitable remedies are discretionary; if she waits too long, the court can refuse it and leave her with damages only. She needs "fast feet".

</details>

**Q3.** A strata corporation is chasing an owner for **$61,000** in unpaid special levies. Which forum, and why doesn't the amount matter?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

The **Civil Resolution Tribunal**. Strata disputes, including unpaid levies, are within its jurisdiction with **no monetary limit**. The $5,000 cap applies only to the CRT's small claims category.

</details>

**Q4.** A commercial tenant wants **$28,000** from the landlord over failed HVAC repairs. The lease has an arbitration clause. What happens, and why is the clause there?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Without the clause, $28,000 would fall in **Small Claims Court** ($5,001–$35,000); it's far over the CRT's $5,000 limit. With an arbitration clause covering this kind of dispute, it goes to **arbitration** under the *Arbitration Act* (unless the parties agreed on different rules). An arbitrator, possibly chosen for commercial property expertise, hears it privately and issues a **binding, final** award with only limited appeal rights.

The clause exists because long commercial leases generate recurring disputes, and both sides prefer a **private, faster, expert** process to public litigation.

</details>

**Q5.** November 2023: a licensee gives a buyer a wrong lot dimension. March 2026: the buyer discovers the error and couldn't reasonably have known sooner. June 2026: the brokerage emails, *"We acknowledge the measurement was incorrect and are reviewing it."* (a) Was the claim alive in March 2026? (b) What did the June email do? (c) Until when is the claim alive now?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

(a) **Yes.** The 2-year clock started at **discovery** (March 2026), not at the error, and the 15-year clock (from November 2023) was nowhere near expiring.

(b) A **written confirmation of liability** like this would likely count as an **acknowledgement**, which **restarts both clocks** from June 2026.

(c) Basic period to about **June 2028**; ultimate period to about **June 2041**. The email bought the buyer extra time. This is exactly why Alert 2 says to route the response through your broker and a lawyer.

</details>

**Q6.** In one sentence: why does a Norman king handing out land in 1066 still affect what you sell in Kelowna in 2026?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Because BC still runs on the feudal estates system: the **Crown holds underlying title to all land**, so what you sell is a **fee simple estate**, a bundle of rights held from the Crown, with property tax in place of feudal service, and subject to expropriation and tax sale.

</details>

**Q7 (applied).** Back to Priya and the roof. Name three things you should do when she calls you angry, and one thing you must be careful **not** to say.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Do:**
1. **Tell your managing broker immediately.**
2. **Notify the E&O insurer in writing as soon as practicable**, even if you think the claim has no merit.
3. **Preserve everything**: emails, texts, notes, the listing, what the seller told you and when. It's all discoverable and it's your defence.
4. If documents arrive by **registered mail**, only the named person signs, and they go straight to your broker.

**Don't say:** anything that admits liability, such as *"That's my fault, I'll cover it."* It can count as an acknowledgement and restart the limitation clocks. Be human (*"I understand how serious this is and I'm getting it to the right people right now"*) without taking legal responsibility on the spot.

</details>

## Mindset: the law is the product

Most people entering the business treat law as **hoops**: boxes to tick before the real job of selling.

Flip it. **The law is the product.**

Nobody pays you a commission to unlock a door or post photos. They pay you because a real estate transaction is a legally binding transfer of a bundle of Crown-derived rights, often worth more than they'll earn in a decade, under a hard deadline, with risks they can't see. **Your value is carrying that risk for them.**

Look at what this lesson keeps showing:

- **Costs discourage weak cases**: whoever understood their position didn't have to find out in court.
- **Most cases settle after discovery**: discovery reveals who documented their work.
- **Limitation periods end rights completely**: whoever acted in time kept a right the other person lost.
- **Politeness can restart the clock against you**: unexamined instinct becomes liability.

Each time, **the person who understood the structure beat the person who was improvising.**

**And for commercial specifically.** Residential deals mostly use standard forms, so the law comes pre-packaged. Commercial deals run on **negotiated documents**: custom leases, assignment clauses, environmental representations, arbitration provisions. A lawyer drafts the final paper, but **you're in the room when the terms are agreed**, and the lawyer can only paper what was negotiated.

You don't need to out-lawyer the lawyers. You need to be the one who *notices*: the agent who reads a lease and says *"every rent review here goes to private arbitration and you choose the decision-maker, so plan for months, not years."*

> **This week's habit.** Every time you meet a legal rule, finish the sentence:
>
> ***"This rule exists because someone once…"***
>
> If you can name the failure, you own the rule. If you can't, you've only memorized it, and memorized rules vanish under exam pressure and client pressure alike.
