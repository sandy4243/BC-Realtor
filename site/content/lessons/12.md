## Why a salesperson needs mortgage math

> **Scenario.** Your seller client has two offers on a $900,000 house.
>
> - **Offer A:** $860,000, all cash (the buyer has bank financing).
> - **Offer B:** $900,000, with $300,000 down and the seller "carrying" a $600,000 mortgage at **3%** when banks are charging **6%**.
>
> Offer B *looks* $40,000 better. But the seller would be lending $600,000 at half the market rate for years. Run the numbers (Lesson 13 shows how) and Offer B can be worth **less** in real terms than Offer A.

Almost every real estate deal runs on borrowed money. If you can't explain how interest, payments and balances work, you can't explain what an offer is really worth, why a buyer doesn't qualify, or why one loan is cheaper than another. This lesson covers **why** real estate uses debt, **who** lends, the **types** of mortgages and repayment plans, the federal **Interest Act** rules on quoting rates, and the **HP 10bII+** calculator skills the exam tests: future and present value, payments, **equivalent rates** and **outstanding balances**.

> 💡 **Don't panic about the math.** The calculator does the heavy lifting. Your job is to (1) put the right numbers in the right keys, (2) make the **frequencies match**, and (3) get the **signs** right. Nearly every wrong answer on the exam comes from one of those three.

## Vocabulary first

| Word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| **Mortgage** | A loan secured by an interest in land. The **mortgagor** borrows; the **mortgagee** lends. |
| **Principal** | The amount borrowed (or still owing). |
| **Interest** | The "rent" paid for using someone else's money. |
| **Face value** | What the borrower promises to repay at the contract rate. Can be more than the cash actually received. |
| **Book value** | The balance owing at a given moment. On day one it equals face value. |
| **Term** | How long the **contract** lasts (often 1 to 5 years). |
| **Amortization period** | How long it would take to pay the loan off completely with the regular payments (often 25 to 30 years). |
| **Fully / partially amortized** | Paid off by the end of the term / a balance is still owing when the term ends. |
| **Outstanding balance (OSB)** | Principal still owing at a point in time. |
| **Balloon payment** | Any principal paid on top of the regular payments, including the lump sum due at the end of the term. |
| **Nominal rate (jₘ)** | The quoted yearly rate, compounded *m* times a year (e.g. j₂ = 5% means 5% a year, compounded semi-annually). |
| **Periodic rate (i)** | The rate per compounding period (jₘ ÷ m). |
| **Effective annual rate (j₁)** | The true yearly rate once compounding is counted. |
| **Equivalent rates** | Different-looking rates that produce the same amount of interest (same j₁). |
| **Annuity** | A series of equal payments at equal intervals. |
| **Conventional mortgage** | Uninsured; max **80%** of lending value for federally regulated lenders. |
| **Insured (high-ratio) mortgage** | Over 80% LTV; default insurance from CMHC, Sagen or Canada Guaranty. |
| **Junior mortgage** | Any mortgage ranking behind the first (second, third...). |

## Why real estate runs on debt

Real estate costs a big lump sum today, but its benefits (rent, or rent you don't have to pay) arrive slowly over decades. Very few buyers could save the full price first, and sellers don't want to wait while they do. So debt fills the gap: credit is really **future savings brought forward**, and interest is the price of having the property now instead of later.

Even investors who **have** the cash often borrow anyway:

- **Diversify**: spread equity over several properties instead of one.
- **Positive leverage**: borrow at 5%, invest in something earning 8%, keep the difference.
- **Inflation hedge**: property values and rents may rise with inflation while the debt is fixed, so it's repaid in "cheaper" dollars.
- **Free up equity**: a business may prefer to put its cash into inventory or equipment, not bricks.

Both the owner and the lender are **investors** in the property, but they want different things:

| Owner-investor wants | Lender-investor wants |
|---|---|
| **Use** of the property | A **regular, predictable return** (interest) |
| **Income** (rent, or rent saved) | The **return of capital** (principal) on schedule |
| **Growth** (appreciation) | **Security**: the right to take the property if things go wrong |

That last item is what makes a loan a **mortgage** loan: the lender can take over the borrower's interest (subject to earlier claims) on default.

> 🏢 **Commercial angle: leverage cuts both ways.** Commercial investors live on leverage. If a property earns more than the cost of debt, borrowing magnifies the return on equity. If income falls or rates rise, the same leverage magnifies losses, and the lender is paid first. Lenders know this, which is why commercial loans are sized by the property's **income** (debt coverage ratio, Lesson 13), not just its value.

## The capital market and interest rates

The **capital market** is where **savers** (income greater than spending) supply money to **borrowers** (spending greater than income). The **mortgage market** is just one corner of it, competing for the same savings as bonds, stocks, GICs and everything else.

**Interest rates balance supply and demand:**

- More demand for loans → competition for funds → **rates rise** → more people are tempted to save → the rise slows.
- Less demand → lenders compete → **rates fall** → saving is less attractive.

(Real life adds the Bank of Canada's policy rate, government policy and expectations, but supply and demand is the core idea.)

**Why mortgages pay more than government bonds:**

| Government bond | Mortgage |
|---|---|
| Very safe promise | Borrower may default |
| Identical units, easy to sell (**homogeneous**, liquid) | Each one is unique, hard to sell (**illiquid**) |
| Little management | Appraisals, legal work, collecting payments |

So a well-secured first mortgage pays more than a bond of similar term, and second or third mortgages pay more still. **Longer terms** usually carry higher rates too.

From the lender's side, a mortgage rate has **three parts**:

1. a **base return** on capital (set by the market)
2. a **risk premium**
3. a charge for the **cost of managing** the loan

Lenders price for the borrower's credit and equity, the property type (vacant land, owner-occupied home, rental, commercial, industrial, hotel) and the administrative work. But individual lenders usually keep the **market rate** and adjust **non-rate terms** (term, amortization, loan-to-value) to handle extra risk.

## The mortgage market

- **Primary market**: new loans are made (bank, credit union, trust company, insurer, broker, private lender or the seller).
- **Secondary market**: existing mortgages are bought and sold as investments. The buyer acquires the right to the borrower's payments.

Rates in the mortgage market tend to be **sticky**: they lag bond yields, partly because of **advance commitments** (a rate promised 30 to 60 days before the money is advanced).

### Why mortgages were hard for small investors

| Feature | The problem |
|---|---|
| **Uniqueness** | Every loan has its own borrower, property and contract. |
| **Illiquidity** | Hard to sell quickly. |
| **Reinvestment problem** | A small lender gets small monthly payments that are too small to make a new loan, so they sit in a low-interest account. Big lenders can recycle payments into new loans. |
| **Administration** | Appraisal, legal, surveys, collections. Big lenders have **economies of scale**. |
| **Capital cost** | You need a lot of money for even one loan, and putting it all in one loan concentrates risk. |

### Government's fixes

- **NHA Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS)**, created by **CMHC** in **1986**: pools of **CMHC-insured** residential mortgages turned into small, tradable units. CMHC guarantees **timely** payment of principal and interest, paid **monthly**. Issuers must be **approved by CMHC**. RRSP/RRIF eligible.
- **Canada Mortgage Bonds (CMBs)**, launched **2001**: the **Canada Housing Trust** (created and managed by CMHC) sells bonds and uses the money to buy NHA MBS. Bondholders get fixed interest **every six months**, guaranteed by the federal government through CMHC. The **10-year CMB** (2008) helped make longer-term mortgages possible.
- **Mortgage Investment Corporations (MICs)**: investors buy **shares**; the pool lends on Canadian real estate. Under the *Income Tax Act* a MIC can pass **100%** of net income to investors. Professionally managed, varied risk; **not insured**; typical terms **1 to 3 years**, often higher rates plus lender, broker, legal and appraisal fees.

## A short history of Canadian mortgages

| Period | What changed |
|---|---|
| 1900s–1920s | **Interest-only** loans; principal due at the end. |
| 1930s Depression | Borrowers couldn't repay principal → shift to **long-term, fully amortized, constant payment** loans. |
| Post-WWII | Federal **mortgage default insurance** under the *National Housing Act* (indirect federal housing policy). |
| Late 1960s–70s | Inflation and rising rates → lenders faced **interest rate risk** → the **partially amortized** mortgage (long amortization, short term). Borrowers were partly protected by **s.10 of the *Interest Act*** (prepay after 5 years). |
| 1980s | Rates peaked above **21%** (1981); renewals caused payment shocks and defaults. **Interest rate insurance** (1984), **NHA MBS** (1986). **1987**: the "four pillars" (banks, securities, trusts, insurance) separation ended. |
| 1990s–2000s | *Bank Act* (1992) lets banks buy trust companies; internet banks; 95% LTV and up to 40-year amortizations; **mortgage brokers** become mainstream (lenders pay the fee). |
| 2010s | Very low rates; Ottawa tightens insured lending; **stress test** (2016 insured, **2018** uninsured at federally regulated lenders). |
| 2020s | Pandemic; sharp rate hikes 2022–23 (prime peaked at **7.2%** in July 2023), then cuts from mid-2024. Qualifying rate since **June 2021**: greater of **5.25%** or **contract rate + 2%**. |

### Federal rule changes you should recognize

| Date | Change |
|---|---|
| 2011 | Insured max amortization **30 years**; refinance max **85%**; no insurance backing on HELOCs. |
| 2012 | Insured max amortization **25 years**; refinance max **80%**; no insurance on homes over **$1 million**. |
| 2016 | Min down payment **10%** on the part of the price over **$500,000**; stress test for **all** insured mortgages (GDS ≤ **39%**, TDS ≤ **44%**). |
| 2018 | Stress test extended to **uninsured** loans at federally regulated lenders. |
| June 2021 | Qualifying rate = greater of **5.25%** or contract rate **+ 2%**. |
| Nov 2024 | No stress test for an uninsured **straight switch** (same amount and amortization, new lender). |
| Dec 2024 | Insured price cap rises to **$1.5 million**; **30-year** insured amortization for **all first-time buyers** and **new builds**. |
| 2025 | OSFI caps the share of a federally regulated lender's new uninsured loans above **4.5×** income. |

## Classifying mortgage loans

Mortgages are classified four ways:

1. **Type of property**: residential (owner-occupied or rental) or non-residential (retail, office, industrial, recreational, plus **development** loans, which are usually short, under 2 to 3 years).
2. **Default insurance**:
   - **Conventional**: uninsured, up to **80%** of lending value. Lender relies on the borrower's covenant and the property.
   - **Insured / high-ratio**: over 80% LTV. Insurers: **CMHC** (federal Crown corporation), **Sagen** (formerly Genworth) and **Canada Guaranty**. The **borrower pays** the premium (roughly **0.6% to 4%** of the loan, higher at higher LTV), usually **added to the loan**. The insurance protects the **lender**.
3. **Source of funds** (below).
4. **Priority**: the **first** mortgage is the earliest **registered**, not necessarily the biggest. Everything after it is a **junior** mortgage. Juniors are riskier, so they're smaller and dearer.

### Who lends

| Lender | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Chartered banks** | Largest institutional source since **1979**; *Bank Act* revisions (1954, 1967) let them do mortgages. Strongest in residential. |
| **Credit unions / caisses populaires** | Member-owned co-operatives; reinvest locally. **Not federally regulated**, so the federal stress test doesn't automatically apply. |
| **Trust and loan companies** | Like banks, plus can run estates, trusts and pensions. Classic **"spread lenders"**: match 5-year GICs to 5-year mortgages, aiming for about a **1.5%** spread. Largest source **1967–1978**. Many are now bank-owned. |
| **Monoline lenders** | Mortgage-only companies (e.g. MCAP, First National); no branches, lower overhead; **only through brokers**. |
| **Life insurance companies** | Once the biggest source (long-term savings, scale, no liquidity worries). Shifted toward **commercial** mortgages and **equity participation** for inflation protection. |
| **Private lenders** | Individuals, **vendors** (take-back mortgages or agreements for sale), syndicates, MICs, estates, pension funds (mostly secondary market). Fill gaps institutions won't. Expect **higher rates and fees**. |
| **Government** | Direct lending, especially in "tight money" periods. Different from government-**insured** loans. |

> 🏢 **Commercial angle: know the lender universe.** For income property your clients will meet **life insurance companies** (big, long-term, conservative commercial loans), **banks** and **credit unions** (relationship lending), **CMHC-insured multi-family** loans (higher LTV and longer amortization for apartments), **MICs and private lenders** (bridge, construction and "story" deals at higher rates) and **mezzanine/participation** lenders. A deal that fails at a bank may work with a different lender type. That's why commercial brokers keep a strong mortgage broker in their network.

## Repayment plans

The main financial promise in a mortgage is to repay principal and pay interest. How that happens is the **repayment plan**.

| Plan | How it works | Who carries the risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Interest accruing** | No payments; interest is added to the balance; everything due at the end. | Lender's principal **and** income at risk the whole time. Usually short (≤ 1 year). |
| **Interest only** | Pay just the interest each period; full principal due at the end. | Principal at risk throughout. Common in **development**: low payments during construction, repaid from sale. |
| **Straight line principal reduction** | Equal principal each period plus interest on the balance, so payments **shrink** over time. | Biggest payments early. Rare for real estate; suits depreciating equipment (vehicle fleets). |
| **Constant payment** (blended) | Equal payments; each covers that period's interest plus some principal. Early payments are mostly interest; later ones mostly principal. | The standard Canadian mortgage. |

> 🧮 **Interest accruing vs interest only.** $45,000 at 6% per half-year for 2 years.
>
> - **Accruing**: nothing paid; it grows 6% every six months: $45,000 × 1.06⁴ = **$56,811.46** due at the end.
> - **Interest only**: $2,700 every six months; at the end, $45,000 + the last $2,700 = **$47,700**.

### Fully vs partially amortized

- **Fully amortized**: term = amortization. Regular payments wipe out the debt.
- **Partially amortized**: payments are calculated over a long **amortization** (say 25 years) but the **term** is shorter (say 5 years). At the end of the term the large remaining **outstanding balance** must be repaid, refinanced or renewed. This is normal in Canada.

**Amortization limits today:** insured **25 to 30 years** (30 for first-time buyers and new builds); uninsured **30 to 35 years** with **20%+ down**, depending on the lender.

> 🔑 **Longer amortization = smaller payment, more total interest, with diminishing returns.** $500,000 at j₂ = 5%: about **$2,908/month** over 25 years, **$2,668** over 30. Stretching further barely helps, because the payment can never drop below the interest-only amount (about $2,062 here).

### Making a mortgage disappear faster

- **Bigger payments** or **lump-sum prepayments** (e.g. up to 15% a year, or "double-up" payments), if the contract allows.
- **More frequent payments** (semi-monthly, biweekly, weekly): slightly less interest.
- **Accelerated biweekly**: pay **half the monthly payment every two weeks**. That's 26 half-payments, or **13 monthly payments a year**, so one extra month goes straight to principal.

### Open, closed and convertible

| Type | Prepayment | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Open** | Pay off any amount any time (small admin fee) | Higher rate; short terms (6 months to 1 year) |
| **Closed** | No prepayment without penalty, except as the contract or the *Interest Act* allows | Lower rate |
| **Convertible** | Short closed term that can be converted to a longer term at current rates | Useful when rates are expected to rise |

### Fixed vs variable

- **Fixed rate**: payment can't change during the term; terms up to **10 years**. Fixed rates track **bond yields** of similar maturity.
- **Variable rate (VRM)**: floats with **prime** (e.g. "prime + 0.5%"). Usually **cheaper** than fixed because the borrower carries the rate risk (2023 was an unusual exception). When rates change, the **payment**, the **amortization**, or both adjust. May have a **cap** and a **convert-to-fixed** option.

### Other plans to recognize

| Plan | One-line description |
|---|---|
| **Vendor take-back (VTB)** | The seller lends part of the price. Detail in Lesson 13. |
| **Graduated payment (GPM)** | Low payments early, rising later; helps affordability. |
| **Reverse annuity (RAM)** | The **lender pays the borrower** (usually a retiree); repaid on sale, death or refinance. |
| **Combination** | Fixed for a first period (2 to 10 years), then variable. |
| **Hybrid** | Split between fixed and variable at the same time (e.g. 50/50). |
| **Home equity loan / HELOC** | Borrow against equity, typically up to **65%** of value (lower of price or appraisal) minus existing mortgages; also called a **collateral mortgage**. HELOCs are **no longer government-insured**. |
| **Construction (interim) financing** | Advanced in **stages** as work is completed; usually **interest only**; first mortgage. |
| **Line of credit (developer)** | Unsecured by the project (to avoid conflicting with other financing); backed by other security and **personal guarantees**; payable **on demand**. |
| **Improvement mortgage** | CMHC/Sagen: buy and renovate with one insured loan, **5–10%** down, up to **4 units** (one owner-occupied); reno money held in trust until work is done and inspected. |
| **Participation mortgage** | Lender also shares in **income or appreciation**; used as **mezzanine** financing in development. |
| **Shared appreciation (SAM)** | Lower rate in exchange for a share of the **gain** in value; works where there's no income (homes). |
| **Green mortgage** | Discounts or rebates for energy-efficient upgrades. |

> 🏢 **Commercial angle: interest-only and staged advances are normal.** Development and construction loans are typically **interest-only**, **advanced in draws** as stages are certified, and repaid from sales or a long-term "take-out" loan. A **mezzanine** or **participation** lender may fill the gap between the senior loan and the developer's equity in exchange for a higher rate or a slice of profits.

## The Interest Act: how rates must be quoted

Interest is charged at the end of each **compounding period**. The same "1.5% per period" costs more if it's charged monthly than semi-annually, so you always need to know **both** the rate **and** how often it compounds.

Canada's federal ***Interest Act*** (s.6) requires a **blended-payment mortgage** to state the rate calculated **yearly or half-yearly, not in advance** (annually or semi-annually). That's why Canadian mortgage rates are almost always quoted **compounded semi-annually** (it also makes them look a little lower than annual compounding would).

"**Not in advance**" means interest is charged at the **end** of the period. Assume this unless told otherwise.

**Section 10 prepayment right** (more in Lesson 13): an **individual** borrower whose mortgage runs **more than 5 years** may, **after 5 years** from the mortgage date, pay off everything plus **three months' interest**. Once that's tendered, no more interest accrues. With most terms now 5 years or less, it rarely comes up.

## Nominal and periodic rates

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **jₘ** | Nominal annual rate compounded *m* times a year |
| **m** | Compounding periods per year |
| **i** | Periodic rate = **jₘ ÷ m** |

So **jₘ = i × m** and **i = jₘ ÷ m**. Shorthand for periodic rates: i_d (daily), i_w (weekly), i_mo (monthly), i_q (quarterly), i_sa (semi-annual), i_a (annual).

| Given | Calculation | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| j₂ = 5% | 5% ÷ 2 | i_sa = **2.5%** |
| j₁₂ = 6% | 6% ÷ 12 | i_mo = **0.5%** |
| i_q = 1.25% | 1.25% × 4 | j₄ = **5%** |
| i_mo = 0.375%, j = 4.5% | 4.5 ÷ 0.375 | m = **12** |

**j₁** (compounded once a year) is the **effective annual rate**: the honest, apples-to-apples yearly cost.

|---|---|

| **Decimals** | ■ DISP 6 shows six decimal places. Never round in the middle of a problem. |
| **Begin/End** | Stay in **End mode** (payments at the end of each period). If **BEG** shows on the display, press ■ BEG/END to turn it off. |
| **Comma vs period** | If decimals show as commas, press ■ ,/. |

### The financial keys

| Key | Holds |
|---|---|
| **N** | Number of periods (same frequency as P/YR) |
| **I/YR** | **Nominal** annual rate, as a percent (5, not 0.05) |
| **■ P/YR** | Periods per year (compounding/payment frequency) |
| **PV** | Present value (loan amount) |
| **PMT** | Payment per period |
| **FV** | Future value (balance or lump sum at the end) |
| **■ NOM%** / **■ EFF%** | Nominal rate / effective annual rate (for conversions) |

> 🔑 **The sign rule.** At least one cash flow must be **positive** and one **negative**. From the **borrower's** view: money received is **+** (PV), money paid out is **−** (PMT, FV). From a **lender's or investor's** view, flip them. Use the **+/−** key, not the minus key.

> 🔑 **The frequency rule.** **N, I/YR (with P/YR) and PMT must all use the same frequency.**
>
> - If there's a **payment**, its frequency drives everything (monthly payments → P/YR 12, N in months, a j₁₂ rate).
> - If there's **no payment**, the **compounding** frequency of the given rate drives it.

> 🧭 **The T-chart habit.** Before touching the calculator, jot the six keys down one side and fill in what the question gives you. Exactly **one** should be blank: that's what you solve for.

## Future and present value of a lump sum

The core formula is **FV = PV × (1 + i)ⁿ**. The calculator does this for you, as long as:

1. PV is at the **start** of the first period,
2. FV is at the **end** of the last period,
3. the rate matches the period, and
4. **PMT = 0** (no payments in between).

> 🧮 **Interest accruing, by hand.** $100,000 at 8% compounded annually for 3 years:
>
> - Year 1: $100,000 × 1.08 = $108,000
> - Year 2: $108,000 × 1.08 = $116,640
> - Year 3: $116,640 × 1.08 = **$125,971.20**
>
> Same as $100,000 × 1.08³. Notice interest is earned on interest: that's compounding.

> 🧮 **Future value: land banking.** A developer buys land for **$250,000** and expects it to rise **0.75% a month** for **4 years**.
>
> - 0.75% per month = **j₁₂ = 9%**; 4 years = **48** months.
> - Keys: 12 ■ P/YR · 9 I/YR · 48 N · 250000 +/− PV · 0 PMT · **FV** → **$357,851.33**
>
> (PV is negative because the developer pays it out; FV is positive because they receive it on sale.)

> 🧮 **Present value: what to pay today.** An investment will pay **$1,000,000** in **5 years**. You want **j₁₂ = 8%**.
>
> - Keys: 12 ■ P/YR · 8 I/YR · 60 N · 1000000 FV · 0 PMT · **PV** → **−$671,210.44**
>
> Pay no more than about **$671,210** today. Money in the future is worth less than money now; present value tells you **how much** less.

To change just one input, re-enter only that key. Everything else stays stored. Solving for **I/YR** or **N** works the same way: fill four keys, solve the fifth.

## Annuities: regular payments

An **annuity** is a stream of equal payments at equal intervals. The mortgage payment is the classic example; regular savings deposits are another.

> 🧮 **Sinking fund.** Save **$500/month** at **j₁₂ = 4%** for **10 years** toward a roof replacement.
>
> - Keys: 12 ■ P/YR · 4 I/YR · 120 N · 0 PV · 500 +/− PMT · **FV** → about **$73,625**
>
> A **sinking fund** is a series of deposits set aside for a future purpose (repaying a bond, replacing equipment).

> 🧮 **Interest-only payment.** A **$2,000,000** commercial loan at **j₁₂ = 7%**, interest only, monthly.
>
> - Shortcut: 7% ÷ 12 = 0.583333% per month × $2,000,000 = **$11,666.67**
> - Or on the calculator: PV = 2,000,000, FV = −2,000,000 (nothing repaid), any N, solve PMT.
>
> If the rate were j₄ = 6% with **quarterly** payments, it's simply 6% ÷ 4 = 1.5% × the balance each quarter.

## Equivalent interest rates

**Two rates are equivalent if the same amount borrowed for the same time ends up owing the same amount.** Equivalently: they have the **same effective annual rate (j₁)**.

You need to convert rates because:

1. The ***Interest Act*** wants the rate stated semi-annually or annually.
2. You can only **compare** rates fairly at the same compounding.
3. The **calculator** needs the rate's compounding to match the **payment** frequency.

### The five-step conversion template

| Step | Keys | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *rate* ■ NOM% | Enter the given nominal rate |
| 2 | *m* ■ P/YR | Enter its compounding frequency |
| 3 | ■ EFF% | Solve for the effective annual rate |
| 4 | *new m* ■ P/YR | Enter the frequency you want |
| 5 | ■ NOM% | Solve for the equivalent nominal rate |

> 🧮 **The conversion you'll do most: j₂ → j₁₂.** A mortgage at **j₂ = 5%** with monthly payments.
>
> 5 ■ NOM% · 2 ■ P/YR · ■ EFF% → **5.0625%** · 12 ■ P/YR · ■ NOM% → **j₁₂ = 4.948699%**
>
> Leave it in the calculator. **Never re-key a converted rate**: you'd lose decimals and make typos.

> 🧮 **Going the other way for disclosure.** A lender charges **j₁₂ = 6%**. What must it disclose under the *Interest Act*?
>
> 6 ■ NOM% · 12 ■ P/YR · ■ EFF% → 6.167781% · 2 ■ P/YR · ■ NOM% → **j₂ = 6.075502%**

> 🧮 **Which loan is cheaper?** Lender A: **j₂ = 6%**. Lender B: **j₁₂ = 5.94%**. B *looks* cheaper.
>
> - A: 6 NOM%, P/YR 2 → EFF% = **6.0900%**
> - B: 5.94 NOM%, P/YR 12 → EFF% = **6.1044%**
>
> **Lender A is cheaper.** More frequent compounding makes a lower-looking number cost more. Always compare **effective** rates.

A **periodic** rate must be turned into a nominal rate first: i_mo = 0.5% → j₁₂ = 6% → then convert (j₁ = 6.167781%).

### A licensee case: get the rate wording right

In *Haptom v. Pahl* (BCSC 2013), a loan said "24% per annum" with "interest only, $3,000 per month" on $150,000. Someone had simply divided a year's interest by 12. But monthly payments of $3,000 are worth **more** than 24% compounded annually, because the lender gets money earlier and can reinvest it. The court sided with the **borrowers**, treated the rate as 24% compounded **annually**, recalculated the monthly payment (about $2,713) and credited them the overpayment.

> 🎯 **Lesson for you:** if you draft or explain financing terms, the **rate, its compounding and the payment** must agree. Ambiguity gets read against the lender, and can drag you into litigation.

## Constant payment mortgage analysis

Every constant payment mortgage has four financial pieces: **loan amount (PV)**, **nominal rate** (converted to match payments), **amortization (N)** and **payment (PMT)**. FV is **0** when fully amortized. Know any three (plus FV) and the calculator gives you the fourth.

Conditions: PV at the start; equal payments at regular intervals at the **end** of each period; rate converted to the **payment** frequency.

### 1. Payment

> 🧮 **$500,000** loan, **j₂ = 5%**, **25-year** amortization, monthly payments.
>
> 1. Convert: j₁₂ = **4.948699%** (template above; it's now in I/YR with P/YR = 12).
> 2. 300 N · 500000 PV · 0 FV · **PMT** → **−$2,908.02** (round to the cent unless told otherwise).
>
> If the contract says "rounded up to the next dollar", the payment is **$2,909**.

### 2. Loan amount (what a payment will support)

> 🧮 A buyer can afford **$3,000/month**. Rate **j₂ = 6%**, **25 years**.
>
> Convert: j₁₂ = **5.926346%**. Then 300 N · 3000 +/− PMT · 0 FV · **PV** → **$468,891.68**.
>
> Rates matter hugely: the higher the rate, the smaller the loan the same payment supports, because more of each payment goes to interest.

### 3. Interest rate

Enter PV, PMT, N and FV, solve **I/YR** (this gives j₁₂ if P/YR is 12), then convert to j₂ for the *Interest Act* statement.

### 4. Amortization period

> 🧮 **$400,000** at **j₂ = 5%**, payments of **$2,500/month**. How long to pay off?
>
> With j₁₂ = 4.948699% stored: 400000 PV · 2500 +/− PMT · 0 FV · **N** → **262.01** months.
>
> That means 262 full payments plus a tiny 263rd. If you see **"no Solution"**, the payment doesn't even cover the interest, so the loan would never be repaid.

### 5. Outstanding balance

Because most Canadian mortgages are **partially amortized**, you'll often need the balance at the **end of the term**.

**Checklist:**

1. Convert the rate if needed.
2. Calculate the payment over the full **amortization**.
3. **Re-enter the rounded payment** (as a negative).
4. Use **INPUT** and **■ AMORT**.

> 🧮 **$500,000**, j₂ = 5%, 25-year amortization, **5-year term**, payments rounded up to **$2,909**.
>
> 2909 +/− PMT · 60 INPUT ■ AMORT, then press **=** three times:
>
> 1. principal in payment 60
> 2. interest in payment 60
> 3. **balance after payment 60: $442,471.31**
>
> After 5 years and almost $175,000 in payments, the borrower has paid off only about **$57,500** of principal. The rest was interest. That's the reality of a long amortization.

### 6. Principal and interest over a range

| You want | Keys |
|---|---|
| One payment (e.g. #36) | 36 INPUT ■ AMORT |
| Payments 1 to 36 (whole term) | 1 INPUT 36 ■ AMORT |
| Year 2 only | 13 INPUT 24 ■ AMORT |

In the example above, year 1 (1 INPUT 12 ■ AMORT) is about **$24,510 interest** and only **$10,398 principal**. Interest can matter for **tax** (deductible on investment property), and borrowers like to know how much principal they're paying down.

### Final payments

If payments are **rounded up**, the borrower overpays slightly each time, so the last payment of a fully amortized loan is **smaller** (and if rounded up by a lot, there may be fewer payments). Rounded **down**, the last payment is a bit **larger**. You don't need to calculate final payments, just understand why they differ.

### Accelerated biweekly payments

> 🧮 Same **$500,000** loan at j₂ = 5%: monthly payment **$2,909**.
>
> - **Accelerated biweekly** = $2,909 ÷ 2 = **$1,455** (rounded up), paid **26** times a year.
> - Convert j₂ = 5% to **j₂₆** (P/YR 26), then solve N → about **558** biweekly payments, or **21.5 years** instead of 25.
> - Roughly **$60,000 less interest** over the life of the loan.
>
> A plain (non-accelerated) biweekly payment, calculated to pay off in exactly 25 years, saves only a little interest. The magic is the **extra month's payment** each year.
>
> Watch for a borrower paid **semi-monthly** (24 paycheques) but paying biweekly (26 payments): twice a year, two payments land in one pay period.

## Two alerts

> 🚨 **ALERT 1: Match the frequencies, every time.** Monthly payments with a j₂ rate means you **must** convert to j₁₂ first. Putting 5 into I/YR with P/YR = 12 when the rate is really j₂ = 5% is the single most common exam error. And keep the calculator in **End** mode.

> 🚨 **ALERT 2: Don't give financial advice you're not qualified to give.** Understand the math so you can explain offers and spot problems, but refer clients to a **mortgage broker or lender** for financing advice, and to a **lawyer** for prepayment and contract questions.

## Trap list: things the exam likes to twist

1. A **mortgage** is security for a loan. Mortgagor = **borrower**; mortgagee = **lender**.
2. Lender's rate = **base return + risk premium + management cost**.
3. Mortgages pay more than bonds: more **risk**, less **liquid**, more **management**.
4. **Primary** market = new loans; **secondary** = existing loans traded.
5. **NHA MBS**: CMHC, **1986**, insured residential pools, **monthly** payments. **CMBs**: **2001**, Canada Housing Trust, payments **every 6 months**.
6. **MICs**: shareholders, **100%** of net income passed through, **not insured**, short terms, higher rates.
7. Banks are the biggest institutional source (since **1979**); trust companies were biggest **1967–78**; life insurers moved to **commercial**.
8. **Monolines** lend only through **brokers**.
9. **Conventional** = uninsured, ≤ **80%** LTV. Insured = over 80%. **Borrower pays** the premium; the **lender** is protected.
10. **First mortgage** = first **registered**, not the biggest.
11. **Interest accruing**: no payments; everything at the end. **Interest only**: principal never declines. **Straight line**: payments **decline**.
12. **Partially amortized**: amortization **longer** than term; balloon at the end of the term.
13. Insured amortization **25–30 yrs** (30 for first-time buyers/new builds); uninsured **30–35 yrs** with 20%+ down.
14. **Accelerated biweekly** = **half the monthly** payment every two weeks = **13** monthly payments a year.
15. **Open** = prepay anytime, higher rate, short term. **Closed** = penalty to prepay.
16. **VRM** usually cheaper than fixed; borrower carries rate risk. Fixed rates follow **bond yields**.
17. **Reverse annuity**: **lender pays borrower**. **SAM**: lender shares the **appreciation**. **Participation**: lender shares **income/appreciation**.
18. **HELOC**: up to **65%** of value minus existing mortgages; **no** government insurance backing.
19. ***Interest Act* s.6**: blended-payment mortgages must state the rate **yearly or half-yearly, not in advance**.
20. **s.10**: individuals, after **5 years**, pay off + **3 months' interest**.
21. **jₘ = i × m**; **i = jₘ ÷ m**. j₁ = effective annual rate.
22. Equivalent rates have the **same j₁**. Compare rates by converting to j₁.
23. A lower-looking j₁₂ can cost **more** than a higher j₂.
24. Calculator: **End** mode; **orange** shift; I/YR as a percent; P/YR sets the frequency.
25. Borrower: PV **+**, PMT and FV **−**. Lender: the reverse.
26. **N, I/YR and PMT** must share one frequency. Payment frequency drives; if no payment, compounding frequency drives.
27. **Never re-key** a converted rate; use it straight from the calculator.
28. Outstanding balance: **re-enter the rounded payment**, then INPUT ■ AMORT.
29. Year 2 interest = payments **13 to 24**.
30. "**no Solution**" for N = payment too small to ever repay.
31. Stress test (since June 2021): greater of **5.25%** or **contract + 2%**.

## Drill

**Q1.** Convert j₂ = 4% into (a) an effective annual rate and (b) an equivalent j₁₂.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

4 ■ NOM% · 2 ■ P/YR · ■ EFF% → **j₁ = 4.04%** (that's 1.02² − 1). Then 12 ■ P/YR · ■ NOM% → **j₁₂ = 3.967068%**. Notice the monthly-compounded rate is *lower*, because compounding more often makes each percent "work harder".

</details>

**Q2.** A seller's existing mortgage is $350,000 at j₂ = 4.5%, with a 25-year amortization and monthly payments. What's the payment?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Convert: 4.5 ■ NOM%, 2 ■ P/YR, ■ EFF%, 12 ■ P/YR, ■ NOM% → j₁₂ ≈ 4.458383%. Then 300 N · 350000 PV · 0 FV · PMT → **−$1,937.16**. (Keep the converted rate in the calculator; re-keying a rounded rate can throw off the cents.) Check yourself: the payment must be more than the first month's interest (350,000 × 4.458383% ÷ 12 ≈ $1,300), and it is.

</details>

**Q3.** Two loans: Bank X at j₂ = 7.1%, Bank Y at j₁₂ = 7.0%. Your buyer says, "Y is obviously cheaper." Are they right?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Compare effective rates:

- X: 7.1 NOM%, P/YR 2 → EFF% ≈ **7.226%**
- Y: 7.0 NOM%, P/YR 12 → EFF% ≈ **7.229%**

They're almost identical; **Y is actually a hair more expensive**. The lesson: you can't compare rates with different compounding by eye. Convert both to j₁.

</details>

**Q4.** A $600,000 investment property loan at j₁₂ = 6% has a 25-year amortization and a 3-year term. The payment is rounded up to the next dollar. Explain the steps to find what's owing at the end of the term.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

1. No conversion needed: the rate is already monthly (12 ■ P/YR, 6 I/YR).
2. Payment over the amortization: 300 N, 600000 PV, 0 FV, solve PMT (about −$3,865.81) → round **up** to **$3,866**.
3. **Re-enter** 3866 +/− PMT.
4. 36 INPUT ■ AMORT, press = three times; the third number is the **balance after payment 36** (about **$565,935**). That balance must be repaid, renewed or refinanced at the end of year 3.

</details>

**Q5.** A client says, "My mortgage is closed, so I can never pay it off early." Is that right?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Not necessarily. Most "closed" mortgages **allow** prepayment **with a penalty** (often the greater of 3 months' interest or an interest rate differential; Lesson 13), and many include **prepayment privileges** (e.g. 10–15% a year). And under ***Interest Act* s.10**, an **individual** with a mortgage running **more than 5 years** can pay it off **after 5 years** with **3 months' interest**. Tell them to check the contract with their lender or a mortgage broker before they sell or refinance.

</details>

**Q6.** Why would a lender agree to interest-only payments on a construction loan, but not usually on a 25-year home loan?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

On a construction loan the developer needs every dollar for building; there's **no income yet**; and the loan is **short** and repaid from **sales or a take-out loan** when the project is done. Advances are staged so the lender's exposure grows only as value is created. On a long-term home loan, interest-only would leave the **entire principal at risk for decades**, which is exactly what went wrong in the 1930s.

</details>

## Mindset: numbers are part of your client service

Clients don't need you to be a mortgage broker. They need you to understand **enough** to spot when an offer, a renewal, a rate quote or a "great deal" doesn't add up. A licensee who can say, "That vendor financing at 3% is costing you about $156,000 in today's dollars," is worth far more than one who just reads out the price.

Practise until the keystrokes are automatic: **convert the rate, set the frequency, check the signs, solve**. The exam rewards speed and accuracy, and so do your future commercial clients.

> 🏢 **For commercial specifically.**
>
> - **Leverage** is the engine of commercial returns. Know how payment size, amortization and rate change an investor's cash flow.
> - Commercial loans are often **shorter-term**, sometimes **interest-only** (construction, bridge), and come from **life insurers, banks, credit unions, CMHC-insured multi-family programs, MICs and private lenders**.
> - Expect to calculate **balances at maturity**: refinancing risk at renewal is one of the biggest risks in a commercial deal.
> - **Equivalent rates** let you compare a lender's j₂ quote, a private lender's j₁₂ quote and a MIC's "effective" quote on the same footing.

> **This week's habit.** Every day, do one calculator problem from scratch: a conversion, a payment, a loan amount, a balance. Write the T-chart first. Time yourself. Aim for under 90 seconds per problem by exam day.
