## Why these documents decide whether you get paid

> **Scenario.** You list a small warehouse. The seller says, "Asking $3.2M. I'll carry a $1M vendor take-back mortgage if needed." You write that on the listing as "terms: cash or VTB".
>
> - A buyer offers exactly **$3.2M**: "Balance by mortgage and/or agreement for sale at 6%."
> - Your seller accepts, then gets a better offer and wants out.
> - The seller's lawyer says there was **never a contract**: nobody can tell whether the balance is a mortgage or an agreement for sale, how long it runs, or how it's paid.
>
> The lawyer is probably right (*Block Bros. v. Occidental Hotel*, below). The seller walks, the buyer loses the deal, and **your brokerage earns no commission**, because commission depends on an **enforceable** contract.

Lesson 7 taught the rules of contract. This lesson applies them to the documents you'll actually write: the **listing contract**, the **contract of purchase and sale** (CPS), **subject clauses**, **deposits**, the **Property Disclosure Statement**, and the law and skills around them (the *Competition Act*, the Home Buyer Rescission Period, assignments, negotiation and risk management).

## Vocabulary first

| Word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| **Listing contract** | The agreement between a **seller** and a **brokerage**: the brokerage tries to find a buyer, the seller pays commission if it succeeds. The buyer isn't a party. |
| **Contract of purchase and sale (CPS)** | The BC standard form contract between buyer and seller. Before 1987 it was called an "interim agreement". |
| **Party to be charged** | The person you're trying to enforce a contract **against**. Their signature is the one that matters. |
| **Estoppel** | A rule that stops someone denying something their own words or conduct showed was true. |
| **Metes and bounds** | Describing land by measurements and landmarks, used when part of a parcel is sold before subdivision. |
| **Term / condition / warranty** | A promise in the contract / an **essential** term / a **minor** term. |
| **Deposit** | Good-faith money paid by the buyer, usually held in trust by the brokerage. **Not** the consideration. |
| **Stakeholder** | Someone holding money for **all** parties, who can only release it by agreement or court order. |
| **Doctrine of merger** | After completion, the contract "merges" into the transfer, so most pre-completion promises can no longer be sued on. |
| **Collateral warranty** | A side promise, outside the main contract, that a court treats as binding. |
| **Error in substantialibus** | A mistake about the fundamental identity or character of what was sold. |
| **Builders lien** | A claim against land by someone unpaid for work or materials supplied to it. |
| **Holdback** | Money kept back from a payment (10% for builders liens) to protect against lien claims. |
| **Condition precedent ("subject")** | Something that must happen before the main obligations kick in, e.g. "subject to financing". |
| **Condition subsequent** | An event that **ends** obligations already in place. |
| **Covenant** | An unconditional promise. Failing to keep it is a breach, but it doesn't let the other side walk away. |
| **Time clause** | Lets a seller with a backup offer force the first buyer to remove subjects within a short time. |
| **HBRP** | **Home Buyer Rescission Period**: 3 business days to cancel a residential purchase for a 0.25% fee. |
| **PDS / PNDS** | **Property Disclosure Statement** (seller's answers about the property) / **Property No-Disclosure Statement** (seller chooses to say nothing extra). |
| **BATNA / resistance point** | Your best alternative if no deal is made / the bottom line at which you walk away. |

## The requirement of writing

At common law, most contracts can be **oral**. Land is the big exception.

**Law and Equity Act s.59**: a contract **respecting land** (or a disposition of land) **isn't enforceable** unless one of these applies:

1. **Writing**, signed by the **party to be charged** (or their agent), showing a contract was made and reasonably identifying the subject matter.
2. **Conduct**: the party denying the contract did (or accepted) an act showing a contract exists. Paying or accepting a **deposit** or part of the price counts.
3. **Reliance**: the person relying on the contract changed their position (usually spent money), so only enforcing it avoids an unfair result.

Key points:

- It doesn't apply to **leases (or agreements to lease) of 3 years or less**.
- Without writing, the contract isn't void or illegal, just **unenforceable**.
- Writing binds only the **signer**. If only the buyer signed, the **seller** can enforce it against the buyer, but not the other way round (unless an exception applies).
- Even when a contract can't be enforced, the court can order **restitution** (e.g. return the deposit) or **compensation** for money spent relying on it.
- A writing can still count even if a term is **missing or wrong**.
- **Rules s.43**: a brokerage needs a **written service agreement** to provide trading services to an **owner**, unless the client waives it.

| Example | Result |
|---|---|
| Buyer writes "I confirm I'll buy Whiteacre for $50,000", signed by the buyer only | Seller can enforce against the buyer; buyer **can't** enforce against the seller |
| Seller replies in writing, signed, asking for the $50,000 by Wednesday | **Both** can now enforce |
| Oral 5-year lease; tenant pays first and last month by cheque with the address written on it; owner cashes it | Likely enforceable (writing on the cheque, plus payment and acceptance) |
| Oral 5-year lease; owner spends $5,000 redecorating for the tenant, who leaves after 3 months | Possibly enforceable (reliance), or at least compensation for the $5,000 |

> 🏢 ***Pepper's Produce v. Medallion Realty* (2013 BCSC).** A buyer of a grocery business had an offer **subject to** the landlord agreeing to a new lease. The landlord said yes **verbally**, and the licensee advised removing the subject. The landlord later refused to sign. The court said the licensee **should have known** a lease over 3 years needs **writing**, and shouldn't have advised removing the subject on a verbal promise. This is everyday commercial leasing risk.

**Cancellation too.** Parties can orally agree to cancel a written contract, but put cancellations **in writing** to avoid a dispute.

**Witnessing signatures.** Not needed for validity, but expected by lenders. A witness must be **19+** and must **actually see** the person sign. Never "witness" a signature that arrived by fax or email. A licensee who did that was disciplined for failing to act with reasonable care and skill.

> 💡 ***T.L.G. v. K.M.G.* (2019 BCSC).** Parents put **$110,000** toward a home in their daughter's name. Years later she said it was a gift. The court applied a **resulting trust** (money given without payment is presumed held for the giver unless a gift is proven) and sided with the parents. Lesson: **document** property arrangements, even within a family.

## Listing contracts

A **listing contract** is between the **seller** and the **brokerage**. BCREA's standard form brings in **designated agency** (clause 7): the brokerage names a specific licensee as the seller's agent (Lesson 9).

**The buyer isn't a party** to the listing, so they can't rely on its terms. Even a full-price, full-terms offer doesn't force the seller to sell. But finding a buyer who meets the listing terms usually means the brokerage has **earned** its commission.

> 🔴 **Strict compliance.** In *Brian Fisher Realty v. Emerald Park Lodge* (1975 BCSC), the listing asked $140,000 with **$30,000 down and vendor financing** for the rest. The licensee brought an **all-cash** $140,000 offer. The seller refused, and the brokerage got **no commission**: the offer didn't match the listing terms exactly (the seller may have wanted the interest income). Vague listing terms like "possession: to be arranged" can also make commission unenforceable.

### Get the facts right at listing time

Many unenforceable deals start with a careless listing. The property must be **accurately described** and the listing signed by **all owners**.

**Search the title.** A title search shows the **registered owners** and **charges** (mortgages, easements, rights of way). Cases:

| Case | What went wrong |
|---|---|
| *De Cotiis v. Hothi* (2018 BCSC) | The licensee's "client" **wasn't the registered owner**. The licensee escaped liability, but only just. Under *LTA* s.23(2) the person on title is presumed the owner; get **written** proof of anyone else's authority to sell, and **written** authority before signing for a client (Rules s.45). |
| *Armitage v. Liptay* (Ont. CA) | Part of an 80-acre development site had been designated a **controlled access highway**. Buyer got the **$50,000 deposit** back. A title search would have revealed it. |
| *Price v. Malais* (1982 BCSC) | Offer promised "**clear title**", but two **easements** were registered. Buyers walked; brokerage breached its duty to the seller. |
| *Chand v. Sabo Bros. Realty* (Alta CA) | Licensee took the seller's word instead of searching title. Liable for an unenforceable contract: "they have only themselves to blame". |

**Measure, or say how you know.** "Approximately" and "more or less" don't always save you. In *Bouskill v. Campea* (Ont. CA), a lot described as **172 feet** deep "more or less" was really **161 feet**, and the contract was **unenforceable**. If boundaries are **visibly marked** (fenced), courts lean toward "what you see is what you get". A representative was suspended **7 days** after buyers were led to believe a grove of trees and a driveway were inside the boundaries, and they weren't.

**Verify with outside sources.** "The seller told me" is **no defence** if you could have checked:

- **Mortgages to be assumed**: confirm balance, rate, payment, term, prepayment penalty and **assumability** with the **lender**. If the contract says "assume approx. $20,000" and only $19,000 is owing, the buyer may walk and your commission is gone.
- **Age, lot size, zoning**: city hall, BC Assessment, plans.

**Environmental awareness.** In *Jung v. Ip* (Ontario), the listing licensee spent **15 minutes** inspecting and described the home as "**move-in condition**". It was in a known **termite** area. The licensee was liable for **20%** of the buyers' damages, and the court said his **employer** also had a duty to verify listing information. Know your area's industrial history and contamination sources, and build a verification step into every listing.

### Privacy

Since **2004**, BC businesses must protect personal information (federally, **PIPEDA**). Collect only what you need, tell people why, get consent, and use it only for that purpose (new purpose = new consent). Breaches can bring penalties up to **$100,000** plus damages. The standard forms have consent wording (listing **clause 11**, CPS **clause 19**).

## The Competition Act

The federal *Competition Act* (enforced by the **Competition Bureau**) targets anti-competitive deals and misleading advertising. Some offences are **criminal** (proof **beyond reasonable doubt**), some **civil** (proof on a **balance of probabilities**), and some are "dual track". Private parties can also sue for losses. **Ignorance is no defence.**

### Criminal provisions

**s.45: conspiracies between competitors.** Agreeing with a competitor to fix prices, allocate customers/territories, or limit supply. Also **wage-fixing** and **no-poaching** agreements between employers. Penalty: up to **14 years** and/or a fine at the court's discretion. The Crown **doesn't** need to prove harm to competition, or even that the deal was carried out, and an **implied** agreement (inferred from circumstances) is enough.

In real estate, s.45 would catch licensees, brokerages or boards agreeing to:

- fix **commission rates** or minimum commissions
- fix **splits** (listing/selling, or brokerage/licensee)
- **allocate** territories or customers
- restrict services (e.g. FSBO support, owner-marketing services)
- ban **advertising** of commission rates, or certain inducements
- control **entry** of new licensees or firms
- set common fees for services

Each brokerage sets its rates and splits **independently**. Discussing the split **on a particular sale** is fine.

> 🚨 **Careful sharing information with competitors.** Swapping pricing forecasts, marketing plans or strategy can let a court **infer** an illegal agreement.

**s.52: false or misleading advertising.** Knowingly or recklessly making a representation to the public that's false or misleading **in a material respect**, by **any means** (print, online, social media, verbal).

- **Indictable**: fine at the court's discretion and/or up to **14 years**. **Summary**: up to **$200,000** and/or **1 year**.
- Courts look at the **literal meaning** and the **general impression** (audience, medium, disclaimers), then ask whether it would affect an ordinary buyer's decision. Nobody needs to have actually been misled.
- **Puffery** ("best view in town") and trivial errors aren't material.
- A picture can mislead even when the words are true (a pricier model shown beside a low price).
- Honest belief plus **due diligence** can be a defence (the Crown must prove knowledge or recklessness).

| Case | Outcome |
|---|---|
| **Checkerboard Realty** | Listing overstated a house by **60.79 sq ft** (1,032 advertised). Brokerage and representative **convicted**: failure to verify. |
| Ontario brokerage, 1981 | Advertised **83 acres** as "industrial"; actually **agricultural** zoning. Convicted, **$5,000** fine. |

### Civil provisions (heard by the Competition Tribunal)

| Section | What it covers | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **s.90.1** | Agreements (usually between competitors) that **substantially lessen competition** | No jail; remedial orders and penalties. Needs real market power, so mainly a risk for brokerages, boards and associations. Can also catch commercial **lease exclusivity clauses** and **restrictive covenants** limiting competing businesses. |
| **s.74.01** | Misleading representations (civil version of s.52) | **No intent needed**, so most cases go here. First-time AMPs up to **$750,000** (individuals) / **$10 million** (corporations); restitution possible. |
| **s.76** | **Price maintenance**: pressuring others to keep prices up, or punishing low pricing | E.g. a board refusing listings because the commission is too low; brokerages pressuring a discount rival. |
| **s.74.05** | Selling **above the advertised price** | Licensees selling for an **owner** are **exempt** (you're supposed to get the best price). Licensees selling for a **developer** are **not** exempt. |

> 🧠 **CREA's three rules:** **Don't collude** (decide independently). **Don't discriminate** against anyone over their pricing. **Don't mislead** in advertising.

## The contract of purchase and sale

BCREA and the Canadian Bar Association (BC Branch) created the standard **CPS**. Always use the **current version**. The standard form:

- makes sure no legal fundamentals are missed
- overrides some **default common law results**

**Risk of loss.** Equity says a buyer becomes the **beneficial owner** once a contract is binding (*Lysaght v. Edwards*), so by default damage after signing would be the **buyer's** problem. The CPS reverses that:

- **Clause 16 (Risk)**: the property stays at the **seller's risk until 12:01 a.m. on the Completion Date**.
- **Clause 8 (Viewed)**: the property must be in **substantially the same condition** at possession as when viewed.
- Because the contract **provides for** destruction, a fire before completion won't **frustrate** the deal (Lesson 7). It's the seller's risk.

**Rules of thumb:**

- Don't encourage anyone to **strike or change pre-printed wording** without **legal advice**.
- **Every party** (or their authorized representative) signs the CPS **and** any addendum.
- Number every page ("Page 5 of 5") so addenda are clearly part of the contract.
- **Type** it, or print very clearly.

### The three P's: parties, property, price

**Parties.** Get full names (and occupations) for the conveyancer. Ask how buyers want to hold title. If nothing is said, BC courts presume **tenancy in common**. **Explain** joint tenancy vs tenancy in common (Lesson 2), but **don't advise** which to choose. In a **court-ordered sale**, changing the buyer needs another court application, so get it right the first time.

> 🚨 **Non-Canadian buyers.** The federal *Prohibition on the Purchase of Residential Property by Non-Canadians Act* (in force **Jan 1, 2023**, banning purchases until **Jan 1, 2027**) bars most non-Canadians from buying **residential property** in census metropolitan areas and census agglomerations.
>
> - Anyone who **helps** a non-Canadian break it (including licensees) faces a fine up to **$10,000**, and BCFSA discipline.
> - "Non-Canadian" includes corporations **controlled** by non-Canadians (**10%+** of equity or votes, or de facto control).
> - Limited exceptions: **international students** (5 years of Canadian tax returns, **244+ days** a year in Canada, one property, **≤ $500,000**) and **work permit holders** (**183+ days** left on the permit, one property).
> - Exemptions include inheritance, separation, gifts, enforcing security, and buying **for development**. Vacant residentially zoned land is **not** "residential property".
>
> **Never advise** whether the ban applies. Discuss it with non-citizen clients and send them for legal advice. Separately, BC's **20% additional property transfer tax** (foreign buyers) has different definitions, so someone could avoid the ban but still owe the tax.

**Property.** Include the **civic address** and **legal description**. A bad description can make the contract **void for uncertainty**.

- Selling **part** of a parcel not yet subdivided: describe it by **metes and bounds**, with landmarks and an attached **sketch**.
- **Chattels vs fixtures**: if in doubt, say so expressly (Lesson 2).
- **Business sales** (hotels, farms, apartment buildings): attach an **inventory schedule** of included personal property, and list excluded items too.

**Price and financing.** Every financing term must be certain. Courts won't enforce "**a contract to make a contract**".

| Case | Wording | Result |
|---|---|---|
| *Block Bros. v. Occidental Hotel* (BCCA) | "Balance by mortgage **and/or** agreement for sale at $4,100/month at 7% P.I." | **No contract**: the two financing methods are completely different. No sale, **no commission**. |
| *Buyers v. Begg* (BCCA) | Agreement for sale "under the **usual terms**" | **Too vague**. There are no "usual" terms. Buyer got the deposit back. |
| *Jackson v. Macaulay Nicolls* (BCCA) | Buyer to "assume the existing **mortgage**", but there wasn't one (only an agreement for sale) | **No contract**; deposit returned. A deposit is **always** recoverable when no binding contract exists. |
| *Arnold Nemetz Engineering v. Tobien* (BCCA) | "Balance by A/S at 8½% P.I. over 10 years" | Too vague: how often are payments? How is interest calculated? **No specific performance, no commission.** Spell out abbreviations. |

> 💡 **Use tested wording.** BCFSA's **Knowledge Base** has clauses reviewed by practitioners and lawyers. Use them rather than inventing your own.

## The Home Buyer Rescission Period (HBRP)

Since **January 1, 2023**, buyers of **residential real property** can cancel within **3 business days** of acceptance by paying the seller **0.25%** of the price (*Property Law Act* **s.42**). It **can't be waived**. The goal: protect buyers pressured into high-risk, often subject-free offers.

**Covers:** detached and semi-detached houses, townhouses, duplex/multi-unit apartments, residential strata lots, manufactured homes affixed to land, and co-op interests with a right to occupy.

**Doesn't cover:** property on **leased land** or leasehold interests, **auction** sales, **court-ordered** sales (e.g. foreclosure), and **REDMA development units** (those get **7 days** under REDMA instead).

**Timing.** A **business day** excludes **Saturdays** and **holidays** (Sunday counts as a holiday, as do statutory holidays). The period starts the **business day after** the acceptance is signed.

| Accepted | Last day to rescind |
|---|---|
| Monday, July 3 | End of **Thursday, July 6** |
| Thursday, July 6 | End of **Tuesday, July 11** (skips Sat and Sun) |

**How.** Written notice served on the seller, containing: the property's address/PID/description, the **buyer's name and signature**, **each seller's name**, and the **date** of rescission. No mandatory form (BCFSA and BCREA offer optional ones). Delivery:

| Method | Deemed delivered |
|---|---|
| **Registered mail** to the seller's address in the contract | When **sent** |
| **Fax** to the number in the contract | When **transmitted** |
| **Email** with a **read receipt requested**, to the address in the contract | When **transmitted** |

**The fee.** 0.25% = **$250 per $100,000**. On $1,000,000, that's **$2,500**.

**Your duties as a licensee:**

1. **Disclosure of Representation in Trading Services** (Lesson 9) explains the HBRP. **Explain** it to clients. To **unrepresented** parties, give **facts only, no advice**, or you risk an implied agency.
2. **Rules s.57.1**: further HBRP disclosure when the **buyer's licensee prepares** an offer and when the **seller's licensee presents** one. Use BCFSA's form, or the **standard CPS** (which already contains it). Either way, **explain** it.
3. If your brokerage holds the deposit and the buyer rescinds: pay the seller the **0.25%** and **promptly** return the rest to the buyer. **No release needed.**
4. Rescission notices are **trading records**: give them to your managing broker; the brokerage keeps them **7 years** and reports them in its annual activity report.

Breaching HBRP duties is deemed **professional misconduct**.

## Terms, conditions and the deposit

A **term** is any promise in the contract. An **essential** term is historically called a **condition** (breach lets the innocent party end the contract). A **minor** term is a **warranty** (damages only). Don't confuse this "condition" with a **condition precedent** (subject).

**Clause 2 (Deposit).** Paid **within 24 hours of acceptance** unless agreed otherwise, held in trust under RESA. If the buyer doesn't pay it, the seller **may terminate**, so the deposit is treated as an **essential** term.

> 🔑 **The deposit is not the consideration.** The **exchange of promises** (seller to transfer title, buyer to pay) is the consideration. A contract can be binding before any deposit is paid. The deposit is **good-faith money** the buyer may forfeit on default.

### The brokerage as stakeholder (RESA s.28)

A brokerage holding a deposit in trust holds it as a **stakeholder** for **all** parties, not as agent for one. It can release it only when:

- the buyer **rescinds under the HBRP** (0.25% to the seller, the rest to the buyer)
- **all parties sign a release**, or
- it's **paid into court** (RESA s.33).

Practical points:

- Prepare a **release** as soon as a deal looks dead. If someone won't sign, that's a red flag for a dispute; get legal advice.
- **Never** tell a buyer they'll "automatically" get the deposit back if the deal fails.
- Stakeholder rules apply even to a cheque **not yet deposited**. If the buyer says "I've stopped payment, give it back", you must still **deposit it** and tell the seller's side immediately.
- **NSF or late deposit**: tell your **managing broker** immediately; they make sure **all parties** are told. With the seller's agreement, the buyer may get a very short time to replace it with certified funds.
- The **only** time you can hand a cheque back without consent: there's no trade (e.g. an offer with a cheque arrives after banking hours and the seller **rejects** it before the bank opens).
- Otherwise, deposit money goes into trust **promptly** (RESA s.27).
- A **buyer's lawyer** holding a deposit is **not** a stakeholder under RESA unless they **undertake** to be one, so it gives the seller little security.
- **Interest**: by default, trust interest goes to the **Real Estate Foundation**. Parties can agree it goes to the buyer or seller (interest-bearing account clause), but that needs a **separate trust account** (RESA s.29(3)) and has costs and redemption risks. Talk to your managing broker.

> 🧮 ***Lee v. Skalbania* (1987 BCSC): can the seller keep the deposit?** Price **$975,000**, deposit **$50,000**, "time is of the essence". The buyer missed completion, even after a one-day extension (which repeated "time is of the essence"). The seller resold for **$31,000 more**, and still **kept the deposit**.
>
> - Rule of thumb: deposits of **5–10%** are reasonable.
> - Courts can relieve against forfeiture (*Law and Equity Act* **s.24**) only if the deposit isn't a reasonable pre-estimate of loss **and** keeping it would be **unconscionable**.
> - Reasonableness is judged **when the contract is made**; later price changes don't matter.
> - Always include "**time is of the essence**" in contracts **and extensions**.

## Warranties and the doctrine of merger

**Types of warranty:**

- **Express**: written in the contract ("The Seller warrants that the roof is in excellent condition").
- **Implied**: by courts (rarely) or by law. A builder impliedly promises good workmanship and fitness for habitation. Under the *Homeowner Protection Act*, an owner who isn't a licensed builder and sells a home they built gives a **10-year** warranty.
- **Collateral**: a separate binding promise alongside the main contract. Hard to prove: it must be a definite promise, meant seriously, with the buyer's entry into the main contract as consideration.

**The doctrine of merger.** On completion, the contract **merges into the transfer**. Afterwards, the buyer generally can only rely on what the transfer itself promises (right to convey, quiet possession, free of encumbrances, further documents). Policy: **finality**. Buyers must check things **before** completing.

Exceptions (*Di Cenzo Construction v. Glassco*, plus additions):

1. **Fraud**
2. **Mutual mistake** causing total failure of consideration or an *error in substantialibus*
3. A **contractual condition**
4. A **collateral warranty** meant to survive closing
5. An **innocent misrepresentation** amounting to *error in substantialibus*
6. **Statutory** warranties

**CPS clause 18** says there are no representations or warranties except those **in the contract** (and in an incorporated **PDS**), and that those **survive completion**. That's why written warranties matter.

> 🔎 ***Bouzane v. Murphy* (PEI).** A widow honestly said the roof was "new"; her late husband had only redone **half**. The MLS® listing said "new roof", but the contract had **no warranty** and no inspection subject. After completion, the buyer's claim **failed**: innocent misrepresentation, not fundamental, merged on closing.

**Protect buyers in writing:**

- Turn every seller assurance the buyer relies on into an **express warranty** in the CPS.
- **Incorporate the PDS**: "The attached Property Disclosure Statement dated (date) is incorporated into and forms part of this contract." If it's not available yet, use a **subject** to the buyer receiving and approving it, which then incorporates it.

**Found a problem before completion? Move fast.** Before completion (while the contract is **executory**), innocent misrepresentation can still support **rescission**. In *Olszewski v. Trapman* (Ontario), a lot sold as "approx. **1.5 acres**" was **1.15 acres**, too small for the planned house. The buyer refused to complete and got the **$100,000 deposit** back. Once completed, merger would have blocked the claim. Advise prompt **legal advice**.

**Remuneration in the CPS.** Recording a special bonus (e.g. "the Seller will pay a $1,000 selling bonus to [licensee] over the gross commission") is good evidence of **disclosure**. But you **can't sue** on the CPS to collect it: you're not a party (**privity**).

## Builders liens and Norfolk & Aikens

**Builders liens** (Lesson 3): unpaid contractors, subcontractors and suppliers can lien the land, even if the owner never dealt with them. An owner paying a contractor must hold back **10%** of each payment, limiting liability to the holdback. The holdback is released after the holdback period (**55 days** after the trigger dates) if no liens are filed.

- **Newly built or recently renovated property**: the buyer's licensee should include a **holdback clause** (Knowledge Base wording): the buyer's conveyancer holds back **10% of the price of the improvements** for 55 days after the certificate of completion (or, if none, 55 days after the later of the head contract ending or the occupancy permit).
- **Buying a strata lot from the owner developer**: the holdback is **mandatory by law** (*Strata Property Act* s.88; Regulation s.5.2: **7% of the gross price**), held until the earlier of the last lien-filing date or **55 days** after conveyance. Explain it's to protect against **liens**, **not** a way to withhold money for deficiencies.
- **Strata common property liens** show against **every** strata lot (full amount), blocking clear title until resolved.

**Norfolk & Aikens (1989 BCCA).** Buyers need mortgage money that only arrives **after** registration; sellers need sale money to pay off **their** mortgage. The CPS (**clauses 13 and 14**) lets transfers and mortgages be registered on the strength of **lawyers' and notaries' undertakings**, so both can use the sale and loan proceeds smoothly.

## Conditions precedent ("subjects")

A **condition precedent** is an event (other than just the passage of time) that must happen before the main obligations arise. If it doesn't happen by the deadline, the contract **ends**.

| Situation | Typical subject |
|---|---|
| Buyer must sell their current home first | "Subject to the Buyers entering into a contract to sell [address] that becomes unconditional on or before (date)." |
| Buyer of an auto shop fears contamination | "Subject to the Buyer obtaining an environmental report on or before (date) confirming the Property contains no environmental contamination." |
| Buyer needs a mortgage | Knowledge Base financing subject: amount, maximum rate, amortization, term and approximate payment. |
| Seller unsure about GST | "Subject to the Seller obtaining and being satisfied, on or before (date), with professional tax advice regarding the Seller's GST liability." |

(The standard CPS says GST is **included** in the price unless agreed otherwise.)

**Don't confuse:**

- **Condition subsequent**: ends obligations already in place ("If the builder hasn't got an occupancy permit by (date), this contract terminates").
- **Condition** (essential term): about breach, not subjects.
- **Covenant**: an unconditional promise.

### Removing subjects

Two ways, both by the **benefiting party**, **before the deadline**:

1. **Fulfil** it (e.g. get the mortgage approval), or
2. **Waive** it (e.g. inherit money and proceed without the mortgage).

Removal needs **written notice** to the other party. **Verbal isn't enough.** CPS **clause 3**: unless each condition is waived or declared fulfilled **by written notice** from the benefiting party **on or before** its date, the contract **terminates** and the deposit is returnable under RESA.

**True conditions precedent** depend entirely on a **third party** (e.g. "subject to the municipality annexing the property"). At common law they **can't be waived**. ***LEA* s.54** allows waiver if: it benefits **only** that party; the contract can be performed **without** it; and the waiver comes **before the deadline** (or within a reasonable time if none). Hence the BC habit: end every subject with "**This condition is for the sole benefit of the [Buyer/Seller].**" A subject for **both** parties can't be waived by either alone.

### Subjective vs objective subjects

The leading analysis is Justice Lambert's (dissenting) judgment in *Wiebe v. Bobsien* (1985 BCCA). Each case turns on its own wording.

| Type | Example | Legal effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Wholly subjective** ("whim and fancy") | "Subject to approval by the buyer company's president" | Probably **no contract yet**, just the seller's offer, which the seller may be able to **revoke** before subject removal |
| **Wholly objective** | "Subject to John Smith being elected mayor on Oct 15"; "subject to the buyer's lawyer confirming in writing by (date) that GST doesn't apply" | **Binding contract**; performance waits on the event; **neither** side can walk |
| **Partly subjective, partly objective** | "Subject to planning department approval of the attached subdivision plan" | Usually binding: courts imply a duty to take **reasonable steps**, unless too uncertain |

Recent cases:

- ***Pooni v. Storsley* (2022 BCSC)**: subjects letting the buyer approve "professional advice" about **riparian** limits "unsatisfactory to the Buyer", and the "feasibility of building a residence… in its **sole discretion**", were **too discretionary**. **No contract.**
- ***Davidson v. Merrick* (2021 BCSC)**: a **seller's** subject to buying a "**suitable** waterfront or semi-waterfront home" was wholly subjective. **No contract.**

**CPS clause 22** tries to save deals with subjective subjects: the seller grants the buyer an **option** (under **seal**) to keep the acceptance open until the subject date. After *Pooni*, both parties must **initial** the seal acknowledgement so the seal is adopted knowingly.

> ⚠️ Never tell a seller a subject is so subjective they can cancel. Different facts, different result. Send them to a **lawyer**.

### Trying in good faith to satisfy a subject

A contract with subjects **is** a contract. The benefiting party must at least act **honestly** and **reasonably**:

- ***Bhasin v. Hrynew*** (SCC 2014): duty of **honest performance** in every contract.
- ***Wastech*** (SCC 2021): contractual discretion must be exercised **reasonably**; reasons for not removing a subject must relate to the subject itself.
- ***Griffin v. Martens*** (BCCA 1988): "satisfactory financing" = satisfactory to a reasonable person with the buyer's own reasonable standards; buyer must use **best efforts**.
- ***Gordon Nelson v. Cameron*** (BCCA 2018): "in their **sole discretion**" lowered the standard to **honest efforts**. The exact words matter.
- ***Harris v. Wang***: buyers learned the seller's mother had **died** in the house (naturally), then simply **stopped trying** to arrange financing and inspection. Liable for **$274,000**.

> 🚨 **Subjects aren't a "get out of jail free" card.** Advising a buyer to back out by claiming they "couldn't get financing" when they just changed their mind is legally wrong, exposes them to a lawsuit (and a frozen deposit), and breaches **Rules s.33 (honesty)** and **s.34 (care and skill)**.

**Subject-free offers.** In hot markets buyers may skip subjects. Explain the risks and **document** the conversation (an email they confirm, or a BCREA acknowledgement form).

### Seven drafting best practices

1. **Use conditional language**: "subject to", "conditional upon", "unless". In *Gulston v. Aldred* (2011 BCCA), "the Seller **shall**… remedy the soil contamination… and obtain a certificate by May 29" was a **covenant**. The certificate came a month late, but the buyer **still had to complete** (they could only claim damages).
2. **Say who benefits**, even when it seems obvious.
3. **Avoid wholly subjective wording.** Add objective criteria (e.g. the Knowledge Base financing clause with amount, rate, amortization, term and payment).
4. **Check the other party can satisfy it** (e.g. ask for a mortgage **pre-approval**). The longer the subject period, the more this matters.
5. **Set a deadline** for every subject. Clause 3 assumes one. While subjects are open, the seller's property is effectively off the market, though they can accept **backup offers**. A backup offer needs its own subject: "Subject to the Seller ceasing to be obligated… under the Contract of Purchase and Sale dated (date)… including obtaining a full release… This condition is for the sole benefit of the Seller." Buyers' agents may add that the seller won't amend or extend the first contract.
6. **Consider a time clause**: the seller can (at any time, or on receiving another acceptable offer) give written notice requiring the buyer to remove all subjects within a set number of hours (typically **24–72**), excluding Sundays and statutory holidays, or the contract ends.
7. **Know when to get help**: managing broker first, then a lawyer.

## Amending a contract

- Before discussing a change, confirm with all parties that **talking won't end the existing contract**, and that the original stays **binding** until any amendment is signed.
- An amendment is a new contract, so it needs **fresh consideration or a seal** (Lesson 7).
- **Clause 12** makes **time of the essence**. When dates are changed, the law presumes time stays of the essence, but write it in: "**All other terms and conditions remain the same and time remains of the essence.**"
- **Every** party signs; deliver copies to each promptly.

## Assigning a contract of purchase and sale

**Regulation s.8.2** (Lessons 4 and 7): unless the client instructs otherwise **in writing**, a licensee-drafted offer must include the **standard assignment terms**:

1. no assignment without the **seller's written consent**, and
2. the **seller** gets any **profit** from an assignment.

It applies to **residential and commercial** offers, but **not** to developers' sales of REDMA development units.

- If a licensee-prepared offer leaves them out, the buyer's licensee must deliver the **Notice to Seller Regarding Assignment Terms** with the offer.
- If an **unrepresented** buyer's offer leaves them out, no Notice Form is required, but the **seller's licensee** must tell the seller: the terms are missing, **whether** the contract can be assigned, and on what conditions (including who gets any profit).

Other points:

- Don't describe the buyer as "**and/or nominee**" or "**and/or assignee**": it may make the buyer's identity uncertain (*Dunbar v. Dayson Holdings*).
- Assignment can hurt a seller who checked the original buyer's credit for a **vendor take-back** mortgage.
- If you know the buyer is likely to **flip** the contract, your **fiduciary duty** requires telling your seller client (Lesson 9).

### CSAIR (Condo and Strata Assignment Integrity Register)

A provincial database of **assignments** of purchase agreements for **residential strata lots**, run through REDMA. **Developers** collect and report the information.

- Applies to developments with **5 or more** residential strata lots (pre-sale or completed). Not commercial/industrial strata, bare land strata, or non-strata developments.
- Covers **full or partial** assignments, **subsequent** assignments, and assignments of leases of **3 years or more**.
- If a developer allows assignments, its purchase agreements must require the **developer's consent**, warn that information will be collected, and require the parties to provide it. If it doesn't allow them, the **disclosure statement** must say so.
- Developers collect detailed information (for individuals: legal name, birth date, citizenship, residency, SIN/ITN, address, contact details), plus assignment details (price, fees, amounts paid to the assignor).
- **Quarterly** reports via an LTSA portal until the strata plan is filed, a cumulative report then, and **annual** reports afterwards.
- Data can be shared with the **CRA** (to check the assignor's taxes), and assignment fees count toward **property transfer tax**.

**Why it matters to you:** buyers of pre-sale strata need to know whether they **can** assign, at what **fee**, and that their information will be collected. Some assignees won't accept that, which can make assignments harder to sell.

## Effective negotiation

Your clients hire you partly for your negotiating. The research-based approach:

**What makes a negotiation successful?**

- **Efficiency**: no more time and effort than the stakes justify.
- **Satisfaction** for both sides (sometimes walking away is the right result).
- **No value left on the table**: resources divided creatively so nothing useful is wasted.

**Two mindsets:**

| Distributive (novice) | Integrative (expert) |
|---|---|
| Win-lose tug of war over **one issue** (usually price) | **Joint problem-solving**, attacking the problem, not each other |
| Fixed on **positions** | Focused on **interests** (the "why") |
| Compromise down the middle | Combine issues to create **more value for both** |

### Preparation (the single biggest improvement)

1. **List the issues**: price, dates, subjects, repairs, chattels, storage, financing. More issues = more room for trades.
2. **Find the interests** behind each position: needs, fears, desires. "Must close June 17" might really be "can't carry two mortgages", which a bridge loan could also solve. Parties often have several interests, and spouses may differ. Basic needs (security, control, self-esteem) are often the most powerful.
3. **Set priorities**: what matters most to each side, what's tradeable, what's a deal breaker.
4. **Brainstorm possible solutions**: separate generating ideas from judging them.
5. **Find objective standards**: comparables, appraisals, replacement cost, expert opinion, law.
6. **Know your alternatives** (your **BATNA**): a strong backup keeps you calm and stops you looking desperate. Power depends on **both** sides' alternatives, and on how they're **perceived**. You only get **one** alternative, not a mix.
7. **Set your resistance point** (bottom line) in advance, so ego, emotion or effort already spent don't push you past it.
8. **Set a high aspiration target**. Specific, challenging goals produce better results. Don't fixate on your bottom line.

### At the table

- **Exchange**: focus on **interests** (share yours, ask "why?", offer options and watch preferences); **package** several issues together rather than one at a time; argue about the **standard**, not the number; make concessions **reciprocally** (don't make two in a row).
- **Communication**: **listen actively**, ask genuine clarifying questions, and paraphrase back.
- **Relationship**: be **firm on interests, flexible on positions**, and polite. See the deal **through their eyes** and let them save face.

### Dirty tactics, and how to respond

Name the tactic calmly, discuss it factually, and take a **break** if it continues.

| Tactic | Defence |
|---|---|
| **False authority** ("my partner won't approve") | Confirm up front who can decide; treat any deal as tentative until they agree |
| **Eleventh-hour throw-in** ("just one more thing") | Expect it; ask for something in return |
| **Deception** | Verify facts independently (e.g. logging rights confirmed by the regulator, not the seller) |
| **Stress** (fake deadlines, "another offer is coming", bad venue) | Notice your stress and change the arrangements |
| **Threats and negative emotions** | Point it out; pause until it stops |

**Two more forces:**

- **Time**: the side with more time usually has the upper hand.
- **Anchoring**: the **first number** (list price, first offer) pulls the whole negotiation toward it. Resist anchors with objective standards, and set good anchors yourself.

## Risk management for licensees

Lawsuits are stressful and costly even when groundless. Three strategies: **shift**, **anticipate**, **control**.

### 1. Shift the risk

- **Insurance**: the mandatory E&O Indemnity Plan limit is **$2,000,000**. It excludes **prior acts** and **property management**. Large commercial dealers may buy extra private coverage.
- **Refer out** work you rarely do (e.g. property management) to specialists.
- **Third-party experts**: for technical questions (foundations, contamination), refer to experts, and suggest **several** so the client chooses.
- **The Property Disclosure Statement** (below).

### 2. Anticipate the risk

- **Prevent buyer's remorse**: be clear whom you represent, and that you're a marketing specialist, not a structural engineer.
- **Document** disclosures and disclaimers (a short letter or file note). In court, a consumer's vivid memory beats your vague one.
- **Document your listing price advice**, especially in a hot market ("you undersold it").
- Use experienced colleagues, the **Knowledge Base**, and **continuing education**.

### 3. Control the risk

- Brokerage **complaint procedures**: report complaints to the managing broker early.
- Respond **sensitively**, even to silly complaints. Early explanation avoids the **litigation flashpoint**.

### The Property Disclosure Statement (PDS)

Created by **BCREA** in **1992**. It records what the seller said, so both sides know what was disclosed.

- Four parts of questions: **Land** (encroachments, unregistered easements, tenancies, **oil tanks**, local improvement charges), **Services** (water, sewer, service contracts), **Building** (insulation including asbestos, fireplaces, infestations, structure, additions **and permits**, HVAC, moisture, electrical/gas/plumbing, unauthorized suites) and **General** (cannabis growing, illegal substance production, latent defects, heritage and archaeological restrictions).
- Answers: **Yes / No / Do Not Know / Does Not Apply**, signed and dated by the seller. The buyer signs an acknowledgement.
- Versions for residential, strata, rural, land-only and First Nations leasehold properties.
- The PDS goes **beyond** what common law requires (known latent defects that are dangerous or make the home unfit). The **listing licensee's** separate **Rules s.59** duty still applies, but is satisfied if the buyer already received the disclosure **in writing** (e.g. in the PDS).
- A PDS **doesn't** relieve the licensee from checking what can be checked (e.g. at city hall), or investigating doubtful answers.

**Is a PDS required?** **No** law requires one. Boards' MLS® rules and lenders have historically wanted one. Since **2025**, BCREA offers the **Property No-Disclosure Statement (PNDS)** for sellers who choose to disclose nothing extra, replacing the old practice of crossing out the PDS.

> 🚨 ***Sewell v. Abadian* (2025 BCCA).** The seller **crossed out every PDS question**, noting truthfully that the home had been tenanted. But he **knew** about an addition built without a permit. The court held that the crossed-out form, read as a whole, still represented that he knew of **no** unpermitted additions. The buyer recovered its **$300,000 deposit**. This case prompted the PNDS.

**PDS or PNDS?** Explain the pros and cons, but **don't advise** which to choose; that's legal advice. Send the seller to a lawyer.

| | Advantages | Drawbacks / limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Seller** | Written record of what was disclosed; shields against misrepresentation claims; shows openness | Extra disclosure may lower offers; careless answers ("do not know" when they **do** know) create liability |
| **Buyer** | More information to target inspections and price the offer | Answers cover only the seller's **actual current knowledge**. They're **not a warranty**. A "no" doesn't mean no problem ever existed |

- ***Roberts v. Hutton* (2013 BCSC)**: sellers disclosed a fixed pond leak and honestly believed it was repaired. Hidden rot found later: **claim dismissed**.
- ***Nixon v. MacIver* (2016 BCCA)**: PDS said the roof was **6 years old**; part of the house was an older cabin with an old roof. The seller's belief was honest: **claim dismissed**.

**Making the PDS count.** Because the CPS has an **entire agreement** clause, a buyer usually can't sue on a false (but not fraudulent) PDS answer unless the PDS is **incorporated into the contract**. Whether the seller agrees is a matter for negotiation.

**Changes after signing.** The PDS says the seller will tell the buyer about **material changes** before completion. In *Sproule v. Nicholas* (2024 NSSC), sellers found a new leak, patched the drywall and said nothing. The incorporated PDS required updates, so they were liable (collateral warranty breach and negligent misrepresentation). Advise sellers that updating is safest.

> 🌿 **Cannabis.** The PDS asks whether the premises were used to grow cannabis (other than as permitted by law) or make illegal substances. Grow-ops can mean altered wiring, fire risk, mould, and insurance problems. Advise sellers to answer **honestly**.

### Environmental risk

- If you discover contamination (old dump, buried tank), you **must tell your seller client**. Hiding it to protect the price can make **you** liable.
- Avoid drafting broad "no toxic substances" warranties: "toxic" has no fixed meaning, and a warranty about one substance won't cover another. Refer complex clauses to **lawyers and environmental consultants**.
- You don't have to do environmental assessments, but you should **recommend** them where appropriate, flag things you notice (e.g. suspected asbestos), and **document** your advice.
- **Home inspectors** must be **licensed** in BC (since **March 31, 2009**, under the *Business Practices and Consumer Protection Act*). Check the licence before referring one.

## Two alerts

> 🚨 **ALERT 1: Vague = void.** "And/or", "usual terms", "to be arranged", unexplained abbreviations, "satisfactory to the buyer". Each can mean **no contract and no commission**. Write terms a stranger could enforce.

> 🚨 **ALERT 2: Deposits belong to the deal, not the buyer.** Once there's a trade, the brokerage is a **stakeholder**. No release (or HBRP rescission, or court order), no payout. Even for a cheque not yet banked.

## Trap list: things the exam likes to twist

1. *LEA* **s.59**: land contracts need **writing signed by the party to be charged**, or conduct (e.g. deposit) or reliance. **Leases of 3 years or less** are exempt.
2. Writing binds only the **signer**. A buyer-only signature lets the **seller** enforce, not the buyer.
3. A witness must be **19+** and **see** the signing. Never witness a faxed or emailed signature.
4. The listing contract is between **seller and brokerage**. The buyer isn't a party.
5. Commission needs a buyer whose offer matches the listing **exactly** (*Brian Fisher*: cash offer vs VTB terms → no commission).
6. Search **title** when listing. "The seller told me" is **no defence** if you could have checked.
7. *Competition Act* **s.45** (criminal): fixing commissions or splits, allocating territories. Up to **14 years**. Implied agreements count. Discussing the split on **one particular sale** is fine.
8. **s.52** (criminal) needs **knowing or reckless**; **s.74.01** (civil) needs **no intent**.
9. **s.74.05** (selling above advertised price): licensees selling for an **owner** are exempt; for a **developer**, not.
10. CREA's 3 rules: **don't collude, don't discriminate over pricing, don't mislead**.
11. CPS **clause 16**: risk stays with the **seller until 12:01 a.m. on the Completion Date**.
12. No title instructions → courts presume **tenancy in common**. **Explain** both; don't **advise**.
13. "Mortgage **and/or** agreement for sale" → **no contract** (*Occidental Hotel*). Courts won't enforce a contract to make a contract.
14. A deposit is **always recoverable** where there's no binding contract.
15. **HBRP**: residential only, **3 business days**, **0.25%**, **can't be waived**, starts the **business day after** acceptance. Excludes leasehold, auction, court-ordered, REDMA units.
16. HBRP notice: registered mail (when **sent**), fax or email with read receipt (when **transmitted**).
17. HBRP rescission: the brokerage pays **0.25%** to the seller and returns the rest **without a release**. Notices kept **7 years**.
18. The deposit is **not consideration**; the exchange of **promises** is.
19. The brokerage holds deposits as **stakeholder** for all parties (RESA **s.28**): release only on HBRP rescission, **signed release by all**, or **payment into court**.
20. A buyer's **lawyer** isn't a stakeholder unless they **undertake** to be.
21. Deposit forfeiture: **5–10%** is reasonable; relief only if not a pre-estimate **and** unconscionable (*Lee v. Skalbania*).
22. **Merger**: after completion, only the transfer and **surviving** promises (clause 18) can be sued on. Put assurances in writing, and **incorporate the PDS**.
23. Builders liens: **10%** holdback for **55 days**. Strata lot from **owner developer**: mandatory **7%** holdback, not a deficiency set-off.
24. **Norfolk & Aikens** = completing on **lawyers' undertakings**.
25. Remove subjects by **written notice**, **before the deadline**, by the **benefiting party**. Verbal doesn't count.
26. *LEA* **s.54** waiver: **sole benefit**, contract still workable, **before** the deadline.
27. **Wholly subjective** subject → maybe **no contract** yet; **wholly objective** → binding contract, performance suspended.
28. "**Shall**" without "subject to" = a **covenant**, not a subject (*Gulston v. Aldred*).
29. "Sole discretion" → **honest** efforts (*Gordon Nelson*); "satisfactory financing" alone → **best** efforts (*Griffin*).
30. Amendments need **fresh consideration or a seal**; state that **time remains of the essence**.
31. Reg **s.8.2** standard assignment terms: seller's **written consent**; seller gets the **profit**. Notice Form if omitted. Not REDMA units.
32. **CSAIR**: developers report assignments of residential strata lots in projects of **5+** lots.
33. **PDS** isn't legally required and isn't a **warranty**: it reflects the seller's **actual knowledge**. **PNDS** (2025) for no extra disclosure.
34. E&O limit **$2M**; excludes **prior acts** and **property management**.
35. Home inspectors must be **licensed** (since 2009). Check before referring.

## Drill

**Q1.** You represent a tenant. The landlord verbally agrees to a new 5-year lease and says, "Tell your client to firm up, I'll sign next week." Your client's purchase of the business is subject to getting that lease. What do you advise?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Don't remove the subject on a verbal promise. A lease over **3 years** must be in **writing** to be enforceable (*LEA* s.59). In *Pepper's Produce*, a licensee was found negligent for advising removal of the subject on a verbal assurance. Keep the subject (or extend it) until the signed lease (or a signed agreement to lease) is in hand, and recommend the client's lawyer review it.

</details>

**Q2.** A seller lists at $900,000, "$200,000 down, seller will carry the balance at 5%". You bring a full-price **all-cash** offer. The seller refuses. Is your brokerage owed commission?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Probably **not**. Commission is triggered by a buyer whose offer meets the listing terms **exactly**. A cash offer isn't the same as the seller's financing terms; the seller may have wanted the interest income (*Brian Fisher Realty v. Emerald Park Lodge*). Lesson: write listing terms carefully, and talk to the seller about what alternatives they'd accept.

</details>

**Q3.** An offer is accepted on Thursday, July 6 for a strata condo. The buyer emails a rescission notice (read receipt requested) on Monday, July 10 at 9 p.m. Is it valid? What happens to the $60,000 deposit on a $1,200,000 price?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Valid**. The 3 business days run Friday July 7, Monday July 10 and Tuesday July 11 (Saturday and Sunday don't count), so Monday is in time. Email with a read receipt requested is deemed delivered when **transmitted**. The fee is **0.25% × $1,200,000 = $3,000**. The brokerage pays **$3,000 to the seller** and promptly returns **$57,000 to the buyer**, with no release needed. Keep the notice as a trading record for **7 years**.

</details>

**Q4.** A buyer's deal collapses. The buyer says the seller breached; the seller says the buyer breached. Both want the deposit. What does the brokerage do?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

The brokerage holds the deposit as a **stakeholder** for both parties (RESA s.28). It can't pay either side without a **release signed by all parties**. Prepare a release; if they can't agree, the brokerage can apply to **pay the money into court** (RESA s.33) and should get legal advice. Never tell the buyer they'll "automatically" get it back.

</details>

**Q5.** Your buyer client's offer says "subject to the Buyer being satisfied with the property in the Buyer's sole discretion." Two days later a better property appears and the buyer wants out. They ask you to tell the seller "the inspection didn't work out". Your response?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- Don't misrepresent. Subjects must be pursued **honestly** and discretion used **reasonably** (*Bhasin*, *Wastech*); lying would breach **Rules s.33 and s.34** and could expose the buyer to damages (*Harris v. Wang*).
- The wording is also very **subjective**, so there may be a legal question whether a binding contract even exists (*Wiebe*, *Pooni*), and clause 22's seal option may matter.
- That's a question for a **lawyer**, not you. Explain the risks honestly and recommend legal advice before they act.

</details>

**Q6.** A buyer client discovers, a week before completion, that the lot is 20% smaller than the contract states. What do you advise, and why the urgency?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Get **legal advice immediately**, before completion. While the contract is still **executory**, the buyer may be able to rescind for innocent misrepresentation and recover the deposit (*Olszewski v. Trapman*). Once they complete, the **doctrine of merger** generally bars claims for innocent misrepresentation unless it's an *error in substantialibus* or a surviving warranty. Completing could cost them their remedy.

</details>

**Q7.** Your seller accepts an offer subject to financing in 21 days. Another buyer wants to make a firm offer. How can you structure this to protect your seller?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- Accept the second offer as a **backup offer**, with a subject that the seller ceases to be obligated under the first contract (including a full release) by a set date, for the seller's sole benefit, so the seller never has two firm contracts.
- If the first contract has a **time clause**, give written notice requiring the first buyer to remove subjects within the set hours (e.g. 24–72). If they don't, the first contract ends and the backup proceeds.
- Without a time clause, the seller must wait out the 21 days.
- Make sure the backup buyer understands the first contract can't be amended to their detriment if their licensee negotiated that term.

</details>

## Mindset: an unenforceable contract hurts everyone

An unenforceable contract **always** harms someone: the buyer loses the property, the seller loses the sale, and **you lose the commission**, plus your reputation and maybe your licence. The cases in this lesson weren't about bad people. They were about **careless words**.

Your job when drafting:

- Write terms a **stranger could enforce**.
- Verify facts with **outside sources**.
- Put every promise your client relies on **in writing**.
- Use **tested clauses**.
- Know the line between **explaining** (your job) and **advising on law** (the lawyer's).

> 🏢 **For commercial specifically.** Commercial contracts are usually **custom**, drafted with lawyers and accountants, so the drafting may not be yours, but the **issues** are:
>
> - **Environmental subjects** (Phase I/II reports) and contamination warranties
> - **Leases over 3 years** that must be written
> - **Vendor take-back** and assumed financing that must be precisely described
> - **Builders lien holdbacks** on new buildings
> - **Assignments and nominee buyers**
> - **Inventory schedules** for business assets
> - The *Competition Act*: never discuss fees or splits with competing brokerages beyond a specific deal, and watch **lease exclusivity clauses** and **restrictive covenants** that limit competing businesses
>
> Negotiation skill matters even more: commercial deals have **many issues** to package and trade, and sophisticated parties who use anchoring and false-authority tactics.

> **This week's habit.** Write three subject clauses from scratch (financing, environmental, sale of the buyer's property), then compare them with BCFSA's Knowledge Base versions. Check each one: conditional language? Who benefits? Objective criteria? A deadline?
