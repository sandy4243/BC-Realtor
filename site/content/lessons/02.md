## Why "what am I actually selling?" is the first real question

> **Scenario.** Marco wants to buy a small industrial lot in Abbotsford with a 6,000 sq ft warehouse on it. Asking price: $3.1 million. On the first walkthrough you notice four things:
>
> 1. A neighbour's delivery trucks cut across the back corner of the lot to reach their loading bay.
> 2. A tenant, a cabinet maker, is operating out of half the building. The seller says "it's a handshake deal, two years, nothing on paper."
> 3. A row of heavy steel pallet racking is bolted to the warehouse floor.
> 4. The title lists the seller *and* her brother as owners.
>
> Marco asks: "So for $3.1 million, what exactly do I get?"

Every one of those four things is a question from this lesson:

| What you saw | The legal question | Section |
|---|---|---|
| Trucks crossing the corner | Is that an **easement** (binds Marco) or just a **licence** (doesn't)? | Interests less than estates |
| The handshake tenant | Does an unregistered short lease bind a buyer? | Leasehold estates |
| The bolted racking | **Fixture** (comes with the land) or **chattel** (seller takes it)? | Fixtures vs chattels |
| Two names on title | **Joint tenants** or **tenants in common**? Who has to sign? | Co-ownership |

A buyer is never buying "a building". They're buying **a bundle of legal rights**, with some of the rights already held by other people. Your job is to know which rights are in the bundle and which have been carved out. This lesson gives you that vocabulary.

> 🧭 **How this lesson is built.** It covers the course's Preface (the big picture of land, value and who's in the market) and Chapter 3 (estates and interests in land). The Preface is easier reading; Chapter 3 is the heavier exam material.

## Vocabulary first

| Word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| **Real property** | Land, plus everything permanently attached to it (buildings, fixtures). Also called *real estate* or *realty*. |
| **Personal property** | Everything else you can own: cars, furniture, shares, equipment. Also called *chattels* or *personalty*. |
| **Estate** | A right to *possess and use* land for a period of time. The "period" is what separates one estate from another. |
| **Fee simple** | The biggest estate a private person can hold. It lasts indefinitely and can be sold, mortgaged or left in a will. The closest thing to "ownership". |
| **Freehold estate** | An estate of uncertain length: fee simple and life estate. |
| **Leasehold estate** | An estate for a set period: a lease. |
| **Interest in land** | A right *over* someone else's land that's smaller than an estate: easements, restrictive covenants, profits à prendre. |
| **Charge / encumbrance** | Anything registered against a title that limits the owner's rights: a mortgage, easement, covenant, lien, life estate. |
| **Run with the land** | A right or burden that stays attached to the land when it's sold, binding (or benefiting) the next owner automatically. |
| **Grantor / grantee** | The person giving a right / the person receiving it. |
| **Instrument** | A legal document that creates or transfers rights, such as a transfer, mortgage or easement agreement. |
| **Land Title Act (LTA)** | BC's statute for registering land ownership and charges. Lesson 3 covers it in depth. |
| **Property Law Act (PLA)** | A BC statute filling in many land-law rules: co-ownership presumptions, cancelling old covenants, and more. |

> 💡 **The single most useful picture for this lesson.** Imagine land ownership as a **bundle of sticks**. Each stick is one right: to live there, rent it out, build on it, dig under it, sell it, leave it to your kids. Fee simple is (almost) the whole bundle. Everything else in this lesson is someone *holding one or more sticks* that aren't the owner's.

## Who really owns land in BC

Short answer: **the Crown.** Lesson 1 traced this back to 1066. Here's what it means in practice.

Only the Crown (the government, acting for the public) holds **absolute** title. Private people hold **estates granted out of the Crown's title**. The Crown's land was once called the *regal estate*, which is where the phrase **"real estate"** comes from.

The Crown keeps three powers over every fee simple owner. A fee simple owner's rights are limited **only** by:

1. **Expropriation and tax sale.** The Crown can take the land for public use (with compensation) and can sell it if property taxes go unpaid.
2. **Land use and building regulation.** Zoning, building codes and similar rules.
3. **Other people's common law rights.** You can't use your land in a way that harms your neighbours' rights (see nuisance, later).

Inside those limits, a fee simple owner can:

- **sell** the whole bundle,
- **grant all the rights for a set time** (a 99-year ground lease, for example), or
- **grant part of the rights** (an easement to a neighbour, a licence to a billboard company).

> 🏢 **Commercial angle.** Long ground leases are common in commercial real estate. A developer may never own the land under its tower; it holds a 60- or 99-year leasehold estate from the fee simple owner (often a government, university or First Nation). When you value or sell such a property, you're selling the *leasehold*, and its value shrinks as the term runs down.

### Why "real" property?

In medieval England, if someone took your *land*, the court could order the land itself returned to you: a **"real" remedy** (from the Latin *res*, thing). If someone took your *personal* belongings, the court would usually only award money. That historical split is why land is "real" property and everything else is "personal" property.

## First Nations land rights

BC was occupied by First Nations long before European settlement, and most of BC was never covered by historical treaties. First Nations property rights fall into **three categories**:

| Category | Who holds the land | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Reserves under the *Indian Act*** | The **federal** government owns it. | The First Nation holds a **collective right to use and occupy** it. It doesn't own it. |
| **2. Treaty settlement lands** | The First Nation, often in **fee simple**, with law-making powers over it. | Modern treaties since 2000: **Nisga'a, Tsawwassen, Maa-nulth, Tla'amin**. |
| **3. Aboriginal title** | The First Nation, **collectively**. | A right that **survived** Crown sovereignty unless a treaty extinguished it. Established by a court declaration or by agreement. |

**Aboriginal title**, as the courts describe it, gives the title-holding group the right to:

- decide how the land is used,
- enjoy and occupy it,
- possess it,
- benefit economically from it, and
- proactively use and manage it.

Two features to remember: it's **collective** (held for present and future generations), and it **can't be sold to anyone except the Crown**. That's why Aboriginal title isn't an "estate" in the normal sense.

> 🏢 **Commercial angle.** Some of BC's largest commercial and residential developments are on First Nations land under long leases (for example, Musqueam, Squamish and Tsawwassen lands in Metro Vancouver). Lesson 3 covers how title is registered on these lands, which is often *not* in the provincial land title system.

## What makes land different from other things you can buy

The Preface identifies four characteristics of real property. They explain why real estate markets behave the way they do.

### 1. Immobility

You can't move land to a better market. If Kamloops has a shortage of industrial lots, the surplus in the Slocan Valley doesn't help. Consequences:

- **Markets are local.** Prices in Prince George say little about prices in Victoria.
- **Location drives value.** A parcel's value depends on how easily people and goods get to and from it.
- **Externalities.** What happens *next door* changes your value, and you can't escape it. A rendering plant moving in beside a house drags its value down; a new SkyTrain station pushes it up.
- **Development patterns.** A site first attracts a use for natural reasons (waterfront, rail line). Later, the *existing* use attracts more of the same, and the area becomes "the industrial area" or "the office district".

**Myth to unlearn: "They're not making any more land."** The physical surface is fixed, but the supply of **usable urban land** is not:

- Farmland converts to urban use when developers can pay more than its farming value. That **agricultural value acts as a floor** under land prices at the edge of a city.
- **Zoning** is often the real limit, a legal one, not a physical one.
- Land can be **intensified**: a house lot becomes a fourplex, then a mid-rise.
- Faster transport makes distant land usable.

### 2. Durability

Land itself is essentially indestructible, and buildings last decades. But **durable doesn't mean valuable forever.**

- New construction rarely adds more than about **1% a year** to the existing stock. So supply responds slowly to demand, and prices can swing a lot in the short run.
- A building has a **physical life** (until it falls down) and an **economic life** (until it's worth more to demolish it and build something else). The economic life usually ends first.
- An empty building produces nothing; its shelter "service" for that month is gone forever.

### 3. Indivisibility of services

A property's usefulness comes from the land *and* its improvements *and* off-site services (roads, transit, utilities) working together. You can't really separate them. Even so, two systems split land from buildings on paper:

- **Property assessment** values land and improvements separately.
- **Income tax**: commercial investors can claim depreciation on buildings but not on land, so they must allocate the price between the two.

### 4. Divisibility of ownership

One parcel can be split many ways, physically and legally:

- **Physically**: horizontal boundaries (a subdivision into lots) or vertical ones (floors, air space parcels, strata units).
- **Legally**: owner–landlord–tenant–subtenant; lender and borrower; partners; shareholders in a company that owns the building.

This is what lets **small investors** buy a slice of a large asset and **diversify** across many properties. Real estate investment trusts (REITs) and syndicates are built on it.

### Where land value comes from

Nobody wants land for its own sake. They want what it lets them **do**: live, sell goods, store inventory, grow food. So the value of land is the **value of the services it provides**, and a price is the market's estimate of all those future services rolled into one number (the *capitalized* value). Trading only happens when both sides think they're better off.

## Who's in the market

You'll work with all of these people. Know what each one does, and just as importantly, **what they don't do**.

| Participant | What they do | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **Buyers and sellers** | The two essential parties. Everyone else serves them. | Their real motivations matter more than the stated ones. |
| **Licensees (you)** | Sell **services**, not land. Usually paid by **commission**. Most belong to a local board, the provincial association, and CREA. | You're regulated by BCFSA under RESA (Lessons 4–5). |
| **Appraisers** | Estimate **market value** from the property and comparable sales. Lenders rely on them. | |
| **Lawyers and notaries** | Draft and review contracts, check title, prepare closing documents, register the transfer, handle the money. | **Notaries** have a narrower scope set by the *Notaries Act*. They can do conveyancing, but can't litigate or give broad legal advice. |
| **Inspectors** | A visual inspection of structure and systems, with a written report. | |
| **Land surveyors** | Fix boundaries on the ground (*cadastral* surveys) and spot **encroachments**: a building or fence over the line. | |
| **Assessors (BC Assessment)** | Set each property's assessed value and **property class** each year under the *Assessment Act*. Local governments use it to set taxes. | ⚠️ **Assessed value ≠ market value.** It's a mass-valuation snapshot from a fixed date. Never price a property off its assessment. |
| **Rental property managers / strata managers** | Manage rental properties for owners, or run a strata corporation's affairs. | Must be **licensed under RESA** unless exempt. |
| **Borrowers (mortgagors)** | The owner who **gives** a mortgage to a lender as security for a loan. | The borrower is the mort*gagor*: the one who grants. Exam favourite. |

## Residential vs IC&I: the two markets

Licensees usually specialize in one of two markets. **IC&I** stands for **Industrial, Commercial and Investment**. Since you're headed toward commercial, read this table twice.

| | Residential | IC&I |
|---|---|---|
| **Deal size** | From a $10,000 mobile home to a $20M+ mansion | Typically **$5M** (small development site) to **$100M+** |

| **Segments** | Few: house, condo, by price and area | Many: office, regional shopping centres, industrial, retail, apartments, warehouses, mixed use, development sites and land assemblies, mobile home parks, hotels, businesses and franchises, nursing homes |

## Estates: the "how long" of ownership

Under the *Law and Equity Act*, English law as it stood on **19 November 1858** applies in BC unless it doesn't fit local conditions or has been changed by statute. That's why BC still uses medieval English land concepts, including the **doctrine of estates**.

An **estate** is a right to possess and use land **for a period of time**. Estates differ by how long they last.

> 🔎 **Two practical survivals of the old system.**
> - **Expropriation**: the Crown can take land back (with compensation).
> - **Escheat**: if someone dies **without a will and without heirs**, their land goes to the Crown.

> ⚠️ **Licensees must never give legal advice about estates and interests.** Know them well enough to spot issues and refer the client to a lawyer.

### Fee simple

The largest estate. Historically it lasted "as long as the owner has heirs", which in practice means **indefinitely**.

- **"Fee"** = it can be inherited.
- **"Simple"** = no restriction on *which* heirs can inherit (as opposed to the old *fee tail*, which limited inheritance to direct descendants and is no longer possible in Canada).

A fee simple owner can freely sell, mortgage, lease or will the land. That freedom to transfer is called **alienability**. In BC, a fee simple is transferred by registering a **Form A Freehold Transfer** in the land title office.

### Life estate

A life estate lasts for **one person's lifetime**. Like fee simple, it's a *freehold* estate, because its length is uncertain. It's registered as a **charge** on the fee simple title.

**Who's who:**

| Role | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Life tenant** | The person who holds the life estate and can use the property while alive. |
| **Reversioner** | The original owner, if the property comes **back** to them when the life tenant dies. ("A gives B a life estate"; when B dies it *reverts* to A.) |
| **Remainderman** | A third person who gets the fee simple when the life tenant dies. ("A gives B a life estate, *remainder* to C.") C is on title as owner; B's life estate is a charge. |

> **Example.** A will says: *"To my wife, Clara, for life, with remainder to my son, John."* Clara can live in (or rent out) the house for the rest of her life. John owns the fee simple but can't take possession until Clara dies.

**Estate *pur autre vie*** ("for the life of another"). Clara remarries and moves out. She sells her life estate to Mrs. Smith. Mrs. Smith now holds an estate measured by **Clara's** life, not her own. When Clara dies, Mrs. Smith must leave, and John takes the property. Because nobody knows how long these estates will last, they're **hard to sell for value** and rare today.

**Who pays for what?**

| Life tenant pays | Remainderman / reversioner pays |
|---|---|
| Yearly running costs: utilities, heat, **property taxes** | **Mortgage principal** |
| **Mortgage interest** | **Insurance premiums** |

The life tenant also keeps any income the land produces (rents, crops).

### Waste: protecting the person who gets the land next

A life tenant mustn't run down the value of what the remainderman inherits. The law calls damage or change by a life tenant **waste**, in four kinds:

| Type | What it is | Example | Liable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Voluntary** | Actively damaging or destroying | Tearing down the detached garage | **Yes** |
| **Permissive** | Letting it deteriorate by doing nothing | Not fixing the leaking roof | **Generally no** |
| **Ameliorating** | Actively changing it in a way that *improves* it | Building a backyard deck | Technically yes, but **usually no damages** (it's worth more) |
| **Equitable** | Flagrant, malicious destruction | Burning the house down | **Yes**, even if excused from the other types |

A will or deed can say the life tenant holds **"without impeachment for waste"**. That excuses the **three common law types** only. To excuse **equitable** waste too, the document must say so **expressly**.

> 🧠 **Memory trick.** **V**oluntary = **V**andal. **P**ermissive = **P**assive. **A**meliorating = **A**dds value. **E**quitable = **E**vil.

### Why a life estate matters to you

- **Listing:** if there's a life estate on title, the life tenant **and** the fee simple owner must all sign the listing and agree to the sale. A buyer wants a clean fee simple; the life estate has to be released.
- **Financing:** lenders dislike lending against a life estate. If the life tenant dies, the security disappears. A lender may insist on life insurance.
- **Property management:** you need to know who has the right to rent the property and collect income.

### Leasehold estate

A lease gives a tenant the right to exclusive possession for a **set term**. Leases started out as mere personal contracts and only later became recognized as estates that bind later owners. Lessons on tenancies come later; for now, one rule matters a lot:

> 🔑 **LTA s.23: the short-lease exception.** A buyer normally takes title subject only to what's registered. But a lease of **3 years or less**, where the tenant is **actually occupying** the property, binds the buyer **even if it's not registered**.

Back to Marco: the cabinet maker's two-year handshake lease, with the tenant in occupation, **binds Marco** if he buys. You must find out the terms (rent, end date, renewal rights) **before** he writes an offer, and the contract must deal with it. Look at the tenant, not just the title.

### Aboriginal title is not an estate

Aboriginal title is sometimes mentioned alongside estates, but it's a different thing. It comes from **prior occupation**, not from a Crown grant, it's **collective**, and it can be transferred **only to the Crown**.

## Interests less than an estate

These are rights over land that **don't** give possession. They matter because they're usually **on title** and they **bind the buyer**.

**Why they "run with the land."** In ordinary contract law, only the people who made a contract are bound by it (*privity of contract*). If that applied here, a new owner could ignore a neighbour's right of way. Interests in land are an exception: they **attach to the land itself** and bind later owners. In BC, they generally must be **registered** under the LTA to do that.

Three main types: **easements**, **restrictive covenants** and **profits à prendre**.

### Easements

An **easement** is a right to use a neighbour's land in a particular way, without possessing it, for the benefit of your own land. Examples: a right of way across a driveway, a right to run a drain or pipe, a right to light.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Dominant tenement** | The land that **benefits** (gets to use the right). |
| **Servient tenement** | The land that **carries the burden** (has to put up with it). |

**Three requirements for a valid easement:**

1. **There must be a dominant and a servient tenement.** A right that isn't attached to any benefiting land (an "easement in gross") is usually just a **licence**, except for statutory rights of way (below).
2. **It must benefit the dominant land itself,** making the land more usable, not just give the current owner a personal perk.
3. **It must be capable of being granted.** The right must be clearly defined, and the grantor must have the power to grant it. A tenant can't grant an easement lasting longer than its own lease.

**How easements are created:**

- **By statute.**
- **Expressly**, in a written agreement (the usual way).
- **By implication**, when the parties obviously intended it but didn't write it down. Examples: support between two attached houses; access to a shared well; **necessity** (a lot that's otherwise landlocked).
- ~~**By prescription**~~ (long use): **abolished in BC.** Using a neighbour's path for 30 years doesn't create a legal right here.

**Statutory right of way (SRW), LTA s.218.** A special BC tool: governments, utilities and certain others can hold a right of way **without** any dominant tenement. BC Hydro power lines, gas pipelines, municipal sewers and public walkways are typically SRWs. Expect to see them on many titles.

**On the title:** an easement shows up as a **charge on the servient** land's title, and as a **legal notation on the dominant** land's title (so the buyer of the benefiting land can see what it gets).

**Ending an easement:** by agreement between the current owners of both properties, or by court order under **PLA s.35** (below).

> ⚠️ **Very short-term arrangements** may be treated as a **licence**, not an easement, so they won't bind a buyer.

### Restrictive covenants

A **restrictive covenant** is a promise, registered on title, **not** to do something on your land, made for the benefit of a neighbouring property.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Covenantor** | The owner who is **bound** (burdened land). |
| **Covenantee** | The owner who **benefits** (benefited land). |

> **Example.** Jerome owns a view lot in West Vancouver. He sells the lot below, and the buyer agrees, by a registered covenant, never to build higher than 15 feet. Every future owner of the lower lot is bound, and every future owner of Jerome's lot can enforce it.

**Five requirements:**

1. It must be **negative** in substance (a promise *not* to do something). It can be phrased positively, but its effect must be a restriction.
2. It must **benefit** the covenantee's land.
3. Both the benefited and burdened lands must be **precisely identified**.
4. It must be **intended to bind the land**, not just the original owner (wording like "heirs and assigns" does this).
5. The person bound must have **notice**. In BC's registration system, **registration is notice**.

> 🚨 **ALERT: positive covenants don't run with the land.** A promise to *do* something (pay for maintenance, paint the fence every five years) generally does **not** bind the next owner.
>
> **But** in *Crystal Square* (Supreme Court of Canada, 2020), an office tower owner had agreed to pay towards maintaining a neighbouring parking garage. The tower later became a strata, which never formally signed on to that promise, yet it **paid the fees for years** while using the parking stalls. The court held that **by its conduct** the strata had entered a **new contract**, so it was bound to keep paying.
>
> Lesson: a buyer can become bound by an old positive obligation through **conduct** or by formally **assuming** it. Check what the seller has actually been paying, and why.

### Building schemes

A **building scheme** is a set of restrictive covenants applying to **every lot in a development**, so that each lot is both bound by and benefits from the same rules (house size, style, roof colour, number of buildings). Developers use them to protect the look and value of a subdivision.

- Created by registering a **Declaration of Creation of Building Scheme** under **LTA s.220(1)**. All lots must come from a **common vendor** (or common landlord).
- Requirements: restrictions must be **negative** in effect; the benefited and burdened lands must be **defined**; the title must be **registered** under the LTA.
- **Very common in bare land strata** developments.
- Changed or removed by agreement of **all** current owners (and possibly mortgage lenders' consent), or by a court under PLA s.35.
- In practice, enforcement can be weak: if everybody has ignored a rule for years, a new owner may not be able to enforce it.

**As a buyer's agent**, give the buyer the full building scheme, point out the restrictions, have them confirm in writing that they've read and approved it, and recommend a lawyer for anything complex.

### Profits à prendre

A **profit à prendre** (French: "right to take") is a right to go onto someone's land and **take something from it**: minerals, gravel, timber, fish, game, oil and gas. Different from an easement:

- It **can exist without a dominant tenement** ("in gross"). A logging company can hold one without owning neighbouring land.
- It **doesn't need a fixed term**.
- It **can't be implied**; it must be expressly granted.

### Getting rid of old charges: PLA s.35

Under **Property Law Act s.35**, the BC Supreme Court can **cancel or modify** a registered easement, statutory right of way, building scheme or restrictive covenant. Grounds include:

- it's **obsolete** because the neighbourhood or the land has changed;
- it **blocks reasonable use** of the land without any real benefit to anyone; or
- the person who benefits has **agreed**, expressly or by conduct (for example, by ignoring breaches for years).

You shouldn't advise on s.35. Just know it exists and refer the client to a lawyer.

> ⚠️ **"Clear title"** If a property has an easement, covenant or other charge that won't be discharged before completion, **never advertise or describe it as "clear title"**. Your listing and contract wording must match the real state of title.

### Licence: the thing that looks like an interest but isn't

A **licence** is personal **permission** to do something on someone else's land: park a truck, dump topsoil for a month, run a sign. It's a **contractual right only**:

- it's **not an interest in land**,
- it **can't be registered**, and
- it **doesn't bind a buyer** (doesn't run with the land).

> **Example.** In 1956, two friendly neighbours agreed that a pipe could run under one lot to bring lake water to the other. Nothing was registered. Decades later, both lots have new owners, the pipe needs replacing, and the owner of the lot with the pipe says "get off my land." A court would most likely find this was a **licence**, not an easement, so the new owner of the benefiting lot **can't enforce it**.

**Back to Marco:** if the neighbour's trucks cross the corner under a registered easement, Marco is bound. If it's an informal arrangement with the seller, it's probably a licence, and Marco can end it. Either way, **search title and ask the seller**.

## Above, below and around: how far ownership reaches

### Airspace

The old saying was that an owner owned "up to the heavens and down to the centre of the earth." Today, you own the airspace above your land **only as far as you can make effective use of it**. Federal law (the *Aeronautics Act*) lets aircraft fly over without liability unless they cause physical damage.

BC lets owners **subdivide airspace** into **air space parcels** under the LTA, with their own titles. Typical use: a building with commercial space on the lower floors and residential strata above, each in a separate legal parcel so the two can be managed and financed separately. These come with complicated easements for support, elevators, utilities and access.

> ⚠️ **Air space parcel ≠ density.** Density (floor space ratio, FSR) is a **zoning** limit on how much floor area you can build per square metre of land. An air space parcel is a **legal parcel** of space with its own title.

### Subsurface

Below ground, the **Crown keeps most of the valuable rights**. When BC granted land to private owners, it **reserved** minerals for itself (*Land Act* s.50). What exactly was reserved depends on the date of the original Crown grant:

| Reserved since | What |
|---|---|
| From the start | Gold and silver |
| 1897 | Base minerals (other than coal) |
| 1899 | Coal and petroleum |
| 1951 | Natural gas |

The province also claims **geothermal resources** (*Geothermal Resources Act*) and, unless the Crown grant says otherwise, **the beds of lakes and rivers** (*Land Act* s.55).

> 🏢 **Commercial angle.** For rural, resource or industrial deals, mineral reservations matter. A buyer of a gravel-rich parcel may not own the gravel the way they assume. Refer it to a lawyer to trace the original Crown grant.

### Water

Under the **Water Sustainability Act** (in force since **29 February 2016**), the right to use **all water, surface and ground**, belongs to the **province**. To use water you need a **water licence** (applied for through FrontCounter BC) and must pay annual fees. **Domestic use of groundwater** (a household well) is the main exception.

Key points for licensees:

- Water licences are **not recorded in the land title office**. Search the province's water rights databases separately.
- A licence can be **cancelled** after **three consecutive years** of not using the water, or of not paying the fees.
- A licence attached to the land **passes to the buyer, along with any unpaid fees**. Check for arrears.
- On a sale, the province must be notified with a **Change of Water Licence Holder** form.

> 🏢 **Commercial angle.** Farms, wineries, nurseries, campgrounds and some industrial users depend on water licences. A missing or lapsed licence can gut a property's value.

### Right to support

Every parcel has a natural right to **support from neighbouring land**, both **vertical** (from below) and **lateral** (from the side). If a neighbour excavates to the property line and your land slumps, they're liable.

But that natural right covers **the land only, not buildings**. A building's extra weight needs an **easement of support** to be protected.

## Fixtures vs chattels: what stays and what goes

This is one of the most common sources of fights between buyers and sellers.

- A **fixture** is something that was a chattel but has become **part of the land** by being attached. It **stays** with the property.
- A **chattel** is personal property. The **seller takes it** unless the contract says otherwise.

> **Example.** John lists his acreage. Annika's offer is accepted. On moving day, John starts dismantling the tool shed. Annika says the shed is part of what she bought. Who's right? It depends on the test below, and a good contract would have answered it in one line.

### The legal test (from *Stack v. T. Eaton Co.*, 1902)

1. **Not attached at all** (resting only by its own weight) → presumed a **chattel**, unless the circumstances show it was meant to be part of the land.
2. **Attached, even slightly** → presumed a **fixture**, unless the circumstances show it was meant to remain a chattel.

The presumption can be overturned by looking at:

- **Degree of attachment**: how firmly is it fixed, and how easily can it be removed without damage?
- **Object (purpose) of attachment**: was it attached to **improve the land permanently**, or just to **use the item better** as a chattel?

That second question is sometimes called the **"better use" test**. If an item is bolted down only so it works properly *as equipment*, it's likely still a chattel.

**Worked examples:**

- **Pictures hung on wires from screws**: technically attached (so presumed fixtures), but the attachment is minimal and only for displaying them → **chattels**.
- **A BC ranch sale** (*Monical*, 2013): free-standing **cattle troughs** → chattels. A cattle-handling machine **welded and bracketed** to the ground → still a **chattel**, because it was attached only to stabilize it while in use (better use of the machine, not improvement of the land).

**Back to Marco's pallet racking:** bolted to the floor (presumed fixture), but bolted mainly so it won't tip over while in use, and removable. It could go either way. Which is exactly the problem.

### Best practice

1. **Walk through with the seller** at listing. Note anything a buyer might assume is included (racking, machinery, appliances, window coverings, a hot tub, a generator, signage).
2. Give buyer and seller every chance to say what they want **included or excluded**.
3. **Write it into the contract** clearly, and let both sides read and confirm it.

A dispute here can cost your client money and, in practice, can cost you **part of your commission** when the deal goes sideways at completion.

## Co-ownership: two ways to share

Any estate can be held by more than one person, in one of two ways. Licensees **must not advise** buyers on which to choose (that's legal and tax advice), but must understand the difference.

### Joint tenancy

- Each owner holds the **whole** property together with the others; no one has a separate share.
- **Right of survivorship**: when one joint tenant dies, their interest passes **automatically to the surviving joint tenants**. It doesn't go through the will or the estate.
- So a joint tenant **can't leave their interest in a will.**

**The four unities** (all four must exist):

| Unity | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Time** | All got their interest **at the same time**. |
| **Title** | All got it from **the same document**. |
| **Interest** | All hold the **same kind and size** of interest. |
| **Possession** | Each is entitled to possess **the whole**. |

> 🧠 **Memory trick: "T-TIP."** **T**ime, **T**itle, **I**nterest, **P**ossession.

> **Example.** Abraham transfers half of his land to his son Isaac during his life. When Abraham dies, his will leaves the other half to his son David. Isaac and David hold identical half-interests, but they got them **at different times** from **different documents** → **not** joint tenants. They're tenants in common.

**The modern presumption.** Joint tenancy can produce harsh results between people who aren't close (your business partner's family gets nothing when they die). So BC law (the *Property Law Act*) **presumes a tenancy in common** unless the documents **clearly say joint tenancy**. Even a will saying "equally to my sons" creates a tenancy in common.

**Ending a joint tenancy:**

1. **Severance by operation of law**: one joint tenant **sells, mortgages** or has their share seized. The unity of title is broken for that share.
2. **Partition by agreement** of all the joint tenants.
3. **Court order** under the *Partition of Property Act*.

> **Example.** Xavier, Yasmin and Zeb are joint tenants. Xavier sells his share to Quinn. Now Yasmin and Zeb are joint tenants *between themselves* of two-thirds, and together they're **tenants in common** with Quinn, who holds one-third.

> ⚠️ **A joint tenant can sever without telling anyone**, for example by transferring their interest to themselves and registering it. The other co-owners may not know until it matters.

### Tenancy in common

- Each owner has a **separate, undivided share**. Shares **can be unequal** (e.g., 50% / 25% / 25%).
- Only **one unity** is required: **possession** (each can use the whole property).
- **No right of survivorship**: each owner can sell, mortgage or **leave their share in a will** to anyone.
- A sale by one owner doesn't end the tenancy in common; the buyer simply steps in.
- The usual choice for **business partners and investors**.

### What this means for you

- **All co-owners must sign** the listing and the contract. One sibling can't sell the whole property.
- **Court-ordered sales are real.** Under *Partition of Property Act* s.6, a co-owner (or group) holding **at least half** of the interest can ask the court to order a sale, and the court **must** order it unless there's a good reason not to. Co-owners with less than half can also apply, with less certainty. In *ter Borg v. Morris* (BC, 2012), two friends who'd bought as tenants in common couldn't agree on a buyout, and the court ordered the property sold.
- Recommend that co-owners have a **written co-ownership agreement** (drafted by a lawyer) covering how and when they can sell, and what happens if one wants out.

**Back to Marco:** the seller and her brother must **both** sign. If they're tenants in common, check whether either share is mortgaged or has a judgment against it.

## Torts that affect land

Lesson 1 defined a **tort** as a private wrong, other than a breach of contract, for which a court gives a remedy. Three matter here.

### Trespass

Trespass is:

1. **wrongfully entering** someone else's land,
2. **remaining** there after being told to leave, or
3. **placing something** on it (including in the airspace or underground: a crane boom swinging over, an anchor rod under the lot line).

**Remedies:** damages; an **injunction** (an order to stop); and **self-help**: the person in possession can remove a trespasser, but **only after asking them to leave**, and using **no more force than reasonably necessary**.

**Lawful excuses** (not trespass): permission (express or implied, like walking up to a front door), statutory authority (a meter reader), or exercising an easement.

### Private nuisance

**Substantial and unreasonable** interference with someone's **use and enjoyment** of their land. "Substantial" means more than trivial. Whether it's "unreasonable" depends on:

- the **nature of the harm**,
- the **character of the neighbourhood** (noise in an industrial park vs a quiet residential street),
- the **sensitivity** of the person complaining,
- the **defendant's conduct**, and
- **how often and how long** it happens.

Typical nuisances: smoke, smells, fumes, noise, vibration, heat, flooding, escaping animals.

Two kinds of harm:

- **Loss of amenity** (can't enjoy the property, little physical damage): the court looks closely at whether it's unreasonable.
- **Material injury** (physical damage): much easier to prove.

**Remedies:** damages, injunction, and **abatement**: stopping the nuisance yourself (cutting off an overhanging dead branch, removing a wasps' nest). Abatement must be **reasonable**, must **not endanger** anyone or their property, and normally requires **notice** first, unless it's an emergency.

> **Case.** *Segal v. Derrick Golf & Winter Club* (Alberta, 1977). A family's backyard backed onto a golf course, and so many balls landed in it that they couldn't use the yard in golf season. The club wasn't liable for **trespass** (the members hit the balls, not the club), but it **was** liable in **nuisance** for failing to take reasonable steps to stop it. The family got **damages and an injunction**.

> 🏢 **Commercial angle.** Nuisance is everyday life for industrial and mixed-use property. A new residential building next to an existing plant, brewery or nightclub is a nuisance claim waiting to happen. When you sell either property, ask about complaints and any history of disputes.

### Occupiers' liability

Who's responsible when someone is hurt on a property? In BC, the **Occupiers Liability Act** (1974) replaced the old common law categories (invitee, licensee, trespasser, child), which had become confusing.

**Who's an "occupier"?** Anyone in **physical possession** of the premises, or who has **responsibility for and control over** their condition, activities, or who's allowed in. **There can be more than one.** Owners, tenants, house-sitters, property managers, strata corporations **and real estate licensees running an open house** can all be occupiers.

**The duty (s.3):** take reasonable care, in all the circumstances, that people and their property will be **reasonably safe** on the premises. It covers the property's condition, activities on it, and the conduct of third parties.

**Lower duty for risks people willingly accept (s.3(3)):** the occupier only has to avoid **intentionally harming** them or acting with **reckless disregard**. This applies, for example, to a trespasser who's committing a crime, and to people on certain rural or recreational premises without paying.

**Higher duties still apply (s.3(4)):** if another statute imposes a stricter standard (the *Residential Tenancy Act*, the *Workers Compensation Act*), the stricter one wins.

> 🚨 **Open house = you are the occupier.** Before you open the door: secure the loose stair tread, turn on the lights, flag the icy step, keep pets away, lock up medications and valuables. If a visitor falls, you can be one of the people sued.

## Two alerts

> 🚨 **ALERT 1: Don't call it "clear title" unless it is.** Easements, covenants, building schemes, life estates and statutory rights of way are all charges. If they'll still be on title after completion, the listing, the ad and the contract must reflect them.

> 🚨 **ALERT 2: Write down what's included.** Fixtures-vs-chattels disputes are avoidable. Walk through, ask, and put every doubtful item in the contract.

## Trap list: things the exam likes to twist

1. **Fee simple is not absolute ownership.** It's subject to expropriation, tax sale, land-use regulation and other people's rights.
2. **Life tenant pays interest; remainderman pays principal and insurance.** Not the other way round.
3. **Permissive waste**: the life tenant is generally **not** liable. Voluntary and equitable waste: liable.
4. **"Without impeachment for waste"** excuses the three **common law** types, **not equitable** waste (unless expressly stated).
5. **Reversioner** = the property comes back to the grantor. **Remainderman** = it goes to a third party.
6. **Estate *pur autre vie*** ends on the death of the **original life tenant**, not the buyer.
7. **Short leases (≤ 3 years) with occupation bind a buyer even if unregistered** (LTA s.23).
8. **Prescriptive easements are abolished in BC.** Long use doesn't create an easement here.
9. **Statutory rights of way don't need a dominant tenement.** Ordinary easements do.
10. **Profits à prendre can't be implied**; easements can.
11. **Positive covenants don't usually run with the land**, but a buyer can become bound by conduct (*Crystal Square*).
12. **A licence is not an interest in land**, can't be registered and doesn't bind a buyer.
13. **Natural right of support covers land, not buildings.**
14. **Water licences aren't in the land title office**, and unpaid fees go with the land.
15. **Slightly attached → presumed fixture**; not attached → presumed chattel. Both presumptions can be rebutted.
16. **BC presumes tenancy in common** unless joint tenancy is clearly stated.
17. **Four unities** are needed for joint tenancy; **only possession** for tenancy in common.
18. **A joint tenant can sever without notice** to the others.
19. **A ≥ 50% co-owner** can normally get a court-ordered sale under the *Partition of Property Act*.
20. **Licensees can be occupiers** (open houses).
21. **Assessed value ≠ market value.**
22. **Mortgagor = borrower** (grants the mortgage); **mortgagee = lender**.

## Drill

Try each before opening the answer.

**Q1.** A will reads: *"To my brother Raj for life, then to my niece Anika."* (a) What does Raj hold? (b) What does Anika hold and what's she called? (c) Raj wants to sell the house. What has to happen?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

(a) A **life estate**: Raj is the **life tenant**.
(b) The **fee simple in remainder**: she's the **remainderman**. She appears on title as owner, with Raj's life estate as a charge.
(c) Raj can sell only his **life estate**, which would give the buyer an **estate pur autre vie**, ending on Raj's death. That's nearly unsellable. To sell a clean fee simple, **both Raj and Anika** must agree and sign, and the life estate must be released on completion.

</details>

**Q2.** Raj, the life tenant above, (a) lets the gutters rot, (b) demolishes the old carport, (c) adds a sunroom. For which is he liable, and would a court award damages?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

(a) **Permissive waste**: generally **not liable**.
(b) **Voluntary waste**: **liable** for the loss in value.
(c) **Ameliorating waste**: technically liable, but **usually no damages**, because the property is worth more.

If the will said "without impeachment for waste", he'd be excused from all three of these (but still liable for equitable waste, such as deliberately torching the house).

</details>

**Q3.** For 25 years, a neighbour has driven across a corner of your seller's lot to get to his shop. Nothing is registered. The buyer asks if the neighbour has a legal right to keep doing it. What's your understanding, and what do you do?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Prescription is abolished in BC**, so 25 years of use alone doesn't create an easement. An unregistered arrangement is most likely a **licence**, which doesn't bind a buyer. (An **implied** easement is possible in narrow cases, such as true necessity.)

What you do: **search title** to confirm nothing's registered, **ask the seller** how the arrangement started, and recommend the buyer get **legal advice** before relying on it. Don't give the legal opinion yourself.

</details>

**Q4.** A retail strip mall's title shows a restrictive covenant from 1974 prohibiting "any building over one storey" for the benefit of a neighbouring house lot that is now a 12-storey office tower. Your buyer wants to add a second storey. What's the tool, and what's your role?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Property Law Act s.35**: the BC Supreme Court can **cancel or modify** a covenant that's **obsolete** because of changes in the neighbourhood, or that **blocks reasonable use** without practical benefit to anyone. A one-storey limit protecting a house that's been replaced by an office tower is a strong candidate.

Your role: **spot it** and **refer the buyer to a lawyer**. Make the offer **subject to** the buyer's legal review or a satisfactory resolution, and don't describe the property as having "clear title".

</details>

**Q5.** A seller lists a restaurant property. The commercial-grade walk-in cooler is bolted to the floor and wired in; the stand-alone ice machine is plugged into the wall. A buyer assumes both are included. How does the law likely see each, and how should you have prevented the argument?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Ice machine**: not attached except by a plug, so presumed a **chattel**. The seller can take it unless the contract says otherwise.

**Walk-in cooler**: attached (bolted and hard-wired), so presumed a **fixture**. The seller could argue it was attached only for the better use of the cooler and is designed to be removable, and courts have gone both ways on equipment like this.

**Prevention:** at listing, walk through and list every piece of equipment as **included or excluded**, and put it in the contract. For a restaurant, attach a full equipment schedule.

</details>

**Q6.** Three investors bought a warehouse as "joint tenants". One of them, Lena, mortgages her interest to fund another project and then dies. Who gets her interest?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Mortgaging her interest **severed** the joint tenancy as to her share (it broke the unity of title). So Lena's one-third became a **tenancy in common** interest, which passes under **her will** (or to her estate), **subject to the mortgage**. The other two remain joint tenants between themselves of the other two-thirds.

Practical lesson: investors usually choose **tenancy in common** with a written co-ownership agreement precisely to avoid surprises like this.

</details>

**Q7 (applied).** Go back to Marco and the Abbotsford warehouse. List what you'd check before Marco writes an offer, for each of the four issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

1. **Trucks crossing the corner**: search title for a registered **easement** or **SRW**. If none, ask the seller how the arrangement started; it's likely a **licence** Marco could end. Flag it either way.
2. **Handshake tenant**: a lease of **≤ 3 years with occupation binds Marco** even unregistered (LTA s.23). Get the terms in writing from the seller and tenant (rent, term, renewal options, deposit), and deal with the tenancy in the contract.
3. **Pallet racking**: decide with the seller whether it's **included or excluded**, and write it into the contract.
4. **Two names on title**: confirm whether they're **joint tenants or tenants in common**, and make sure **both** sign the listing and the contract. Check for mortgages or judgments against either share.

Plus: recommend Marco get **legal advice** on title before removing conditions.

</details>

## Mindset: read the title like a story

New licensees tend to treat the title search as paperwork to attach to a listing. The best ones read it like a **story about the property**: who holds which sticks in the bundle, who gave them away, and what promises came along with it.

Every charge on a title is a past event:

- a **right of way** means someone once needed to cross;
- a **restrictive covenant** means someone once paid to protect a view or a neighbourhood;
- a **life estate** means a family made a plan for a surviving spouse;
- a **building scheme** means a developer wanted a certain look and feel.

When you can read those events, you can predict the questions a buyer, a lender and a lawyer will ask, and you can answer them **before** they become problems at completion.

> 🏢 **For commercial specifically.** In IC&I deals, the money is often in the **charges**. A covenant restricting use can cut a site's value in half; an easement can make a landlocked parcel developable; a long lease with a strong tenant *is* the asset. Commercial clients will judge you on whether you saw these things first.

> **This week's habit.** Whenever you see a property (a listing, a building on your commute), ask: *"Who might hold a stick in this bundle besides the owner?"* A tenant, a lender, a utility, a neighbour, the Crown. You'll start seeing titles before you've even searched them.
