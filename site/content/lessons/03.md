## Why the title search is your first move, not your last

> **Scenario.** An email arrives from "Mr. Leung", who says he lives in Hong Kong and owns a retail strata unit in a Richmond shopping plaza. He wants it listed **this week**, priced about 15% below market, with **no sign and no online advertising**. "Quiet sale, cash buyers only." He sends a **scanned** passport and a power of attorney naming his "cousin" in Vancouver to sign everything. His phone number is different from the one in your brokerage's old file.
>
> You pull the title. It shows Mr. Leung as registered owner, a bank mortgage, a **claim of builders lien** filed six weeks ago by a flooring contractor, and a **certificate of pending litigation**.

Stop and count the problems:

| What you see | Why it matters | Section |
|---|---|---|
| Rush, below-market, no marketing, scanned ID, power of attorney, new phone number | Classic **title fraud** red flags | Title fraud |
| A registered owner's name on title | In BC that name is **guaranteed**, which is exactly why fraudsters want to impersonate it | Torrens and indefeasibility |
| A mortgage, a lien and a CPL | Each is a **charge** that must be dealt with before the buyer gets the title the contract promises | Common items on title |
| It's a strata unit | You also need the **strata plan**, amendments and common property resolutions | Strata |

If you'd skipped the search and signed a listing, you'd be marketing a property that might not belong to your "client", with three encumbrances you couldn't explain. This lesson is about how BC records who owns what, and how to read that record like a professional.

## Vocabulary first

| Word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| **Subdivision** | Dividing one parcel into two or more. Even selling a 3 m strip to your neighbour is a subdivision. |
| **Approving officer** | The local official who decides whether a subdivision can go ahead. |
| **Strata / condominium** | The same thing, legally. Individually owned **strata lots** plus shared **common property**. |
| **Strata plan** | The plan filed at the land title office that creates the strata lots and common property. |
| **Title / certificate of title** | The official record of who owns a parcel in fee simple and what charges affect it. |
| **Torrens system** | BC's land registration system: the register itself *is* the proof of ownership. |
| **Indefeasible** | Can't be defeated or overturned. In BC, a registered fee simple owner's title is (with exceptions) indefeasible. |
| **Charge** | Any registered interest less than a fee simple: mortgage, easement, lease, lien, judgment, covenant. |
| **Encumbrance** | A claim or burden on title. In practice, used interchangeably with "charge". |
| **PID** | Parcel identifier: a permanent 9-digit number for each titled parcel. |
| **LTSA** | Land Title and Survey Authority of BC, which runs the land title system. |
| **myLTSA** | LTSA's online service for searching titles and documents. |
| **Assurance fund** | A fund that compensates people who lose land because of how the Torrens system works. |
| **Bona fide purchaser for value** | A buyer acting honestly (*in good faith*) who pays a real price. The Torrens system protects them. |

## Who owns the land in BC?

Five categories:

| Category | Share of BC | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **Provincial Crown land** | ~**94%** | Defined in the *Land Act*. Tracked in a system called **Tantalis**, in parcels called **district lots**. The province can grant leases, licences, rights of way or fee simple. |
| **Federal Crown land** | ~**1%** | Reserves, national parks, defence lands, federal harbours. |
| **Private land** | ~**5%** | Granted by the Crown in fee simple. Small overall, but most of the land in places like Metro Vancouver. |
| **Treaty settlement lands** | small | Nisga'a, Tsawwassen, Maa-nulth, Tla'amin: owned by the Nation, often in fee simple. |
| **Aboriginal title lands** | growing | Declared by courts or recognized by agreement. |

> 🔎 **Wait: only 5% private?** Yes. Almost everything you'll ever list or sell sits in that thin slice. The rest is forests, mountains, parks and Crown leases.

**Reserves.** Owned by the federal Crown for First Nations' use. Individual members may hold an **allotment** (a right to use a particular piece). Reserves **aren't under the Land Title Act** and generally aren't subdivided.

**First Nations as owners in the provincial system.** First Nations have long held fee simple land in the provincial system through corporations, societies or trustees. Since **21 May 2024**, a First Nation recognized as a legal entity under federal law (such as an Indian Act band) can **hold and register land in its own name**, like any person or company. This came from amendments made to align BC law with the *Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act* (DRIPA).

**Treaty settlement lands.** BC never made historical treaties for most of the province, so it's now negotiating modern ones. Four are in effect. Any deal on First Nations land can involve several different legal regimes, so **encourage the parties to get legal advice early**.

**Aboriginal title.** Confirmed by the Supreme Court of Canada in ***Tsilhqot'in Nation v. British Columbia* (2014)**, which declared Aboriginal title over more than **1,700 km²** in BC's interior. To prove title, a group must show **regular and exclusive** use of the land **before Crown sovereignty**, and if relying on present-day use as evidence, **continuity** from then to now. Once title is established, others need the title holders' **consent** to use the land. Without consent, government can only **justify** an infringement by consulting, showing a compelling and substantial public objective, and respecting its fiduciary duty.

In **2025**, Canada signed the **Haida Title Lands Agreement**, recognizing Haida Aboriginal title on Haida Gwaii: the first time the federal government recognized Aboriginal title **through negotiation** rather than a court order.

## Subdividing land

**Subdivision** means creating smaller parcels from a larger one. A developer carving 50 lots from a farm and a homeowner selling the back 3 metres of their yard to a neighbour are both subdividing.

- At common law, an owner could subdivide freely. **In BC that right is gone.** **LTA s.73**: you can't subdivide except in compliance with the Act. (Small exceptions exist, such as **leases of less than 3 years** of part of a parcel.)
- An **approving officer** decides whether the subdivision is in the **public interest**. If they act in **good faith, on a factual basis and without discrimination**, their decision is largely final.
- The subdivider must meet local bylaws and may have to **dedicate land for roads or parks** and **install services** at its own cost.
- The land title office won't deposit a subdivision plan without the approving officer's sign-off.

Three ways to subdivide:

1. **"Traditional"** two-dimensional subdivision: one lot into two, or two lots reconfigured into three.
2. **Air space** subdivision: carving out three-dimensional parcels of space (Lesson 2).
3. **Strata** subdivision: below.

> 🏢 **Commercial angle.** Development sites and land assemblies are a major IC&I segment. A site's value depends heavily on whether, and how cheaply, it can be subdivided or stratified: road dedications, park requirements and servicing costs can swing a land price by millions. Developers will expect you to understand the approval path.

## Strata: owning part of a building (or part of a site)

### The concept

A strata development lets people **own separate parts** of a property while **sharing ownership of the common property**.

- The privately owned parts are **strata lots** (informally "units").
- Everything else is **common property**: hallways, roof, exterior, land, parking ramps, amenities.
- **"Strata" and "condominium" mean the same thing.** "**Townhouse**" has **no legal meaning**; it's a marketing word. If a strata plan has been filed, it's a strata, whatever it looks like.
- Each strata owner owns their lot in fee simple **plus** a share of the common property as a **tenant in common** with the other owners. The size of that share comes from the **Schedule of Unit Entitlement**, which is also generally used to split the strata's expenses.
- For property tax, each strata lot (with its share of common property) is assessed as a **separate parcel**.

### Four kinds of strata development

**1. Subdividing a building.** The strata lots are carved out of the **building**, not the land. The **land itself becomes common property**. A 20-storey tower might become 180 strata lots plus common property.

**Conversions.** Turning an existing building (apartments, offices, a warehouse) into strata lots. The developer needs approval from the local council or regional board, which must consider, among other things:

- the **priority of rental housing** over owned housing in the area,
- plans for **relocating current occupants**, and
- the building's **life expectancy**.

If there are residential tenants, the developer must also follow the *Residential Tenancy Act* to end their tenancies. Some municipalities have an **effective ban** on converting rental buildings because rental housing is so scarce.

**2. Bare land strata.** The **land** is divided into strata lots, with or without buildings. Typical example: a five-acre parcel split into house lots with a **private road as common property**.

- Looks just like a traditional subdivision from the street, **but the owners own and pay for the road**, not the municipality.
- Often has a **building scheme** for consistent design (Lesson 2).
- Used for **manufactured home parks** (each lot a concrete pad; services and roads as common property) and **recreational developments** (cottages around shared amenities).
- Also used for **commercial and industrial** parks.

**3. Air space strata.** The developer files an **air space plan** to separate, say, the commercial podium from the residential tower, and then files a separate strata plan in each. Result: two separate strata corporations in one building. Expect **many easements** (support, elevators, pipes, shared facilities) and a **long list of charges** on title.

**4. Mixed-use strata.** Residential and commercial lots in **one** strata corporation, with **no air space plan** separating them.

> 🏢 **Commercial angle.** Industrial and office strata units are a growing market, especially for small business owners who'd rather own than lease. These buyers face the same strata issues as condo buyers (bylaws, special levies, contingency reserves) plus commercial ones (permitted uses, loading access, signage). And in an air space development, a commercial buyer may be in a different strata corporation from the residents above.

### Three principles that apply to every strata

**1. "A strata is a strata is a strata."** The same law governs a 500-unit tower, a 100-unit industrial complex, a 20-lot bare land strata and a **two-unit duplex**.

> 🚨 **ALERT: duplexes are stratas too.** In *Whiting v. Peters* (CRT, 2021), the two owners of a duplex strata kept suing each other over meetings, financial statements and repair costs. It was their **third** CRT decision. A two-unit strata has all the legal duties of a big one, and no buffer of other owners to outvote a difficult neighbour. Explain this to buyers.

**2. Self-enforcing.** There are **no "strata police"**. If the strata corporation isn't following the law, the **owners** must act: by majority vote at an annual or special general meeting, then if necessary through the **Civil Resolution Tribunal** (usually the preferred forum, under *CRTA* s.121) or the **BC Supreme Court** (*Strata Property Act* s.165).

**3. Not on reserves.** Reserve land is federal, so the provincial *Strata Property Act* **doesn't apply** there. Developments on reserves that *look* like condos are usually built on **long-term leases** with strata-like terms in the contracts. One exception: certain bodies (for example the **Sechelt Indian Band** and **Musqueam Block F Land Ltd.**) are designated so that they can act as leasehold landlords of real leasehold stratas. On **treaty** lands, which are no longer reserves, the *Strata Property Act* **does** apply.

### How a strata is created

The **owner developer** deposits a **strata plan** at the land title office. For a building strata, this happens near the **end of construction**, because the plan must match the actual building.

**Every strata plan must show:**

- a **unique number** (e.g. "Strata Plan VR 150"),
- the **boundaries of the land**,
- the location of **buildings** (except bare land plans),
- strata lots **numbered or lettered consecutively**, and
- each strata lot's **area in square metres**.

A **bare land strata plan** must say so beside the approving officer's signature. That's the quick way to tell which type you're looking at.

**Filed with the plan** (each separately registered):

- the **Schedule of Unit Entitlement**,
- a **Schedule of Voting Rights**, if there's at least one **non-residential** strata lot, and
- any **bylaws that differ** from the Standard Bylaws.

(Older stratas created before the *Strata Property Act* have these schedules printed on the plan itself.)

Once the plan is deposited, the registrar issues a **separate title for each strata lot**.

> 🔑 **BCFSA expectation.** When you list a strata lot, search its **title** *and* review the **strata plan**, any **amendments**, and any **resolutions affecting common property**. You can get all of these through **myLTSA**.

### Freehold vs leasehold strata

| | Freehold | Leasehold |
|---|---|---|
| Developer holds | The land in **fee simple** | A **ground lease** from a leasehold landlord |
| Buyers get | **Fee simple** strata lots | A **lease** of their strata lot (an assignment of part of the developer's lease) |
| Who can be the landlord | n/a | Only **governments, certain First Nations, universities** and similar designated bodies |
| Minimum term | n/a | Ground lease of at least **50 years** |
| At the end | n/a | The landlord either **renews** or, if it ends the lease, **must buy** the tenant's interest |

Leasehold buyers may pay rent over the term or a lump sum up front. Leasehold stratas get fuller treatment in Lesson 11.

### Where does my unit end?

**Strata Property Act s.68**: in a building strata, a lot's boundary is the **centre** of each wall, floor and ceiling that separates it from another lot, the common property, or another parcel. In a bare land strata, the boundaries are as shown on the plan.

## Before Torrens: why proving ownership used to be a nightmare

Under the old common law system, you proved ownership by producing the **chain of title**: every deed and document affecting the land for decades back. Every buyer's lawyer had to check every link.

**The void deed problem.** Some deeds look real but have **no legal effect**:

- a **forged** deed,
- a deed given for an **illegal** purpose, or
- a deed the signer can disown as ***non est factum*** ("not my deed"), for example because they were tricked about what they were signing.

The rule was ***nemo dat quod non habet***: **you can't give what you don't have.** One void deed broke the chain, and **every later transfer failed too**, however honest the buyers.

> **Example under the old rules.** Mike goes travelling. Dave forges a deed from Mike to himself and sells to Scott, who knows nothing. The land is resold to Leslie, then Adele, then Deborah. Mike returns, proves the forgery and **gets his land back**. Deborah has to sue Adele, who sues Leslie, who sues Scott, who sues Dave, who has vanished.

A buyer could only feel secure after holding the land for **20 years** (the limitation period for void deed claims).

**Deeds recording systems** (filing deeds at a public office) helped keep documents safe but **didn't fix** void deeds: a forged deed was still void even after filing. Some Canadian provinces still use recording systems, and buyers there rely heavily on **title insurance**.

> ✅ **As a licensee:** always ask for **government photo ID** from sellers, and complete **CREA's Individual / Corporation-Entity Identification Record** to meet your obligations under the federal anti-money-laundering law (the PCMLTFA). It's not foolproof, but it's a key defence.

## The Torrens system: the register *is* the truth

In 1858, **Robert Richard Torrens**, a customs officer in South Australia, asked a simple question: ships are bought and sold by recording ownership in a central register, so why not land? Under his system, you don't register *documents*; you register **title itself**.

The heart of it is the **certificate of title**: owner's name, legal description, and the charges affecting the land. The system aims to guarantee three things:

1. The title is an **accurate** reflection of the state of title.
2. The title is the **only place you need to look**.
3. A good-faith buyer for value gets an **indefeasible** fee simple.

> **Same example under Torrens.** Scott honestly paid market value and registered. His title is **guaranteed**, and so is everyone's after him. Deborah keeps the land. Mike's remedy is money from the **assurance fund**.

BC's version lives in the **Land Title Act**. It rests on **four pillars**:

1. **Indefeasibility**
2. **Effect of registration**
3. **Abolition of the doctrine of notice**
4. **The assurance fund**

### Pillar 1: Indefeasibility

If you're shown on the title as the fee simple owner, that's **conclusive proof** against the world. A **good-faith purchaser for valuable consideration** who registers keeps the land **even if the seller got title by fraud**.

But fraudsters themselves get **no protection**, and neither does anyone who didn't pay real value.

> **Worked examples.** Anton owns a property worth $100,000. Marta forges a transfer to herself and registers it.
>
> | What happens next | Who gets the land? | Why |
> |---|---|---|
> | Marta still holds it | **Anton** | Fraud by the registered owner is an exception (LTA s.23). |
> | Marta **gives** it to Clara, who knows nothing | **Anton** | A gift isn't **valuable consideration** (LTA s.25.1). |
> | Marta sells it to Clara for **$1** | **Anton** | $1 isn't valuable consideration either. |
> | Marta sells to Clara at **market value**; Clara is honest and registers | **Clara** | Good faith + value + registration. **Anton claims from the assurance fund.** |

**The 10 exceptions (LTA s.23).** Even a registered fee simple is subject to:

1. **Reservations in the original Crown grant** (minerals, for example).
2. **Federal or provincial taxes**, rates or assessments.
3. **Municipal charges**, rates or assessments.
4. **Leases of 3 years or less** where the tenant is in **actual occupation**.
5. **Highways, public rights of way, watercourses**, public easements.
6. **Expropriation** and **escheat**.
7. Any **caution, caveat, charge, builders lien, judgment, CPL** or similar **noted on title**.
8. A claim that the land was included in the title by a **wrong description of boundaries**.
9. **Fraud, including forgery, by the registered owner.**
10. A **restrictive condition under the Forest Act** endorsed on title.

> 🧠 **Focus on the three that trip up deals most often:** **unpaid taxes**, **short-term tenants** and **Crown mineral reservations** all bind a buyer **without appearing on title**.

> **Examples.**
> - Title shows no charges, but the province asserts its **reserved mineral rights**. The province wins: exception 1.
> - A tenant is living in the house under an unregistered **2-year lease**. The buyer must honour it: exception 4. Always ask about tenants, and check the **Possession** and **Title** clauses of the contract.

### Charges are NOT guaranteed

Here's where BC differs from some other Torrens systems: **indefeasibility protects the fee simple owner only, not charge holders.** Registering a mortgage, easement or lien raises only a **rebuttable presumption** that it's valid (LTA s.26). The registrar doesn't decide whether it's enforceable.

> **Example.** Arthur owns a property. Bart forges a transfer to himself, registers it, and borrows $50,000 from Carla on a registered mortgage. Arthur gets his property back **free of Carla's mortgage**. Carla isn't a fee simple purchaser, so indefeasibility doesn't protect her, and because at common law she'd have received nothing anyway, she **can't claim from the assurance fund** either.

> **Case.** ***Gill v. Bucholtz* (BC Court of Appeal, 2009).** A fraudster transferred an owner's property to himself and mortgaged it to innocent lenders. The court restored the owner's title, **discharged the mortgages**, and held the lenders couldn't claim from the fund: **lenders should bear the cost of fraud, not the public.** Lesson for lenders: verify the borrower's title yourself.

### Pillar 2: Effect of registration

At common law, a deed transferred title as soon as it was **signed, sealed and delivered**. Under Torrens, **a transfer doesn't pass an interest until it's registered** (**LTA s.20(1)**), except as between the parties to it.

- An **unregistered** transfer or mortgage is still enforceable **against the person who granted it**.
- But it **doesn't bind honest third parties** who register first.
- Registration isn't mandatory, but **not registering is a gamble**.

> **Example.** Alan mortgages his building to Royal Bank on December 1, and then to CIBC on December 4. CIBC registers; Royal Bank doesn't. **CIBC has first priority**, even though Royal Bank's mortgage is older. Royal Bank can still sue **Alan** for the money it lent.

### Pillar 3: No doctrine of notice

At common law, a buyer was **presumed to know** about any interest they could have discovered by reasonable inspection and inquiry, even if it wasn't recorded, and took the land subject to it. That's the **doctrine of notice**.

**LTA s.29 abolishes it**: a buyer dealing with the registered owner only has to worry about **what's on title** (plus the s.23 exceptions).

**But not if the buyer is part of a fraud.** Courts have held that **merely knowing** about an unregistered interest isn't fraud. Knowledge **combined with dishonest conduct** is. And the key moment is **when the contract is formed**.

> **Examples.**
> - Anna sells to Patrick, who doesn't register. Anna then sells again to Mark, who pays market value and **doesn't know** about Patrick. After paying a big deposit, Mark finds out, then completes and registers. **Mark likely keeps the land**: he learned of Patrick's interest only **after** contracting.
> - ***Woodwest Developments* (BCSC, 1982)**: before making his offer, a buyer was told about an **unregistered lease longer than 3 years**, was given an unsigned copy, didn't bother getting a signed one, and right after buying tried to evict the tenant under s.29. **That was fraud.** He knew **before** contracting and bought in order to defeat the lease.

### Pillar 4: The assurance fund

When the Torrens system protects an innocent buyer, someone else (the original owner) loses. The **assurance fund** compensates them. Since **January 2005** it's been administered by the **LTSA**.

**Purpose 1: compensating people who lost land because of indefeasibility.** The claimant must prove all three:

1. They **lost an estate or interest** because someone else was registered as owner;
2. **Without the Land Title Act**, they'd have **recovered it** at common law; and
3. They **can't recover** it (or compensation) by suing anyone else.

**It's a last resort.** If you can sue the fraudster and collect, you must. The fund pays only if the wrongdoer is **dead, gone, or has no money** ("judgment proof"), and the fund can then go after the wrongdoer itself.

**No compensation for your own fault (LTA s.294.6(f)).** If the loss was caused or contributed to by the claimant's own **act, neglect or default** (for example, failing to register their interest), the fund won't pay that share.

**Purpose 2: errors by the land title office.** The fund also compensates for losses caused by mistakes or misconduct of the registrar or staff.

> ⚠️ **Plans are not guaranteed.** The accuracy of a registered plan (boundaries, dimensions) is **not** guaranteed by the land title office. A boundary error on a plan **can't** be claimed from the fund. That's one reason buyers get **surveys** or **title insurance**.

Claims are usually brought by suing with the LTSA as a defendant, but the LTSA can also settle claims directly.

> **Example.** Harold forges a transfer of Nick's property, registers it, and sells to Mac, an honest buyer at market value. **Mac keeps the land.** Nick can claim from the fund if Harold has disappeared or has no money: Nick lost the land, can't recover it from Mac, and would have recovered it at common law.

## Trusts, forms and the documents behind the title

### Trusts on title (LTA s.180)

Sometimes the registered owner isn't the real ("beneficial") owner. A child can't hold land, so an adult **trustee** holds it for them; an **executor** holds a deceased person's land for the heirs.

- A **pure** Torrens register shows no trusts. BC allows title to be registered in a trustee's or personal representative's name, but **no details** of the trust go on the register.
- Registering the trust isn't mandatory. If it is noted, searchers can see the owner is a trustee, but they must get the **trust document** from the land title office to know its terms.

> ✅ **If title shows "in trust" or an executor**, find out who has authority to sell, and get legal advice before the listing is signed.

### Prescribed forms

- A fee simple is transferred by a **Form A Freehold Transfer** (what other places call a "deed"). The **contract of purchase and sale is not registered**; only the Form A is.
- A lawyer or notary usually prepares the Form A, though that's not a legal requirement. It must be in writing and signed by the seller.
- **Mortgages** use a standard short form that refers to a set of **standard terms** filed once at the land title office, instead of filing 10+ pages every time.

> ⚠️ **Need to know if a mortgage is assumable, or has a prepayment penalty?** The registered short form won't tell you. **Get the full mortgage** (including the filed standard terms), not just the first page.

## Title fraud: how it happens and how to stop it

**Title transfer fraud** is stealing a property by impersonating the owner. In two BC cases, fraudsters:

- persuaded **rental property managers** to take instructions from new phone numbers and emails, and to share documents that helped with the impersonation;
- got **trading services licensees** to list the homes on the strength of **scanned forged passports**; and
- in one case, fooled a **lawyer** too, and the title was transferred away from the real owner.

**Money transfer fraud** is the cousin: hacked email accounts send **fake payment instructions** to lawyers or brokerages. Brokerages hold large trust deposits, so expect to be targeted.

**Red flags:**

- 📞 **Changed contact details**, especially from owners living abroad.
- ⏰ **Urgency or odd listing requests**: well below market, no online listing, no sign.
- 🪪 **Brand-new or scanned ID documents.** Always insist on seeing **originals**.
- ✍️ **A power of attorney** being used to list and sell. Recommend that buyers get legal advice, and if you act for a seller through an attorney, try to confirm directly with the actual owner.

**Prevention:**

- **Know your client**: verify identity **early** (a PCMLTFA requirement; Lesson 6). Search the **title** and the **Land Owner Transparency Registry** to check the seller's claims. Ask conversational questions: does the "owner" know the property? Do the documents hang together?
- **Verify instructions** in person or by phone **using a number you already trust**, never one supplied in the email or text giving the instruction.
- **Set a password** with the client when you open the file, record it in the physical file, and require it before acting on listing, transfer or payment instructions.

> 🚨 Once title passes to an innocent buyer, **the true owner can't get the property back**. Their remedies are title insurance, the assurance fund, and suing the professionals involved: **licensees, notaries and lawyers.** You are one of the last lines of defence.

**Back to Mr. Leung:** every red flag is present. Insist on meeting in person or by verified video with **original** ID, contact the owner through the number on your existing file, ask your managing broker, and don't sign a listing until you're satisfied.

## Searching title

### Where titles live

- **7 land title districts** (Kamloops, Nelson, New Westminster, Prince George, Prince Rupert, Victoria, Vancouver), served by **3 offices** (New Westminster, Kamloops, Victoria).
- Since **January 2005**, the **LTSA** has run the system.
- Each parcel has its own **certificate of title**, now kept digitally. The **fee simple owner** is at the top; **charges** are listed below. Abbreviations for charges are explained on the title.

### How to search

- Use **myLTSA** (formerly BC OnLine). Small fee per search; available early morning to late evening every day except shortened Sunday hours. **There's no excuse for not searching.**
- Search by **PID** or **legal description**. The **PID** is a **9-digit** number that never changes unless the parcel is subdivided or consolidated. It's the best way to search.
- Find the PID from **BC Assessment's eValue BC** site, a **municipal GIS map**, or the **property tax notice**.

### Why you must search, early

> 🚨 **A licensee who doesn't review title** risks liability to buyer and seller, **loss of commission**, and **BCFSA discipline**. A real estate board won't accept an MLS® listing without a title search.

> **Case.** In *Chand v. Sabo Bros. Realty* (Alberta CA, 1979), an agent who never searched title was held liable to a buyer for a contract that turned out to be unenforceable. The court had little sympathy: a search is simple and an elementary precaution.

An early search tells you:

- whether the person who called you **is actually the owner**, or one of several;
- whether title is held by an **executor**;
- whether the property is **in foreclosure** with a lender controlling the sale; and
- whether there are **charges** that will stop the seller delivering the title the contract promises.

### What the standard contract promises

The standard BC contract of purchase and sale promises title **free and clear of all encumbrances**, **except**:

- conditions and reservations in the **original Crown grant**,
- registered or pending **restrictive covenants and rights of way in favour of utilities and public authorities**,
- **existing tenancies** listed in the contract, and
- anything else the contract expressly says.

**Everything else** (a private easement to a neighbour, a building scheme, a lien, a judgment) must either be **discharged before completion**, or the buyer must **expressly agree** to take title subject to it. If the seller can't deliver what the contract promises, the buyer may be able to **terminate** for breach of a fundamental term, and a seller who loses the sale may sue **you and your brokerage**.

> 🚨 **Discipline case (BC, 2016).** A licensee attached an **incomplete** title search, supplied by the seller, to a contract for rural land. The missing page listed **seven charges** restricting building, and the licensee knew the buyers wanted to build. Result: found in breach of the duty to discover relevant facts and the duty of reasonable care; **14-day suspension**, remedial course at her own cost, and **$1,500** in enforcement costs. **Get the full title yourself.**

> 🔎 **Not every property is in the provincial system.** Most **reserve** lands are recorded in **federal** registries, and some First Nations run their own systems. See "First Nations land registration" below.

## Common items you'll see on title

### Duplicate certificate of title

A holdover from pre-Torrens days, when owners gave lenders their title deeds as security. Since **1979**, duplicates are issued only on the owner's written request. The title will say **"None Outstanding"** if none exists.

- Can't be issued if there's a **mortgage** or agreement for sale on title.
- If a duplicate is **out**, the registrar **won't register a transfer, mortgage or long-term lease** until it's returned. (Short leases, easements, CPLs and liens can still be registered.)
- A lost duplicate can be replaced (LTA s.193), but it **takes time**.

> ✅ **Check for an outstanding duplicate before the contract is written.** If one is out, find out who holds it straight away. Leave it until after the offer, and the sale may fail to complete, with you potentially liable.

### Caveat

A registered warning from someone **claiming an interest** that **blocks dealings inconsistent with their claim**. It **lapses after 2 months** unless the caveator starts a lawsuit and registers a **CPL**. A caveat filed without good reason can make the caveator liable for damages.

### Certificate of pending litigation (CPL)

Notice that a **lawsuit claiming an interest in the land** has been started (formerly *lis pendens*). Today, other documents can still be registered, but only **subject to the outcome** of the lawsuit. CPLs scare off buyers and lenders.

> **Case.** *Hundal v. Border Carrier* (BCSC, 2012): a former business partner filed a CPL on the other partner's house with no proper basis, stopping him refinancing. The court found it **malicious** and awarded **$25,000** in damages.

### Builders lien

Under the *Builders Lien Act*, a **contractor, subcontractor or worker** who hasn't been paid for work or materials on an "improvement" can file a lien against the land.

- Anyone in the construction chain can file, **even subcontractors the owner never hired**.
- Filed by **Form 5** within **45 days** of certain trigger dates (often project or contract completion). Working out the exact deadline is complicated, so get legal advice.
- A lien can be filed **after the property is sold**, if still within the deadline. **Buyers of recently renovated or newly built properties** are exposed.
- A registered lien lasts **1 year**; to enforce it, the claimant must sue and register a CPL within that year. The owner can shorten this by serving a **Form 6** notice, giving the claimant **21 days** to sue.
- Owners can clear title by getting the lien dismissed, **paying money into court** in place of the property, or settling.

> 🏢 **Commercial angle.** Liens are routine in development and tenant-improvement work. On a new or recently renovated commercial property, ask about unpaid trades and recent work, and make sure the contract and lawyers deal with the lien period.

### Judgment

A court judgment can be registered against an owner's land. It **expires after 2 years** unless renewed. If you find one, talk to the owner straight away about how it'll be paid off on sale, and recommend legal advice.

### Section 219 covenant

Under **LTA s.219**, the province or a local government can require an owner to register a **covenant** controlling use, subdivision or building (often for contaminated land, environmental protection, or development conditions). Unlike common law restrictive covenants, **s.219 covenants can be positive or negative.**

### Notice of special waste (LTA s.392)

Filed by a waste management director or approving officer on **contaminated** land, describing the contamination and how long it's expected to last.

### Heritage designation

Under the *Heritage Conservation Act* or the *Local Government Act*. Restricts demolition, moving and alteration (sometimes interiors too) and appears as a **Legal Notation**. But the HCA can apply **even without** a formal designation, so title alone isn't conclusive.

### Community Charter s.57 notice

A municipal notice under **Legal Notations** warning that the property breaches bylaws or building regulations and **may be unsafe** (failed inspections, unpermitted work, an illegal suite, a former **grow-op or drug lab**). It's a last resort after repeated failed attempts to get compliance, and it's removed only when the property is fully fixed. If you see one, **contact the municipality**, and speak to your managing broker or a lawyer before going further.

## Registration: what happens on completion day

- Since **2012**, almost all documents are **filed electronically**.
- On completion, the lawyer or notary **re-searches title**, signs the Form A (and the Property Transfer Tax return) **electronically**, and files them.
- Within about **30–60 minutes**, the transfer shows on title as a **pending application**. If title is otherwise unchanged, the purchase money is paid out under the statements of adjustment.
- **Once the transfer is submitted for registration**, the seller has no further claim to title and the buyer no further claim to the deposit. The deposit in the brokerage's trust account becomes **the seller's money**, and commission is considered **earned** at that moment. If the seller has authorized it, the brokerage can pay its commission from the deposit.
- Final registration takes about **7–14 days**, after which a new title issues in the buyer's name.

## Title insurance

Three ways of allocating fraud risk:

| System | Who keeps the land after a forgery? | Who's compensated? |
|---|---|---|
| **Common law** | The **original owner** | Nobody, really. The innocent buyer sues a vanished fraudster. |
| **Torrens** | The **innocent buyer** | The original owner, from the **assurance fund** |
| **Title insurance** (e.g. many US states) | The **original owner** | The buyer, from their **insurer** |

Title insurance is **common in BC** despite Torrens, because:

1. **Indefeasibility doesn't cover charges** (lenders are exposed, as in *Gill v. Bucholtz*);
2. it covers things Torrens doesn't: **access, zoning, boundaries**, encroachments; and
3. it's often **cheaper than a survey**, so lenders now commonly accept it instead of a survey certificate.

> ⚠️ **A lender's policy protects the lender, not the owner.** An owner who wants coverage must buy an **owner's policy**.

## First Nations land registration

Most reserve lands aren't in the provincial land title system:

- **Indian Land Registry System (ILRS)**: reserve lands managed under the *Indian Act*.
- **First Nations Land Registry System (FNLRS)**: for First Nations operating under their own **land code** under the *First Nations Land Management Act*.
- **Self-Governing First Nations Land Registry (SGFNLR)**: limited use in BC (e.g. Westbank First Nation).

These are federal, web-based, and **not Torrens** systems. There are **no standard forms**, and many transactions need **consents** from the Minister or band council, which affects timelines. Get help if you're unfamiliar with them.

Treaty nations vary: **Tsawwassen** lands are in the provincial land title office (with special notations to review); the **Nisga'a** run their own Torrens-based system; **Maa-nulth** lands are split between the provincial office and separate registries.

## Contaminated sites

The Ministry of Environment's **Site Registry** (searchable through BC OnLine) records site investigations, cleanups, orders, remediation agreements and certificates of compliance under the *Environmental Management Act*.

- It's **completely separate from the land title system**. A clean title tells you nothing about contamination.
- Searching it costs more than a title search, but for **commercial and industrial** property it's essential due diligence. Site disclosure statements are far more likely to exist for commercial sites.

> 🏢 **Commercial angle.** Gas stations, dry cleaners, auto shops, old industrial lands: environmental liability can exceed the property's value. Commercial buyers will expect you to recommend a Site Registry search and, usually, an environmental site assessment by a qualified consultant.

## Manufactured homes

You can sell a manufactured home as a licensee **if an estate or interest in land is involved** (for example, the home plus the land, or a pad lease). But manufactured homes have their **own registry**, not the land title office.

### The Manufactured Home Act registry

- A **manufactured home** is any structure designed to be **moved** and used as a residence, with or without wheels.
- Registry run by **BC Registry Services**, mostly via **BC OnLine**.
- It's a **registration system, not Torrens**: **no title, no indefeasibility, no assurance fund**, just registered ownership.
- Like LTA s.20, an **unregistered transfer** doesn't bind third parties (**s.7(7)**).
- **Manufacturers, sellers and importers must register** homes; you can't import, sell or move an unregistered home.

> ✅ **Before listing**, search the registry to confirm the home is registered and that your seller is the registered owner, especially for homes built **before 2004**.

- Registration gives the home a **registration number** and **two decals**, valid for the home's life. Not keeping them affixed can mean a fine.
- A **certificate** from the registrar shows the number, make, model, location, owner and any registered security interests.

> 🚨 **ALERT: CSA sticker.** BCFSA warns that you **can't offer for sale** a manufactured home without a valid **CSA** (or equivalent) electrical certification label, or a **silver label** for electrical alterations. Don't confuse the **CSA sticker** (electrical safety, often on the panel or near the front door) with the **MHR sticker** (registration ID, like a car's VIN, usually on the front left corner).

### Moving, taxes and penalties

- **No moving an unregistered home** (s.18(1)), and moving needs a **transport permit** (s.15), valid **30 days** for one destination, issued only if **taxes are paid**.
- Fines of up to **$2,000** for moving or selling an unregistered home, or not keeping decals affixed.
- Unpaid taxes can become a lien registered in the **Personal Property Registry**, with **priority over all other security interests**.
- A home in a park, owned by someone other than the park owner, is **assessed and taxed in the home owner's name**.
- A party to a lawsuit over a home can file a **caution** (like a caveat), valid **3 months** unless extended by a court.

### Pad leases

The *Manufactured Home Park Tenancy Act* governs park owners and home owners renting pads. When a buyer wants to keep the home in the park, the seller's **pad tenancy agreement should be assigned**:

- Benefit: **cost certainty**. The buyer keeps the same rent and rent-increase terms.
- The seller must get the landlord's **prior written consent** using the prescribed request form. The landlord must reply **within 10 days** or is **deemed to consent**.
- The landlord can refuse on set grounds (e.g. the buyer can't meet the agreement or park rules); an arbitrator can order assignment if consent is unreasonably withheld.
- Moving in without a valid assignment leaves the **seller still liable** and the **buyer at risk** of losing the tenancy.

### Financing: security interests

Three ways to finance a manufactured home: (1) seller transfers ownership and takes a **security agreement** back; (2) a **conditional sale** (seller keeps legal ownership until paid; note that registered ownership passes to the buyer when the contract is filed in the registry); or (3) a **third-party lender** takes a security agreement. All are governed by the **Personal Property Security Act (PPSA)**.

- The lender registers a **financing statement** in the **Personal Property Registry**: one search shows all security interests.
- The home's **serial number** must be exact; for registered homes it's the **registration number**. A wrong number can **nullify** the security.
- If the home becomes **affixed to land**, the lender must **also register in the land title office** to protect its priority.

### Chattel or fixture?

All the Lesson 2 rules apply, and the cases go both ways:

- On blocks, wheels and hitch removed, skirting, hooked to water, power and septic → held a **chattel**.
- Steel frame removed, sitting on a **concrete basement foundation** → held a **fixture**.

The answer matters because, among other things, the **home buyer rescission period** applies to a manufactured home **affixed to land**. Get a **written agreement** on the home's status, and recommend legal advice.

## Two alerts

> 🚨 **ALERT 1: Search title yourself, early, and read every page.** Don't rely on a seller's copy or assume the conveyancer will catch it.

> 🚨 **ALERT 2: Verify identity like your licence depends on it.** In title fraud cases, it can.

## Trap list: things the exam likes to twist

1. **~94% provincial Crown, ~1% federal, ~5% private.**
2. **Reserves aren't under the Land Title Act**, and the *Strata Property Act* doesn't apply on them. Treaty lands are different.
3. **Since 21 May 2024**, First Nations can hold land in their own name in the provincial system.
4. **The common law right to subdivide is gone** (LTA s.73).
5. **"Townhouse" has no legal meaning.** Strata = condominium.
6. In a building strata, **the land is common property**.
7. **Bare land strata roads are common property**, paid for by the owners.
8. **A duplex can be a strata**, with all the duties of a big one.
9. **No "strata police"**: owners enforce through meetings, the CRT or the Supreme Court.
10. **Schedule of Voting Rights** is needed only if there's at least one **non-residential** lot.
11. **Leasehold strata**: minimum **50-year** lease; landlord renews or **must buy out** the tenant.
12. **Strata lot boundary = centre** of the wall, floor or ceiling.
13. **Nemo dat**: at common law, a forged deed breaks the chain **for everyone after it**.
14. **Recording systems didn't fix void deeds.**
15. A good-faith buyer must pay **valuable consideration**; a **gift or $1 doesn't count**.
16. **Charges aren't indefeasible in BC** (*Gill v. Bucholtz*).
17. **Unregistered interests bind the grantor**, but not honest third parties who register.
18. **Knowing** about an unregistered interest isn't fraud; knowing **plus dishonest conduct, before contracting** is.
19. The assurance fund is a **last resort** and won't pay for losses caused by **your own neglect**.
20. **Plan boundary errors** can't be claimed from the assurance fund.
21. **The contract of purchase and sale isn't registered**; the **Form A** is.
22. **Caveat lapses in 2 months**; **builders lien lasts 1 year** (Form 6 → 21 days); **judgment expires in 2 years**; **manufactured home caution lasts 3 months**.
23. **Builders lien filing deadline: 45 days** after trigger dates, and liens can be filed **after a sale**.
24. **s.219 covenants can be positive.** Common law restrictive covenants can't.
25. **Commission is earned when the transfer is submitted** for registration.
26. **A lender's title policy doesn't protect the owner.**
27. **Site Registry is separate** from land title.
28. **Manufactured home registry: no title, no indefeasibility, no assurance fund.**
29. **CSA sticker ≠ MHR sticker.**

## Drill

**Q1.** Explain, in two sentences, the difference between the common law and Torrens outcomes when a forger sells land to an honest buyer at market value.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

At common law, the forged deed is void (*nemo dat*), so the **original owner gets the land back** and the honest buyer is left suing the (usually vanished) forger. Under Torrens, the honest buyer who paid value and registered **keeps the land**, and the original owner is compensated from the **assurance fund** if they can't recover from the forger.

</details>

**Q2.** A fraudster forges a transfer of Lena's commercial lot into his own name, then borrows $800,000 from a private lender on a registered mortgage and disappears. Does Lena get her land back? Is it free of the mortgage? Can the lender claim from the assurance fund?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Yes**, Lena gets it back: the fraudster was the registered owner who committed the fraud (s.23 exception), and he never sold to a good-faith purchaser of the fee simple.

**Yes**, free of the mortgage: in BC, **charges aren't indefeasible**, and the lender took from someone who had no valid title (*Gill v. Bucholtz*).

**No** assurance fund claim for the lender: at common law it would have had nothing anyway, so the Land Title Act didn't cause its loss.

</details>

**Q3.** Your buyer is purchasing a small office building. The title shows no leases, but a dental clinic occupies the ground floor. The seller says "they're month to month, don't worry." What do you do, and why?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Under **LTA s.23**, an unregistered lease of **3 years or less** with the tenant **in actual occupation** binds the buyer. And a longer unregistered lease could still cause problems if your buyer knew about it before contracting (*Woodwest*).

So: get the **tenancy terms in writing** (ask the clinic directly, through the seller), confirm the term, rent and any renewal options, review the contract's **Possession** and **Title** clauses, and make the deal **subject to** the buyer's review of all tenancies. Don't rely on "don't worry".

</details>

**Q4.** Your title search shows a **claim of builders lien** filed by a roofing subcontractor. The seller says, "I paid my general contractor in full, so this is not my problem." Is it? What are the seller's options?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**It is the seller's problem.** Under the *Builders Lien Act*, **subcontractors** have lien rights against the land even if the owner paid the general contractor. The lien is a charge the seller must clear to deliver the title promised in the contract.

Options (with legal advice): apply to have the lien **dismissed**; **pay money into court** to stand in place of the property, which clears title; **settle** with the subcontractor; or serve a **Form 6** notice so the claimant must sue within **21 days** or lose the lien.

</details>

**Q5.** A listing is a unit in a 2-unit strata. The seller says "it's basically a house, there's no strata council." What should you tell the buyer?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

**A strata is a strata.** Even a duplex strata has a strata corporation with the same legal duties: meetings, budgets and financial statements, insurance, repair of common property, bylaws. There's no one to enforce it except the two owners, and disagreements end up at the **CRT** (*Whiting v. Peters*).

Get the strata documents (plan, bylaws, minutes, financials, any resolutions affecting common property) and recommend the buyer review them carefully, ideally with legal advice.

</details>

**Q6.** On completion day, when does the buyer's deposit become the seller's money, and when can the brokerage take its commission from it?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

When the lawyer or notary **submits the transfer for registration** at the land title office. At that point the seller has no further claim to title and the buyer no further claim to the deposit, so the deposit becomes the **seller's money**. Commission is **earned** at that moment, and if the seller has authorized it, the brokerage can then pay itself from the deposit.

</details>

**Q7 (applied).** Go back to "Mr. Leung." List five steps you'd take before agreeing to list.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

1. **Verify identity properly**: meet in person (or by verified video) and see **original** government photo ID, not scans. Complete the CREA identification record.
2. **Contact the owner using a number you already trust**, such as the one on your brokerage's file, not the new one in the email.
3. **Treat the power of attorney with caution**: try to confirm the instructions directly with the actual owner, and have a lawyer review the POA.
4. **Search** the title and the **Land Owner Transparency Registry**, and check whether the details match what "Mr. Leung" says about the property.
5. **Address the lien and CPL**: find out what they are and how the seller plans to clear them, with legal advice.
6. **Tell your managing broker**, and don't act on listing, transfer or payment instructions until you're satisfied. Set up a **password** with the verified client.

</details>

## Mindset: you're guarding the system, not just the deal

BC's land title system is one of the most trusted in the world. A name on a BC title is something a bank will lend millions against within hours. That trust isn't automatic: it rests on **every professional in the chain doing their checks**.

Fraudsters don't attack the land title office's computers. They attack **people**: a property manager who answers a new email address, a licensee who accepts a scanned passport, a lawyer in a hurry. Torrens then does exactly what it's designed to do: it protects the innocent buyer, and the real owner loses their property for good.

So the habit to build is **professional skepticism**:

- **Verify, don't assume.** The title, the ID, the phone number, the tenancy, the "it's all paid off".
- **Read the whole title, every time.** Each charge is a question you must answer before your client signs.
- **Know what title won't tell you.** Unpaid taxes, short leases, mineral reservations, contamination, heritage controls, water licences, manufactured home registrations: none of these depends on the title showing them.

> 🏢 **For commercial specifically.** Commercial deals are larger, quieter and more often involve companies, trusts and agents signing for owners, which is exactly where fraud and authority problems hide. The agent who asks *"who's authorized to sign for this company, and how do we know?"* is the one sophisticated clients come back to.

> **This week's habit.** Pull up a real BC title (your own home's, or one your brokerage provides for training). For every line, write down **who** benefits, **what** it restricts, and **whether it'll still be there** after a sale. Do it until reading a title feels as natural as reading a menu.
