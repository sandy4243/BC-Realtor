## Why a licensee needs to know bricks and numbers

> **Scenario.** A client asks you to list their eight-year-old house in Kamloops and a 12-suite apartment building they own in Kelowna.
>
> - The house was **rebuilt** five years ago, leaving almost nothing of the old structure. Is it a **"new home"** that needs **warranty insurance** before you can list it?
> - The basement tested at **350 Bq/m³** for radon last winter. Do you have to disclose it?
> - The seller wants $1.4 million because "that's what we spent." Is cost the same as **value**?
> - For the apartment building, the owner shows you total rents and says "price it at 15 times rent." A buyer's analyst will ignore that and ask for the **net operating income** and the **cap rate**.
>
> Every one of these questions is in this lesson.

This lesson has two halves:

1. **How buildings work** (Ch 20): codes, the *Homeowner Protection Act*, inspections, measurement, building systems, the things that go wrong, and green certifications.
2. **How property is valued** (Ch 21–23): the appraisal process, then the three approaches: **direct comparison** (including your CMA), **cost**, and **income**.

You won't be a builder or an appraiser. But you'll price listings, read inspection reports and appraisals, and explain to clients why a property is worth what it's worth. For commercial, the **income approach** is the language every investor speaks.

## Vocabulary first

| Word | Plain English |
|---|---|
| **Building code** | Minimum construction standards for health, safety, accessibility and fire protection. |
| **New home (HPA)** | A newly built home, or one **substantially reconstructed**, for 10 years after occupancy. |
| **Home warranty insurance** | Mandatory "2-5-10" insurance on new homes under the *Homeowner Protection Act*. |
| **Owner-builder** | An individual authorized to build a home for their own use without a builder's licence or warranty. |
| **Building envelope** | Everything separating inside from outside: foundation, walls, windows, roof. |
| **Rain screen** | A wall with an air gap behind the cladding so water that gets in can drain and dry. |
| **Vapour barrier / air barrier** | Layers that stop moisture-laden air moving through the wall. |
| **R-value** | Resistance to heat flow. Higher = better insulation. |
| **Radon** | A natural radioactive gas from the soil that can build up indoors. |
| **Appraisal** | An independent, impartial, objective opinion of value. |
| **Market value** | The most probable price in an open, competitive market between willing, informed parties. |
| **Effective date** | The date the value opinion applies to. |
| **Highest and best use** | The legal, physically possible, financially feasible use that gives the highest value. |
| **Comparable ("comp")** | A recently sold, similar property used as evidence of value. |
| **Adjustment** | A dollar change to a comparable's price to make it more like the subject. |
| **CMA** | Comparative market analysis: a licensee's short, qualitative version of the direct comparison approach. |
| **Replacement cost** | Today's cost to build a modern equivalent of the building. |
| **Reproduction cost** | Today's cost to build an exact replica. |
| **Depreciation** | Loss in value from any cause: physical, functional or external. |
| **Effective age / economic life** | How old the building "acts" / how long it contributes value. |

## Codes, standards and the people who enforce them

Buildings in BC are built to **codes**, and codes are **minimums**, not a promise of quality.

| Code | Notes |
|---|---|
| **National Building Code** | A model code, updated about every **5 years** (latest edition 2020). Provinces adapt it. |
| **BC Building Code (BCBC)** | Applies across most of BC; latest update **2024**. |
| **Vancouver Building By-law (VBBL)** | Vancouver writes its own (based on the 2018 BCBC). |

The code has four big objectives: **structural safety**, **public health**, **accessibility** and **fire protection**.

Three bodies matter to you:

- **BC Housing's Licensing and Consumer Services**: licenses **residential builders** and **building envelope renovators**, approves **owner-builders**, and runs the **New Homes Registry**.
- **Technical Safety BC**: licenses contractors and issues **electrical and gas permits** (separate from the municipal building permit). Swapping a light fixture, switch or thermostat doesn't need a permit; new wiring or gas lines do.
- **The municipality**: building permits and inspections (Lesson 14).

## The Homeowner Protection Act

Between the late 1980s and early 2000s, poorly built and poorly waterproofed buildings, mostly stratas, caused about **$4 billion** of damage in BC: the **leaky condo crisis**. The *Homeowner Protection Act* (1998) responded with three rules:

1. **Residential builders must be licensed** (unless exempt). To qualify, a builder needs **24 months** of experience managing or supervising residential construction in the last **5 years**, must show competency, and must take continuing education.
2. **Every new home must be registered** in the **New Homes Registry**. The online registry covers homes registered after **November 19, 2007**; for older ones, contact Licensing and Consumer Services.
3. **Every new home must have home warranty insurance**, unless exempt.

### Selling a new home

You can't sell or offer to sell a new home **while it's being built** or within **10 years** of the **occupancy permit** unless it has warranty insurance or is exempt. That 10 years runs **no matter how many times** the home has changed hands, so a second or third owner may be selling a "new home" without realizing it.

A home also counts as new if it's been **substantially reconstructed**:

- **more than 75%** of the structure above the foundation was removed or replaced, **or**
- a **new foundation** was laid and the new structural parts (including the foundation) make up **50%** of the finished structure.

> 🚨 **The rebuilt-home trap.** A listing licensee was fined **$1,200** for listing a Vancouver house that had been entirely rebuilt (1% of the old house remained) without warranty insurance. In another case, a **buyer's** licensee who never asked about warranty insurance was **suspended 7 days** and ordered to pay **$1,250**. Both ends of the deal have a duty here.

### What the warranty covers: 2-5-10

| Period | Covers |
|---|---|
| **2 years** | Defects in **materials and labour** |
| **5 years** | Defects in the **building envelope**, including water penetration |
| **10 years** | **Structural** defects |

Claim limits can't be lower than:

- **fee simple home**: the lesser of **$200,000** or the home's value
- **strata unit**: the lesser of **$100,000** or the unit's value

> 💡 **Use the clause.** The BCFSA Knowledge Base suggests a subject clause for new homes: the buyer must receive a copy of the **warranty policy** and be satisfied with its terms (start and end dates, exclusions) by a set date. Use it on every home under 10 years old or substantially rebuilt, and check the **builder registry** and **New Homes Registry** yourself.

## Owner-builders

An **owner-builder authorization** lets someone build their own home without a builder's licence or warranty insurance. It must be obtained **before** construction starts. To qualify, the person must:

- be an **individual** (not a company)
- intend to **live in it** for at least **1 year** after occupancy and **not rent it** in that time
- engage in, arrange or manage **all or substantially all** of the construction

To stop people using this as a loophole:

- They **can't sell** the home during construction or within **12 months** of the occupancy permit.
- If the home is sold within **10 years** of occupancy (by the owner-builder **or a later owner**), the seller must give an **owner-builder disclosure notice** saying whether there's warranty insurance.
- Without warranty insurance, a buyer's only recourse for defects is against the **owner-builder personally**, who is liable for 2-5-10 type defects.

## Inspections and your duties

- **Recommend** a professional inspection to every buyer. If the buyer declines, **document** it.
- Home inspectors are **licensed by Consumer Protection BC**.
- Give the buyer **at least three** inspector names and let them choose. Don't steer them to someone who will go easy.
- Don't pay for the inspection yourself. Any **referral fee** from an inspector must be **disclosed in writing** (Rules s.56).
- The **listing** licensee should attend the inspection or get the seller's permission not to.
- Give the inspection subject enough time to book the inspection, get the report and decide.

> 🧠 **Know your limits.** Inspections find **visible** problems. Building **defects** come in three kinds: **design** defects (bad plans), **visible** defects (you can see them) and **invisible** defects (hidden in walls). Codes are **minimums**; "built to code" doesn't mean built well. When you spot something, refer it to an expert. Don't diagnose it yourself.

## Measuring a home

Measurement errors are a common source of **insurance claims** against licensees. Measure carefully, state how you measured, and recommend a professional measurement when it matters.

- **Floor area** is measured to the **exterior** of the walls. For attached units, measure to the **centre line** of shared (party) walls. A common reference is the **ANSI** standard; commercial space uses **BOMA** standards.
- A floor counts as **above grade** only if it's **entirely** above the ground.
- **Finished area** does **not** include garages, unfinished areas, "open to below" spaces, bump-outs with no floor, or spaces reached only by ladder.
- **Conversion:** square metres × **10.76** = square feet.
- Zoning and planning measurements (e.g. for FSR) follow the **bylaw's** rules and may differ from marketing measurements.

### Metric in construction

Canada went metric with **soft** conversions (same size, new label) and **hard** conversions (new sizes). A "2×4" is actually about **38 × 89 mm**. The building module is **100 mm**; joists sit at **300 or 400 mm** centres; a standard ceiling is **2,400 mm**; sheets of drywall or plywood are **1,200 × 2,400 mm**.

## How a house is built

### Foundations

- The **footing** spreads the load onto the soil; the **foundation wall** sits on it.
- The wall should rise at least **8 inches** (about 200 mm) above the ground.
- **Dampproofing** slows moisture; **waterproofing** stops water under pressure. They're **not** the same.
- **Perimeter drain tile** carries water to a sump or storm drain.
- In areas with **high water tables** (e.g. Richmond, Delta), houses are often built **without basements**.

### Framing and walls

Most BC houses use **platform framing**: each floor is built as a platform and the next storey's walls go on top. The most common structure in Western Canada is the wood **platform frame**.

A typical wall, from inside to outside:

1. interior finish (drywall)
2. **vapour barrier** (warm side)
3. **insulation** between the studs
4. the **frame** (studs)
5. **sheathing** (plywood or OSB)
6. **building paper** or house wrap
7. **cladding** (siding, stucco, brick)

### Construction types for bigger buildings

| Type | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Wood frame** | Cheapest. Wood **shrinks** as it dries, so tall wood buildings need detailing for movement. |
| **Heavy timber** | Large solid timbers; old warehouses and modern mass timber. |
| **Masonry** | Brick or block walls. |
| **Steel frame** | Common in commercial and industrial (warehouses). |
| **Concrete** | Most common for **mid- and high-rise**. Good fire resistance (about **2 hours**). Floors are often a **flat plate** or **flat slab**. |

## The building envelope

The leaky condo crisis was an **envelope** failure. Rain got into walls and couldn't get out.

- **Face-sealed** walls rely on a perfect outer seal. On the wet coast they **failed**.
- **Rain screen** walls leave an **air gap** behind the cladding so any water that gets in can **drain** and the wall can **dry**. Rain screens are now **required** on the coast.
- The **air barrier** must be **continuous**: gaps let warm, moist air into the wall, where it condenses. A material is "air impermeable" when it leaks less than **0.021 L/s/m²** at **75 Pa**.
- The **vapour barrier** (usually **6 mil polyethylene**) goes on the **warm side** of the insulation.

> 🚨 **Envelope work needs experts.** Remedial envelope work costing more than **$2,000 per suite** generally requires a **building envelope consultant**, and renovators must be licensed. When you see envelope repairs in strata minutes or a depreciation report, dig deeper.

## Roofs, cladding and windows

### Roofs

| Roof | What to know |
|---|---|
| **Built-up (tar and gravel)** | Layers of felt and tar on low-slope roofs. |
| **Torch-on membrane** | Modern low-slope roofing, sealed with a torch or adhesive. |
| **Asphalt shingles** | Most common. Watch for **curling edges** and **fishmouths**. A **zinc strip** along the ridge discourages moss. Need a slope of at least **4:12**. |
| **Cedar shakes vs shingles** | **Shakes** are **split** (rough); **shingles** are **sawn** (smooth). |
| **Concrete or clay tile** | Long-lasting but **heavy**; the structure must be designed for the weight. |
| **Metal** | Long-lasting, light. |

Roofers can offer a **RCABC** (Roofing Contractors Association of BC) guarantee on commercial-quality roofs.

### Cladding

- **Wood**: needs regular maintenance.
- **Vinyl / aluminum**: cheap, low maintenance; must be **nailed loosely** so it can expand and contract.
- **Stucco**: **traditional** (cement-based) or **synthetic**. Synthetic stucco can **trap moisture** if the wall behind it isn't detailed properly.
- **Brick veneer**: needs **weep holes** (about every 2 feet along the bottom) to let water out.
- **Fibre cement** board: durable, wood-like look.

### Windows

Better windows have **double or triple glazing**, **low-E coatings**, **gas fill** (argon) and **thermal breaks** in metal frames. Window types include **casement** (hinged at the side), **awning** (hinged at the top), **hopper** (hinged at the bottom), sliders and fixed panes. Entry doors to a **suite** must be **solid core** for fire separation.

## Insulation, heating and ventilation

- **R-value** measures resistance to heat flow. A 3.5" wall cavity takes about **R-12**; a 5.5" (2×6) wall about **R-20**.
- **UFFI** (urea formaldehyde foam insulation) was **banned in Canada in December 1980**. It's a disclosure item if present.
- **Sound** between suites is measured by **STC**; the code minimum between units is about **STC 50**.
- Heating systems must keep living areas at about **22 °C**.

| Heating type | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Forced air** | A furnace heats air and blows it through ducts. Gas furnace efficiency: old **50–60%**, mid-efficiency **80%** minimum, high-efficiency about **95%**. |
| **Hot water (hydronic)** | Boiler heats water piped to radiators; common in older apartments. |
| **Steam** | Old apartments; largely out of favour. |
| **Electric baseboard** | Cheap to install, expensive to run. |
| **Heat pump** | Moves heat in or out; heats and cools efficiently. |
| **Radiant floor** | Hot water or electric heat in the floor. |

- **Chimneys** must rise at least **3 feet** above anything within **10 feet**.
- **Hot water** tanks should be set to about **60 °C** to control bacteria.
- **Ventilation** matters more as houses get tighter: an **HRV** (heat recovery ventilator) brings in fresh air while saving heat.
- BC has offered rebates for **EV charging** in stratas (e.g. **75%** of costs up to a cap).

## Radon

**Radon** is a colourless, odourless radioactive gas from the soil. It's the **second leading cause of lung cancer** in Canada after smoking (about **3,360 deaths** a year).

- Health Canada's guideline: fix it if the annual average is above **200 Bq/m³**. Fix it within **2 years** if above 200, within **1 year** if above **600**.
- About **3.9%** of BC homes exceed 200. Levels tend to be higher in the **Kootenays**, **Okanagan** and **Prince George** areas, but **no area is risk-free**.
- Highest levels are usually in **basements and ground floors**. In taller buildings, the **stack effect** can pull radon up shafts to **upper** floors.
- The only way to know is a **test**. Health Canada recommends **long-term** testing, with the device on the **lowest occupied level**, because levels swing by season.
- The usual fix is **sub-slab depressurization** (a pipe and fan under the slab), which can cut levels by up to **90%**. Since **2014**, the BCBC requires new homes to include a **rough-in** for it.

> 🚨 **Radon is a disclosure issue.** BCFSA treats radon over **200 Bq/m³** as a **material latent defect**. If a seller knows, their licensee **must disclose** it. The Property Disclosure Statement now asks about radon.

## Electrical systems

- **Watts = volts × amps.** Homes get **120/240 volt** service.
- New or substantially renovated homes over **80 m²** need at least **100 amp** service. Most homes have **100 or 200 A**. Older homes may have **30 or 60 A**: legal, but not enough for electric heat or a modern kitchen.
- **Distribution** (enough circuits and outlets) matters more than service size, because adding circuits means opening walls.
- The service rating is on the **main breaker**, but the actual service wires may be smaller.

| Wiring | What to know |
|---|---|
| **Knob and tube** | Pre-**1950**. Copper, **two wires, no ground** (two-hole outlets). Can be safe if properly installed, but outdated. |
| **Aluminum** | Oxidizes and expands; joints can overheat. Needs **CO/ALR**-rated devices and **anti-oxidant** paste. |
| **Copper** | Modern standard, three-wire with ground. |

## Plumbing, wells and septic

| Pipe | What to know |
|---|---|
| **Galvanized steel** | Pre-**1955** supply pipe; lasts **30–50 years**; corrodes from inside (low pressure, rusty water). Joints between new and old pipe have a high (about **80%**) chance of leaking. |
| **Lead** | Old waste pipe; must be replaced. |
| **Cast iron** | Older drain pipe. |
| **Copper / plastic** | Modern supply. |
| **ABS** | Modern plastic drain pipe. |

**Wells:** never represent that a well has **enough** or **safe** water. Use **well reports**, and write **water quality and quantity tests** into a subject clause.

**Septic:** the tank must be at least **10 feet** from the house. Systems installed after **May 31, 2005** must be designed and installed by an **authorized person**, with records filed with the **health authority**. Ask for those records and recommend a septic inspection.

## Safety details and building styles

- **Deck guards**: about **42 inches** high, with gaps under **4 inches** so a child can't slip through.
- **Stairs** wider than **44 inches** need handrails on **both** sides.
- **Elevators** in larger buildings travel at least **200 feet per minute** or twice the building's rise; **hydraulic** elevators suit 3–4 storey buildings.

**Styles you'll see in listings:** Victorian, Georgian, Tudor, Cape Cod, Chalet, Arts and Crafts (Craftsman), Bauhaus (modernist), Prairie, **West Coast** (low-slope roofs, big overhangs, lots of glass, natural wood), and the **Vancouver Special** (boxy two-storey from the 1960s–80s). **Manufactured homes** are factory-built and governed by the *Manufactured Home Act*.

## Green and energy-efficient homes

| Program | What it means |
|---|---|
| **EnerGuide** | Rates a home's energy efficiency (on a 1–100 scale, higher = more efficient). |
| **R-2000** | A federal high-performance standard since the early 1980s: air-tight, well insulated, with mechanical ventilation (HRV). |
| **Built Green** | Industry program covering energy, materials, water and air quality. |
| **LEED** | Rating system: **Certified, Silver, Gold, Platinum**. Common for commercial buildings. |

Passive solar design orients the **largest windows south**, with roof overhangs sized to block high summer sun but let in low winter sun.

> 🏢 **Commercial angle: green isn't automatically a premium.** LEED and energy upgrades can cut operating costs and attract tenants, which raises **NOI**. But an upgrade that just brings an old building up to market norms may not earn a premium, because the market now expects it.

## What an appraisal is

An **appraisal** is an **independent, impartial and objective** opinion of value. We need appraisals because real estate:

- **trades rarely** (so prices aren't obvious)
- is **unique** (no two properties alike)
- is **durable** (value is spread over decades)
- trades in **local** markets
- is affected by **externalities** (what happens next door)

**Appraisers aren't licensed under RESA**. The Real Estate Services Regulation (s.2.8) exempts appraisal from the licensing requirement. Most belong to professional bodies: the **Appraisal Institute of Canada** (designations **CRA** for residential and **AACI** for all property types), following **CUSPAP** standards, or the Real Estate Institute of BC. Lenders often order appraisals through **appraisal management companies**.

> 🔑 **Licensees and value.** When you estimate value to set a listing price, you're doing a **CMA**, not an appraisal. Don't call it an appraisal, and don't hold yourself out as an appraiser.

## Value concepts

### Floor price, ceiling price and the deal

- A **seller's floor price** is the least they'll accept.
- A **buyer's ceiling price** is the most they'll pay.
- A deal happens only if the ceiling is **at or above** the floor. Example: floor $1,150,000, ceiling $1,220,000, sale at $1,180,000. The price lands somewhere in between depending on bargaining.

### Kinds of value

| Value | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Value to the owner** | What the property is worth to a particular person (including sentiment). |
| **Investment value** | Worth to a specific investor given their own goals, tax and financing. |
| **Market value** | The most probable price between a willing seller and willing buyer, both informed and acting prudently, after **reasonable exposure** to the market, with no undue pressure. |

The appraiser almost always estimates **market value**, which ignores any one owner's special circumstances.

### Other key ideas

- The **effective date** is the date the value applies to. It can be in the **past** (expropriation, capital gains, fire loss, estates).
- An appraisal values **legal interests**, not just bricks. A property with a **lease** below market rent, or a mortgage at a below-market rate, is worth something different from the same building free and clear.
- Value is shaped by **economic, political (government), social and physical** forces.
- A **disclaimer** in a report can't excuse **negligence**.

## The seven-step appraisal process

1. **Define the problem**: who's the client and intended users, what's the **purpose** and **use**, which **value** (usually market value), the **effective date**, which **property rights**, and any assumptions.
2. **Scope of work**: how much research and what kind of report. A $100 drive-by and a $5,000 narrative report have very different scopes.
3. **Collect data**: **general** (region, neighbourhood, economy) and **specific** (the property, the comparables).
4. **Analyze the data**: supply and demand, market trends, **highest and best use**.
5. **Apply the approaches**: direct comparison, cost, income.
6. **Reconcile**: weigh the results and pick a final value. Don't just average.
7. **Report**: **oral**, **form** (fill-in-the-blanks) or **narrative** (full written report).

### Conditions of the sale matter

When studying sales, the appraiser checks for things that make a price **unreliable** as market evidence:

- the **time** since the sale (has the market moved?)
- inadequate **advertising** or exposure
- **related parties** (a parent selling to a child)
- unusual **financing** (a cheap vendor take-back mortgage)
- **special purchasers** (a neighbour who'll pay extra to expand)

> 🚨 **Mortgage fraud and inflated values.** Inflated appraisals, non-arm's-length "sales" and flips between related parties are warning signs of **mortgage fraud**. So is a listing priced far above what the market supports: it goes **stale** and hurts the seller.

## Highest and best use

**Highest and best use** is the use that's:

1. **legally permitted** (zoning, covenants)
2. **physically possible** (size, shape, soil)
3. **financially feasible** (it pays)
4. **maximally productive** (it gives the highest value or net return)

A simple test: if the building burned down tomorrow, would the market rebuild the same thing? If not, the current use may not be the highest and best use.

Land "**ripe for development**" (often on the **fringe** of a city or under a new OCP designation) has **latent value**. The appraiser values it with the **residual method**: estimate the value of the finished project, subtract all development costs (including demolition and profit), and what's left is what a developer can pay for the land.

> **Worked example: the residual method.**
>
> - Value of a finished 40-unit apartment project: **$18,000,000**
> - Less demolition of the old house: **$25,000**
> - Less construction, fees, financing and developer's profit: **$11,975,000**
> - **Residual land value: $6,000,000**
>
> The current house might be worth $1.8 million as a house. As a development site it's worth far more, and that's what BC Assessment will use too (owners may get relief, such as the 10-year resident rule from Lesson 14).

## The three approaches at a glance

| Approach | Core idea | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct comparison** | **Substitution**: a buyer won't pay more than the cost of an equally good substitute. | Houses, condos, vacant lots: anything with lots of sales. |
| **Cost** | Land value + cost to build new − depreciation. | New buildings, special-purpose properties (churches, schools), insurance. |
| **Income** | Value = present worth of future income. | Apartments, offices, retail, industrial: income property. |

An appraiser may use all three and **reconcile**. Example: direct comparison $3.9M, cost $4.3M, income $3.7M. For an apartment building, the appraiser would give most weight to the **income** result.

## Appraisers, licensees and liability

Appraisers and licensees can be liable in **contract** (to their client) and in **tort** for **negligent misstatement** (to others who reasonably rely on them).

> **How an overvaluation hurts.** A buyer offers $900,000, relying on an appraisal. The lender lends 80% ($720,000). The property is really worth $650,000. If the buyer defaults, the lender is short **$70,000** ($720,000 − $650,000). The buyer has lost their entire **$180,000** down payment, and the property is worth less than the loan.

Key cases:

| Case | Lesson |
|---|---|
| ***Hedley Byrne*** (UK) | Liability for **negligent misstatement** to someone who relies on you. The adviser can stay silent, answer with a **disclaimer**, or answer and accept responsibility. |
| ***Windsor Motors v. Powell River*** (BCCA 1969) | Applied Hedley Byrne in BC: a careless statement relied on by the other party led to damages. |
| ***Baxter v. F.W. Gapp*** (1938) | A property valued at $1,800 sold for $850. A large overvaluation shifts the burden to the appraiser to explain it. Damages = the **actual loss**. |
| ***Cantwell v. Petersen*** (BCSC 1982) | An appraiser was liable to buyers for missing obvious **dry rot and insect** damage. |
| ***Bourne and Cox v. Martin*** | A licensee who **underpriced** lots at $35,000 (they were soon resold for $65,000) was liable to the sellers. |

- The test is the **reasonable appraiser**: what would a competent professional have done?
- Appraisals aren't exact. An error of **5–10%** is usually considered reasonable. If a home was appraised at $800,000, a ±10% range is $720,000 to $880,000.
- **Reliance letters** can extend who's allowed to rely on a report.

## Technology in valuation

- **Automated valuation models (AVMs)** use statistics and data to estimate value instantly. They're cheap and fast, but they can't see condition, renovations or view, and they struggle with unique properties.
- Lenders use them alongside or instead of full appraisals. CMHC's **emili** (late 1990s) cut demand for appraisals on insured mortgages, though it's really a borrower-risk tool, not a true AVM. Federally regulated lenders must follow **OSFI's B-20** guideline on property valuation.
- Commercial AVMs are less developed because commercial properties are more varied and trade less often.
- **AI** and **blockchain/tokenization** are emerging; they don't replace professional judgment.

## The direct comparison approach

This is the approach you'll use most. It rests on **substitution**: an informed buyer won't pay more for a property than the cost of an equally useful one.

### Finding comparables

- The best source is the appraiser's or licensee's **own market knowledge**.
- **MLS® sales** are good evidence of open-market transactions.
- **Talk to the listing agent** of a comparable for the story behind the price (an estate sale, a family deal, multiple offers). Brokerages must keep **trade records** (Rules s.84).
- **Land Title Office** records show the **declared value** on a transfer, but not the **circumstances**: a father selling to his daughter at a low price looks like any other sale.
- **Current listings** are weak evidence of value. Asking prices are usually above what properties sell for.

### "Recent" and "similar"

**Recent** means sold during a period when market conditions **haven't changed much**. There's no fixed number of months. In a fast market it might be weeks; in a stable one, six months to a year (seldom longer). **Don't use general price indices** (like the CPI) to update old sales, because real estate doesn't move with the price of consumer goods.

**Similar** means any differences won't **materially** affect price. Paint colour doesn't matter; size, layout and location do. Choosing comps in the **same neighbourhood** removes a whole set of differences.

What to compare:

| Group | Factors |
|---|---|
| **Building** | Design (size, rooms, floor plan; style matters only if unusual), quality of construction and maintenance, equipment (appliances, AC, pool), age |
| **Lot** | Natural features (soil, slope, view), nearby amenities (schools, transit, shopping), size (for houses, **frontage** usually matters more than depth) |
| **Title and sale** | Financing, special relationships, what was included |

> 💡 **Quality vs what the market pays.** Experts tend to **overrate** construction quality. A cedar shake roof costs much more than asphalt shingles, but in a mid-priced neighbourhood it adds far less to the sale price than to the build cost. Adjust for what **buyers pay**, not what things **cost**.

The **three-point test** for every comparable:

1. Is the sale **recent**?
2. Are the **lot and improvements** similar?
3. Are the **title and conditions of sale** similar?

## Making adjustments

You can't change a comparable's features, but you can adjust its **sale price** to show what it **would** have sold for if it were like the subject.

> 🔑 **The golden rule: adjust the comparable toward the subject.**
>
> - Comparable is **better** than the subject → **subtract** from the comparable's price.
> - Comparable is **worse** than the subject → **add** to the comparable's price.
>
> Never adjust the subject, and never adjust comparables toward each other.

### Worked example: a three-comparable grid

Subject: a 1990s house with a double garage, an older kitchen, and nothing special.

| | Comp A | Comp B | Comp C |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sale price** | $910,000 | $870,000 | $905,000 |
| Renovated kitchen (subject's is older) | −$35,000 | | |
| No garage (subject has one) | +$20,000 | | |
| Needs exterior paint | | +$8,000 | |
| Narrower lot frontage | | +$15,000 | |
| Has a large patio (subject doesn't) | | | −$10,000 |
| **Adjusted price** | **$895,000** | **$893,000** | **$895,000** |
| Net adjustment | −$15,000 (1.6%) | +$23,000 (2.6%) | −$10,000 (1.1%) |
| Gross adjustment | $55,000 (6.0%) | $23,000 (2.6%) | $10,000 (1.1%) |

The adjusted range is tight ($893,000–$895,000), so the estimate is about **$895,000**. Comp C needed the fewest adjustments, so it's the strongest evidence.

Rules of thumb:

- **The fewer the adjustments, the better the comparable.** Each adjustment is a judgment call that weakens the evidence.
- Net adjustments shouldn't usually exceed about **20%** of a comparable's price (financing adjustments may be an exception).
- Watch **gross** adjustments, not just net. If a comp gets **+$1,000,000** for waterfront and **−$1,000,000** for having no house, the net is zero but it's not a real comparable.
- Adjustments must be **supported by the market**. Cost is a fallback, and what something cost is often more than it adds in value (a pool is the classic example).
- **Don't average** the adjusted prices. Give most weight to the comparables **most like** the subject. Round the final figure.

## Financing and conditions of sale

### Below-market financing

If a seller offered a **vendor take-back (VTB)** mortgage at a rate **below** the market, the buyer got a bargain on the loan and probably paid a **higher price** for the house. To compare fairly, the appraiser converts the price to its **cash equivalent**.

> **Example.** Comp D sold for $1,200,000 with a $300,000 VTB at **4%** when market rates were **6%** (25-year amortization, 5-year term). At the market rate, that mortgage is worth only about **$276,100**. The cheap financing was worth about **$23,900** to the buyer, so the comparable's price is adjusted **down** by about $23,900.

Assumed mortgages and agreements for sale at off-market rates get the same treatment.

### Special relationships

Sales between **related parties** (family members, a company selling houses to its own employees) usually aren't at market value. Don't use them as comparables.

### Services

Comps outside the neighbourhood may differ in **services** (paved road vs gravel, sewer vs septic) and sometimes **taxes**. Those need adjustments too.

## Beyond houses: condos, income property and lots

### Condos (strata units)

"Similar" must also include the **strata's** features:

- **amenities** (pool, gym, parking)
- hidden assets or liabilities (extra land the strata owns; a lien on common property)
- how **strata fees** are shared (unit entitlement) and **what they include** (heat? hot water?)
- **bylaws** (rentals, pets, age)
- the strata's financial health: check the **depreciation report** and **contingency reserve fund**

The **cost approach** doesn't work well for single strata units: you'd have to cost the entire building and allocate it.

### Income property by units of comparison

For apartments and warehouses, compare **price per unit**:

| Warehouse | Price | Size | Price per sq ft |
|---|---|---|---|
| W1 | $4,200,000 | 12,000 sq ft | $350 |
| W2 | $3,450,000 | 10,000 sq ft | $345 |
| W3 | $5,040,000 | 14,000 sq ft | $360 |

A 10,000 sq ft subject most like W1 suggests about **$3,500,000** (10,000 × $350). Apartment buildings are compared by **price per suite**. But for income property, the **income approach** is usually primary.

### Serviced lots

Subdivided, serviced lots are at their highest and best use, so they can be valued by direct comparison. Lot differences include size, shape, slope and location. **Corner lots** have no fixed rule: sometimes a premium (light, width), sometimes a discount (noise, less privacy).

## Limits of direct comparison

Direct comparison is simple, direct and reflects how buyers think. It struggles when:

- there aren't **enough sales** (unusual properties; frozen markets)
- the market is **changing fast**, so nothing is "recent"
- properties are hard to make **similar**, especially **income property**: a building earning half as much NOI isn't worth exactly half, because risk and lease terms differ

Two questions decide whether to rely on it: **Are there comparable properties?** and **Have there been enough recent sales?** If either answer is no, use another approach, widen the search, or for a truly unique property, consider a different pricing strategy (e.g. bracketing between clearly inferior and clearly superior sales, or an auction). Explain the limits to your client.

## The CMA

A **comparative market analysis** is your short-form direct comparison:

- usually **3 to 5** comparables
- mostly **qualitative**: comps are rated **superior, inferior or similar** rather than given precise dollar adjustments
- includes **current listings** (the competition) and **expired listings** (what didn't sell)
- ends with a **suggested price range** and a recommended **list price**

> **Example (condo).** Three recent sales: $485,000 (higher floor, bigger balcony: **superior**), $454,000 (half bath, no balcony, took 63 days with a price cut: **inferior**), $476,000 (next-door building, similar unit: **similar**). The licensee concludes a value of about $475,000–$480,000 and suggests listing just above, in line with current competing listings.

> 🧠 **Become the local expert.** Your CMA is only as good as your market knowledge. Study MLS® data, walk neighbourhoods, attend open houses, read strata documents, know the OCP and zoning, and find a mentor. **Specialize** in one market so you can speak with authority.

## The cost approach

The cost approach says: **Value = land value + cost to build the improvements new − depreciation.**

It assumes a buyer won't pay more than the cost of buying a lot and building an equivalent building. That's only true when the building is the **highest and best use**. An Arctic hotel or an eccentric castle can cost far more to build than anyone will pay.

### Site value

Land can't be manufactured, so **site value** is estimated by **direct comparison** with sales of similar lots.

### Which cost?

- Use **current** cost (today's prices), never **historic** cost (what it cost when built).
- Use **replacement** cost (a modern equivalent with the same usefulness), not **reproduction** cost (an exact replica). Old materials and craftsmanship may be unavailable or costly, and an exact copy of an outdated design may be worth less.

### Estimating cost

The **unit cost** method multiplies a cost per square foot (cubic foot for industrial) by the building's size. Unit costs come from cost services, builders or quantity surveyors.

> **Example.** A 2,000 sq ft house; similar houses cost **$300/sq ft** to build. Replacement cost new = 2,000 × $300 = **$600,000**.

## Depreciation

**Depreciation** is any loss in value from the building's cost new.

### The age-life method

Depreciation % = **effective age ÷ economic life**.

> **Example.**
>
> - Replacement cost new: **$600,000**
> - Actual age: 20 years, but recently renovated, so **effective age 12 years**
> - Economic life: **60 years**
> - Depreciation: 12 ÷ 60 = **20%** (about 1.67% a year) = **$120,000**
> - Depreciated cost: $600,000 − $120,000 = **$480,000**
> - Add land value **$700,000** → indicated value **$1,180,000**
>
> Using the actual age (20 ÷ 60 = 33%) would overstate depreciation and ignore the renovation.

### Market extraction

Work backwards from sales: price − land value = what the building contributes. Compare that to its cost new.

> **Example.** A 10-year-old house sold for $1,100,000; its land is worth $700,000, so the building contributed **$400,000**. Cost new would be **$500,000**, so it has lost **$100,000 (20%)** in 10 years: **2% a year**. Repeat across several sales to find a market-supported rate.

### The breakdown method

The most detailed method splits depreciation into three types, and each into **curable** (worth fixing: the fix adds at least as much value as it costs) or **incurable** (not worth fixing):

| | Curable | Incurable |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical** (wear and tear) | Old paint, broken fixtures | Cracked, decaying foundation |
| **Functional** (outdated design) | Dated bathroom fixtures, old kitchen cabinets | Poor floor plan, out-of-fashion style |
| **External** (outside the property) | n/a: always **incurable** | Airport noise, a railway next door, a major employer closing, very high interest rates |

- **Curable** items are measured by the **cost to cure**.
- **External** depreciation is beyond the owner's control, so it's always **incurable**. It can be **locational** (a noisy neighbour) or **economic** (the local mill shuts).

> **Worked example: breakdown method.**
>
> | Item | Amount |
> |---|---|
> | Replacement cost new (2,000 sq ft × $300) | $600,000 |
> | Physical curable: repainting | −$6,000 |
> | Physical incurable: settling foundation (3%) | −$18,000 |
> | Functional curable: dated bathrooms | −$12,000 |
> | Functional incurable: awkward floor plan | −$15,000 |
> | External: new highway noise (5%) | −$30,000 |
> | **Depreciated replacement cost** | **$519,000** |
> | Add site value (by direct comparison) | $700,000 |
> | **Indicated value** | **$1,219,000** |

## Limits of the cost approach

- It only works if **cost ≈ value**, which needs the building to be the **highest and best use**.
- Estimating **incurable** depreciation is heavily **subjective**, especially for **older** buildings.
- It's most reliable for **new** buildings and **special-purpose** properties with no sales or income data (**churches, schools, public buildings**), and when separate land and building values are needed (**insurance**, **property tax assessment**).
- For houses, it's mainly a **check** on the direct comparison result. A lender relying mainly on a cost approach for a house is taking a risk.

## The income approach

Income property is bought for its **income**. The **income approach** turns a year's income into a value. This course uses **direct capitalization**:

1. Estimate the property's annual **net operating income (NOI)**.
2. Find the **cap rate** investors accept on similar properties.
3. **Capitalize**: Value = NOI ÷ cap rate.

(The other technique, **discounted cash flow (DCF)**, forecasts income year by year. It's for advanced courses and complex properties like hotels.)

Even an **owner-occupied** building can be valued this way, using the **market rent** the owner could charge.

## Building the NOI

> **The NOI ladder**
>
> | Step | Line |
> |---|---|
> | | Gross potential revenue (100% occupied, at **market** rent) |
> | plus | Other income (parking, laundry, storage) |
> | minus | Vacancy and bad debt allowance |
> | equals | **Effective gross income (EGI)** |
> | minus | Operating expenses |
> | equals | **Net operating income (NOI)** |

### Revenue

- **Gross potential revenue** uses **market** rents, even if actual rents differ. If the building is rented below market, that's an **opportunity** for a buyer (and a key skill for commercial licensees to spot).
- **Leasing inducements** (free rent, tenant improvement allowances) aren't operating expenses. They reduce the **effective rent**.
- Separate **parking** and **laundry** income as **other income**.

### Vacancy and bad debt

- An **allowance**, not an operating expense, deducted from gross potential revenue.
- Based on **long-term market** vacancy for comparable buildings (CMHC data, local surveys), not just this building's history.
- Measured as a percentage of **revenue**, not of the number of units.

### Operating expenses

**Fixed** expenses are paid whether the building is full or empty:

- **property taxes**: usually the **largest** single expense
- **insurance**

**Variable** expenses change with occupancy and use:

- **management**: typically **3–5%** of gross income collected (as low as about 1% for net-leased warehouses). **Always include it**, even if the owner manages the building: no property manages itself.
- **utilities**: water, sewer, gas, common-area electricity
- **repairs and maintenance**: interior painting every **3–5 years**; spread **cyclical** costs evenly (painting costing $20,000 every 4 years = $5,000 a year)
- **replacement reserve**: for items that wear out faster than the building, like appliances (about **7 years**) and hall carpet (**10+ years**)
- caretaker **wages** (plus employer contributions), janitorial, grounds and snow removal, garbage, decorating, miscellaneous

> 🚨 **Immediate repairs are not an operating expense.** If the roof needs $40,000 of work now, don't put it in the annual expenses (that would assume $40,000 every year forever). Capitalize the NOI first, then **subtract the repair cost as a lump sum** from the value.

## What's left out of NOI

NOI is the property's income **before** anything that depends on the **owner**:

| Left out | Why |
|---|---|
| **Mortgage payments (debt service)** | Every buyer finances differently. |
| **Income tax** | Every owner's tax rate differs. |
| **Capital cost allowance (CCA)** | Tax depreciation; owner-specific. |
| **Depreciation** | Hard to measure; the cap rate already reflects it. |

NOI is a return on the **whole property**, not on the buyer's equity. A buyer who wants to know their own **equity yield** works in their own mortgage and taxes.

## Leases: who pays the expenses?

| Lease | Who pays operating expenses | Common for |
|---|---|---|
| **Gross** | **Landlord** pays; tenant pays one fixed rent | Apartments, older/smaller offices, government leases |
| **Single net** | Tenant pays base rent + **some** expenses | |
| **Triple net (NNN)** | Tenant pays base rent + **all** operating expenses; landlord keeps **structural** repairs (and usually management) | Most commercial and industrial |
| **Absolute net** | Tenant pays **everything**, including structural repairs | Long single-tenant leases |

- An **escalator clause** in a gross lease lets the landlord recover cost increases above a base. Example: the landlord pays the first $20,000 of property tax; if tax rises to $23,500, the tenant pays the extra **$3,500**.
- Commercial rent is quoted **per square foot per year**, on **rentable** area: space for the tenant's exclusive use, not shared halls or washrooms.
- For **retail**, **frontage** matters a lot. Depth beyond about **80 feet** adds little for small shops.
- In a triple net building, the landlord still pays the operating costs on **vacant** space.

> 🧠 **Apples to apples.** When comparing NOI and cap rates, check that every property is treated the **same way**: gross vs net rents, replacement reserves included in **all or none**, market vs actual vacancy. Mixing them gives a wrong cap rate.

## Yields and cap rates

### The bond analogy

A bond pays a fixed $50 a year in interest.

| Price paid | Yield ($50 ÷ price) |
|---|---|
| $1,000 | **5.00%** |
| $800 | **6.25%** |
| $1,250 | **4.00%** |

When the **price goes up, the yield goes down**, and vice versa. Real estate works the same way: NOI is the "interest" and the cap rate is the yield.

### IRV: the one formula to own

> **I = R × V**
>
> - **I** = net operating income
> - **R** = cap rate
> - **V** = value
>
> So **V = I ÷ R**, and **R = I ÷ V**.

- **Finding the cap rate from a sale:** a building with NOI of $360,000 sold for $6,000,000. R = 360,000 ÷ 6,000,000 = **6.0%**.
- **Using the cap rate to value:** a subject with NOI of $250,000 at a 5.0% cap rate. V = 250,000 ÷ 0.05 = **$5,000,000**.

> 🚨 **Never capitalize gross income.** A cap rate always applies to **NOI**. Dividing gross rent by a cap rate wildly overstates value. (A **gross income multiplier**, price ÷ gross income, is a separate, rougher tool.)

### What sets the cap rate

Investors compare real estate to other investments on three things:

- **risk** (how sure is the income?)
- **management** (how much work?)
- **liquidity** (how fast can you sell?)

More risk, more work and less liquidity → investors demand a **higher** yield → **higher cap rate** → **lower price** for the same NOI. A government bond is safe, easy and liquid, so it yields less than an apartment building. **Industrial** property can be riskier still (a vacant specialized plant can sit empty for a long time), unless it's leased long-term to a strong tenant.

> 💡 **Small changes, big swings.** Cap rates should come from **recent sales** of properties with similar **risk**, ideally at least **four**. Because value = NOI ÷ rate, a small change in the rate moves the value a lot. Be ready to justify the rate you pick.

## Worked example: valuing a 12-suite apartment building

> **Step 1: Cap rate from comparable sales**
>
> | Sale | NOI | Price | Cap rate |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 1 | $160,000 | $3,200,000 | 5.00% |
> | 2 | $210,000 | $4,100,000 | 5.12% |
> | 3 | $150,000 | $3,050,000 | 4.92% |
>
> Sale 1 is most similar and sits between the other two, so use **5.0%**.

> **Step 2: NOI (gross lease, market rents)**
>
> | Item | Amount |
> |---|---|
> | Gross potential rent (12 suites × $2,000 × 12) | $288,000 |
> | Other income (parking, laundry) | $6,000 |
> | Less vacancy and bad debt (3%) | −$8,820 |
> | **Effective gross income** | **$285,180** |
> | Property taxes | $24,000 |
> | Insurance | $12,000 |
> | Utilities | $18,000 |
> | Repairs and maintenance | $15,000 |
> | Caretaker wages | $12,000 |
> | Management (4% of EGI) | $11,400 |
> | Replacement reserve | $4,800 |
> | Miscellaneous | $2,000 |
> | **Total expenses** (about 35% of EGI) | **$99,200** |
> | **NOI** | **$185,980** |
>
> No mortgage payments, income tax or CCA.

> **Step 3: Capitalize**
>
> $185,980 ÷ 0.05 = **$3,719,600**

> **Step 4: Deduct immediate repairs**
>
> The roof needs $40,000 of work now: $3,719,600 − $40,000 = $3,679,600, **rounded to $3,680,000**.

**Sensitivity:** at a 4.5% cap rate the same NOI gives about **$4.13 million**; at 5.5%, about **$3.38 million**. A one-point swing in the rate moves the value by roughly **$750,000**.

**Cross-check:** the appraiser might also compare **price per suite** from the same sales and **reconcile** the two results, giving most weight to the income approach for an investment property.

## Two alerts

> 🚨 **ALERT 1: New homes and rebuilt homes need warranty paperwork.** Any home within **10 years** of its occupancy permit, or **substantially reconstructed**, is a "new home" under the HPA. Before listing or writing an offer, check the **builder registry**, the **New Homes Registry**, and the **warranty policy** or **owner-builder disclosure notice**. Use the warranty subject clause.

> 🚨 **ALERT 2: Price from evidence, not wishes.** A CMA must rest on **recent, similar, arm's-length** sales, adjusted toward the subject. Cost isn't value, asking prices aren't sales, and a seller's purchase price isn't today's value. Overpricing makes listings go stale; underpricing can make you **liable** (*Bourne and Cox v. Martin*).

## Trap list: things the exam likes to twist

1. Building codes are **minimums**. NBC updated about every **5 years**; BCBC updated **2024**; Vancouver has its own **VBBL**.
2. **Technical Safety BC** issues **electrical and gas** permits, separate from building permits.
3. HPA: builders **licensed** (24 months' experience in 5 years); homes **registered**; **warranty insurance** required.
4. "New home" lasts **10 years** from the occupancy permit, **regardless of how many owners**.
5. Substantial reconstruction: **>75%** above the foundation replaced, or new foundation + **50%** new structure.
6. Warranty **2-5-10**: 2 years materials/labour, 5 years **envelope**, 10 years **structural**.
7. Claim limits: lesser of **$200,000** or value (fee simple); lesser of **$100,000** or value (strata unit).
8. Owner-builder: **individual**; live in it **1 year**, no renting; **can't sell within 12 months** of occupancy; disclosure notice for sales within **10 years**.
9. Home inspectors are licensed by **Consumer Protection BC**. Give **three** names. Referral fees disclosed in **writing** (s.56).
10. Floor area: **exterior** walls; **centre line** of party walls; **above grade** = entirely above ground. m² × **10.76** = sq ft.
11. Finished area excludes garages, unfinished space, open-to-below, and ladder-only areas.
12. **Dampproofing ≠ waterproofing**. Vapour barrier on the **warm** side.

17. New/renovated homes over **80 m²** need **100 A** service. **Distribution** matters more than service size.
18. Knob and tube: pre-**1950**, **no ground**. Aluminum: needs **CO/ALR** devices.
19. Galvanized pipe: pre-**1955**, **30–50 year** life.
20. Septic tank ≥ **10 feet** from the house; post-**May 31, 2005** systems need an **authorized person** and filed records.
21. Appraisers are **exempt** from RESA licensing (RESR s.2.8).
22. **Market value** ignores the owner's special circumstances; **value to the owner** doesn't.
23. Effective date can be in the **past**.
24. Appraisals value **legal interests** (leases, mortgages), not just buildings.
25. Highest and best use: **legal, physical, financial, maximally productive**.
26. Direct comparison = **substitution**; cost = land + cost new − depreciation; income = **NOI ÷ cap rate**.
27. **Adjust the comparable toward the subject**: comp better → **subtract**; comp worse → **add**.
28. **Fewer adjustments = better comparable.** Net adjustments under about **20%**. Watch **gross** adjustments.
29. **Don't average** comparables; weight the most similar.
30. Current **listings** are weak evidence. Don't use **price indices** to update old sales.
31. "Recent" = **stable market conditions**, not a fixed period.
32. Below-market **VTB** → comp's price adjusted **down** to cash equivalent.
33. Related-party sales aren't market evidence.
34. Use **replacement** cost (modern equivalent) and **current** cost.
35. Age-life uses **effective** age, not actual age.
36. **External** depreciation is always **incurable**. Curable = cost to cure.
37. Cost approach best for **new** and **special-purpose** buildings (churches); weak for strata units and old buildings.
38. **Vacancy** is an allowance, **not** an operating expense. Leasing inducements are **not** operating expenses.
39. **Management** is always an expense, even if owner-managed (**3–5%**).
40. **Property tax** is usually the largest expense.
41. NOI excludes **mortgage payments, income tax, CCA, depreciation**.
42. **Immediate repairs**: deduct from **value** as a lump sum, not from NOI.
43. **Triple net**: tenant pays all operating costs; landlord keeps **structural**. **Absolute net**: tenant pays everything.
44. Higher risk → **higher** cap rate → **lower** value. Prices and yields move **inversely**.
45. **Never** capitalize **gross** income.

## Drill

**Q1.** Your seller's house was built in 1985 but completely rebuilt in 2022, with a new foundation and almost all new framing. The seller says "it's an old house, no warranty needed." What do you do?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- A new foundation plus new structure making up at least **50%** of the result (or replacing more than **75%** of the structure above the foundation) is a **substantial reconstruction**. It's a **"new home"** under the *Homeowner Protection Act* for **10 years** from the occupancy permit.
- You **can't list or offer it for sale** unless it has **home warranty insurance** or an exemption (e.g. an **owner-builder** authorization, which requires an owner-builder disclosure notice).
- Check the **New Homes Registry** and **builder registry**, ask for the policy, and if in doubt contact BC Housing's Licensing and Consumer Services or a lawyer.
- Remember the licensee fined **$1,200** for listing a rebuilt home without warranty.

</details>

**Q2.** Adjust these comparables to the subject. The subject has a garage and a basic kitchen. Comp 1 sold for $760,000 and has a basic kitchen but **no garage** (market value of a garage: $25,000). Comp 2 sold for $805,000, has a garage and a **new kitchen** (worth $30,000 to buyers). What do they indicate?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- Comp 1 is **inferior** (no garage) → **add** $25,000 → **$785,000**.
- Comp 2 is **superior** (new kitchen) → **subtract** $30,000 → **$775,000**.
- Indicated range **$775,000–$785,000**. Weight whichever comp is otherwise most like the subject; don't just average to $780,000.

</details>

**Q3.** A house has a replacement cost new of $500,000, an actual age of 25 years, an effective age of 15 years, and an economic life of 50 years. Land is worth $650,000. What's the value by the cost approach (age-life)?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- Depreciation = effective age ÷ economic life = 15 ÷ 50 = **30%** = $150,000.
- Depreciated cost = $500,000 − $150,000 = **$350,000**.
- Value = $350,000 + $650,000 = **$1,000,000**.
- Using the **actual** age (25 ÷ 50 = 50%) would wrongly give $900,000.

</details>

**Q4.** A small office building has gross potential rent of $400,000, a 5% vacancy allowance, and operating expenses of $130,000. The owner also pays $90,000 a year in mortgage payments. Similar buildings sell at a 6% cap rate. What's it worth?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- EGI = $400,000 − 5% ($20,000) = **$380,000**.
- NOI = $380,000 − $130,000 = **$250,000**. Mortgage payments are **not** deducted.
- Value = $250,000 ÷ 0.06 = **$4,166,667**, about **$4,170,000**.

</details>

**Q5.** Classify each item of depreciation: (a) the house backs onto a new rail line; (b) the bathroom has 1980s fixtures that cost $10,000 to update and would add $15,000; (c) the foundation is cracking and would cost more to fix than it adds; (d) the only bathroom is reached through a bedroom and can't be moved economically.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- (a) **External** (locational): always **incurable**.
- (b) **Functional curable**: fixing adds more than it costs, so it's measured by the **cost to cure** ($10,000).
- (c) **Physical incurable**.
- (d) **Functional incurable** (poor floor plan).

</details>

**Q6.** A buyer's analyst says a warehouse with NOI of $300,000 is worth $5,000,000. Another says $6,000,000. What cap rate is each using, and what would make the higher value reasonable?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

## Mindset: price is an opinion; value is evidence

Clients will tell you what their property is worth: what they paid, what they spent, what the neighbour got, what "the market" should pay. Your job is to answer with **evidence**: recent, similar, arm's-length sales, adjusted honestly toward the subject property. When the evidence is thin, say so.

The same goes for buildings. You're not an inspector or an engineer, but you should know enough to **spot** the warning signs (rebuilt without warranty, radon, knob and tube, galvanized pipe, envelope repairs) and **send** the client to the right expert.

> 🏢 **For commercial specifically.** This is the core toolkit of a commercial licensee.
>
> - **Think in NOI and cap rates.** Investors ask "what's the NOI and what cap rate does that imply?" before anything else. Learn to rebuild an owner's income statement into a market **NOI**: market rents, market vacancy, management even if self-managed, reserves, and **no** debt service.
> - **Find the upside.** Below-market leases, unused density, or space that could be reconfigured are where value is created. Spotting them is how commercial licensees build a reputation.
> - **Read leases.** Gross vs **triple net**, escalators, inducements, expiries and **rentable area** drive the income stream, and the income drives the price.
> - **Know cap rate drivers.** Tenant quality, lease length, location and building age change the rate, and a half-point change can move value by millions.
> - **Highest and best use** and the **residual method** price development land. Land designated for density is valued as a site, not as the old building on it.
> - **Building condition matters to investors too**: envelope, roof, elevators, mechanical systems and environmental issues come off the price as **immediate repairs** or higher cap rates.

> **This week's habit.** Pick one apartment building or small strip mall for sale in your area. Find the asking price and any income figures in the listing. Calculate the implied cap rate (NOI ÷ price). Then ask: what's missing from their NOI (management? reserves? vacancy?), and what would the cap rate be if you added it?
