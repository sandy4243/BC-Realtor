## Why "what can I do with it?" and "what will it cost me?" matter so much

> **Scenario.** A client wants to buy a $2.4 million older two-storey building on a commercial street. They plan to open a pet store on the ground floor and rent the four apartments upstairs.
>
> - The **zoning** allows retail, but the city's **business licence bylaw** bans pet stores from selling cats and dogs.
> - One of the apartments is an **unauthorized suite** that the city doesn't know about yet.
> - The land is designated for **six-storey mixed use** in the **official community plan (OCP)**, so it could be worth more as a development site than as a building.
> - The seller lives in Hong Kong, so the buyer may have to **hold back 25% or 50%** of the price for the CRA.
> - **Property transfer tax** alone will be about **$50,000**, and **GST** may apply to the commercial part.
>
> Every one of these is a "relevant fact" your client needs to know before they commit.

This lesson covers two things every buyer cares about:

1. **What the government lets you do with land** (Ch 18): OCPs, zoning, rezoning, non-conforming uses, variances, permits, subdivision and building bylaws.
2. **What the government charges you** (Ch 19): GST, property transfer tax (PTT), the foreign buyers' tax, capital gains and flipping taxes, property tax and assessments, and the vacancy taxes.

You're not expected to be a planner or a tax advisor. You **are** expected to know these rules exist, to ask the right questions, and to send your client to the right expert.

## Three levels of government

The *Constitution Act, 1867* split law-making power between the **federal** government and the **provinces**. The provinces got power over **property**, and can create a third level, **local government**, by handing some of their powers down.

### The federal government

Ottawa can't directly zone land in a province. It controls land use only where that's needed for its own powers, for example:

- **airports**: height limits on buildings and even trees near flight paths
- **fisheries**: rules for activities near certain rivers
- **federal land**: not subject to municipal zoning

You'll meet federal rules only in special situations. Provincial or municipal rules affect almost every property.

### The provincial government

The province hands most land-use powers to local governments, but keeps some for itself:

| Statute | What it does |
|---|---|
| ***Agricultural Land Commission Act*** | Created the **ALR** (about **5 million hectares**). No non-farm use or subdivision without **ALC** approval. One principal residence (new ones ≤ **500 m²**) plus possibly a small secondary residence. |
| ***Public Health Act*** | Health authorities approve **septic systems**. Subdivisions without sewers must show the land can handle septic. |
| ***Local Services Act*** | Some planning rules in rural areas (most rural planning is done by regional districts). |
| ***Islands Trust Act*** | A **trust committee** handles zoning and subdivision on the **Gulf Islands**; the Islands Trust Council sets policy. |
| ***Transportation Act*** | Provincial approval needed to **rezone** land within **800 metres** of an intersection with a **controlled access highway**. |
| ***Environmental Management Act*** | A screening process for **contaminated sites**. If a past use is on **Schedule 2** (e.g. gas station, dry cleaner), the applicant files a **site disclosure statement** and approvals freeze until the province signs off. |
| ***Heritage Conservation Act*** | Protects heritage and archaeological sites, **even undesignated ones**. Alterations need a permit. |

> 💡 **ALR on title.** Most ALR land has a **legal notation** on title ("THIS TITLE MAY BE AFFECTED BY THE AGRICULTURAL LAND COMMISSION ACT…"). It's a **notice**, not a charge. But the **ALC's records win**: if the ALC says the land is in the ALR, it is, even with no notation. Check the ALC's online **ALR map** for anything near farmland.

The ALC can **exclude** land from the ALR (it's then subject only to local bylaws), or allow **non-farm use** or **subdivision** (the land stays in the ALR).

> 🧠 **Heritage: why title isn't enough.** A heritage designation may appear as a legal notation, but whole **heritage conservation areas** in an OCP may not show on title at all, and the *Heritage Conservation Act* protects **undesignated** sites too.
>
> In ***Mackay v. BC*** (2013), a buyer bought a Victoria lot to build a house, then learned it was an undesignated heritage site. The heritage inspection and report alone cost **over $51,000**, and they claimed **$600,000** in losses.
>
> Your due diligence: ask the local government (heritage bylaws, register, OCP), contact the **BC Archaeology Branch**, check the **Property Disclosure Statement**, and put the onus on your client **in writing** to investigate. Recommend legal advice.

### Local government

Local governments are **creatures of the province**. Their powers come from three statutes:

- the ***Community Charter***: core powers of **municipalities** (since 2004)
- the ***Local Government Act***: mainly **regional districts**, plus planning, land use and elections for everyone
- the ***Vancouver Charter***: the **City of Vancouver** only

They can only do what those statutes allow, and the province can change those powers at any time. They also have the powers of a **natural person** (they can contract, buy and sell land).

The *Local Government Act* creates three kinds of local government or authority, which often **overlap**:

| Type | Key points |
|---|---|
| **Regional districts** | Cover almost all of BC, split into **electoral areas**. In **unorganized areas** (no municipality), they act like the municipality. Run by a **board**: appointees from member municipalities plus a director from each electoral area. |
| **Municipalities** | Villages, towns, cities and district municipalities (the form mostly depends on population). All have the same planning powers. Run by a **mayor and 4 to 8 councillors** elected for **4-year** terms. |
| **Improvement districts** | Created by **letters patent** after residents petition. Provide **specific services** (water, sewer, drainage, fire, electricity) mostly in rural areas. Run by elected **trustees**. Can tax, borrow and expropriate, but have **no general land-use planning powers** (advisory only). |

> 💡 **City hall is your best source.** Planning and building departments answer licensee questions every day. Ask about zoning, permitted uses, siting, setbacks, permits, building orders and natural hazard reports (floods, slides, earthquakes). **Ask in writing and get a written answer** where you can. Learn each municipality's process, fees and turnaround times.

## Why local governments regulate land

Four main goals sit behind planning controls:

1. **Public health and safety**: fire codes, safe water and sewer, no building on flood plains.
2. **Protecting property values**: keeping incompatible uses (like heavy industry) away from homes.
3. **Efficiency, convenience and appearance**: compact commercial centres, affordable servicing, landscaping around parking lots.
4. **Conserving natural resources**: the ALR, stream and ravine protection (often **10 metres** from a stream bank).

Knowing **why** a rule exists helps you predict what planners will ask for when your client applies for a rezoning or subdivision.

## The planning process

Three groups interact:

- **the public**: consulted at many stages, especially public hearings
- **elected officials** (council or the regional board): make the final decisions
- **professional planners**: analyze, study options and recommend; later they administer the rules and issue permits

Some councils appoint an **advisory planning commission**: volunteer residents who review proposals and advise council.

The *Local Government Act* is mostly **permissive**: local governments **may** use planning tools, but usually don't have to. That's why the rules vary so much from place to place.

## Official community plans

An **OCP** is a local government's long-range vision. Adopting one is optional, but most urban areas have one. (Vancouver uses "official development plans" and neighbourhood plans.)

An OCP must include policies on:

- residential development needed for at least **5 years** of anticipated housing needs
- commercial, industrial, institutional, agricultural, recreational and utility land uses
- restrictions on **hazardous** or **environmentally sensitive** land
- major roads, sewers and water lines
- public facilities
- **affordable**, **rental** and **special needs** housing

It includes **maps** showing each area's **land use designation**, both current and intended future use. An OCP is adopted by **bylaw** after a **public hearing**, and generally must be updated every **5 years**.

**What an OCP does and doesn't do:**

- It **doesn't** commit the government to build anything, and it **isn't** a zoning bylaw.
- But every later bylaw, permit and project **must be consistent** with it. To rezone farmland for houses, council must first **amend the OCP**.

> 🏢 **OCPs are where the money is.** When an OCP designates a corridor for more density, older properties along it can jump in value as **development sites**. Vancouver's 2013 West End plan did exactly that for older low-rise buildings on a main street. A commercial licensee who reads OCPs and draft plans can spot land assembly opportunities before the market does.

### Rural land use bylaws

In areas the minister designates as **rural planning areas**, a regional district can adopt a single **rural land use bylaw** that combines OCP-style policy with zoning. It's simpler and cheaper, and once adopted, it has the same effect as an OCP and a zoning bylaw.

## Zoning

**OCP vs zoning:**

| | OCP | Zoning bylaw |
|---|---|---|
| Level | High-level, strategic | Detailed, prescriptive |
| Time frame | Current **and future** land use | **Current** land use only |
| Example | "This area is for future high-density housing." | "Max height 12 m, max 45% site coverage, 4 m setback from the road." |

Zoning can't conflict with the OCP.

A zoning bylaw usually has three parts:

1. an **official zoning map**
2. **general** administration and interpretation sections
3. **regulations for each zone** (permitted uses, siting, size)

Zone names aren't standardized, but common patterns are: **A** = agricultural, **R** = residential (RS = single family, RM = multiple family), **C** = commercial, **I** = institutional, **M** = industrial, **CD** = **comprehensive development** (a site-specific zone).

> ⚠️ **How a property is used isn't proof it's legal.** Always check the zoning against the actual use. Online maps may be out of date; the municipality keeps the **certified official map**.

### What zoning bylaws usually control

- **Number of buildings**: usually one per lot plus accessory buildings. **Laneway** or **coach houses** are now commonly allowed.
- **Setbacks and yards**: distances from property lines (projections like bay windows and steps usually don't count).
- **Height**: in metres or storeys.
- **Density**: **floor space ratio**, units per hectare, or minimum lot area per unit.
- **Home occupations**: usually allowed with limits (no signs, no outside storage, no non-resident employees).
- **Off-street parking and loading**: the number of spaces (per suite, per 1,000 m², per seat) and their size.
- **Signs**: size, design and location.

> 🧠 **Infill homes: legal structure beats the name.** Laneway, coach and carriage homes are all **infill housing**. If they're **strata titled**, they can be sold separately. If they sit on the **same legal lot** as the main house (typical for laneway homes), they can't. Check the title, not the marketing name.

### Secondary suites and short-term rentals

**Secondary suites** ("mortgage helpers") are common, and at least one accessory dwelling unit is now generally allowed across BC. A suite built **without permits and inspections** is an **illegal** (unauthorized, non-conforming) suite.

Many municipalities don't actively enforce against illegal suites unless there's a complaint or a safety concern. But if they do, the owner may have to upgrade it, remove it, or pay fines.

> 🚨 **Illegal suites and your duties.**
>
> - As **listing** licensee: don't advertise an illegal suite as a "mortgage helper" or income source. That's misleading advertising (**Rules s.41**). Instead, add a contract clause where the buyer acknowledges the unauthorized accommodation.
> - Missing permits are part of the definition of a **material latent defect**, so you must disclose a known illegal suite to all parties (**Rules s.59**).
> - As **buyer's** licensee: check with the local government (**Rules s.30(h)**: reasonable efforts to discover relevant facts). Never let a buyer think suite income is guaranteed.

**Short-term rentals** (Airbnb, VRBO): many municipalities ban rentals under **30 days**. In 2023 the province passed the ***Short-Term Rental Accommodations Act* (STRAA)**:

- short-term rentals limited to the host's **principal residence plus one secondary suite or ADU**
- exemptions for some small communities (**10,000 people or fewer**), resort areas and others
- tougher fines: regional district maximum fines up from **$2,000 to $50,000**; ticket fines up from **$1,000 to $3,000** per infraction **per day**

STRAA is a **floor**. Municipalities and strata corporations can be **stricter**. Vancouver, for example, doesn't allow a short-term rental in a secondary suite unless the operator lives **in** that suite. Also think about **strata bylaws**, **insurance** and **tax** (GST, PST, income tax).

> 🧭 **Case: 1120732 B.C. Ltd. v. Whistler (2020 BCCA).** Whistler's zoning required hotel strata units with rental pool covenants to be rented through a **single professional rental pool manager**. Owners who wanted to rent on their own argued the bylaw regulated **users**, not **uses**. The bylaw was upheld: it had a valid land-use purpose and wasn't unreasonably discriminatory. Lesson: unusual zoning rules, especially in resort towns, can be valid and enforceable.

### Rezoning

Rezoning means **amending the zoning bylaw**. It usually starts with an **owner's application** (the owner, or someone with their authority), plus a fee.

The process:

1. Staff review the application and may ask for plans, traffic or market studies.
2. **First reading**: council votes to proceed in principle.
3. A **public hearing** is generally required (after first reading, before third) if the bylaw is **inconsistent with the OCP**. Public hearings are generally **prohibited** for residential projects that are **consistent** with the OCP (but notice is still given).
4. **Second and third readings**, often subject to conditions: dedicating land for roads or parks, paying for road widening and utilities.
5. Once the developer meets the conditions (or posts security, usually a **letter of credit**), **final adoption**.

So a rezoning bylaw gets **three readings plus final adoption**: four votes in total. It can take **4 months** for a simple one, or **2 to 3 years** for a complex one.

> 🔑 **No compensation for down-zoning.** Under the *Local Government Act*, a local government isn't liable to an owner for passing, amending or repealing a zoning bylaw or OCP, even if the land loses value.

### Land use contracts

From **1971 to 1978**, municipalities (not Vancouver) could adopt **land use contracts**: site-specific zoning in the form of a contract with the owner, registered on title like a restrictive covenant. They were later replaced in part by **development permits**.

In 2014 the law was changed so that **all land use contracts ended on June 30, 2024** (some were terminated earlier by bylaw). The registered charge is deemed discharged, there's **no compensation**, and a lawful existing use carries on as a **non-conforming use**. If an old land use contract causes an issue, recommend legal advice.

## Non-conforming use

Zoning isn't **retroactive**. If the rules change, a use or building that was legal before becomes **lawfully non-conforming** ("grandfathered"). It can be non-conforming as to **use** (a junkyard in a newly residential zone) or as to **siting** (a house built with a 2 m setback when the rule is now 3 m).

**The rules under the *Local Government Act*:**

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| Change of owner | Status **continues** through sales. |
| Stop using it | Status is **lost** if the use is discontinued for **6 continuous months** (**90 days** in Vancouver). |
| Alterations | **No structural alterations or additions** unless required by law or allowed by the **board of variance**. |
| Expanding | The use can spread through the **existing building**, but not onto **more land**. |
| Damage | If damaged or destroyed to **75% or more of its value**, it must be rebuilt to **current** rules. |

> 🏢 **Why buyers and lenders care.** A non-conforming building may not be rebuildable as is after a major fire, and some **insurers** and **lenders** limit coverage or loans. A commercial buyer planning to keep a grandfathered use (an older service station, an auto body shop in a now-residential area) must know it can't expand and can be lost after 6 months' vacancy. Find out whether a use is **permitted**, **lawfully non-conforming** or **illegal**, and tell the buyer.

## Board of variance

Every local government with a zoning bylaw **must** have a **board of variance** (an appointed body). It can grant a **minor variance** where complying with the zoning bylaw would cause **undue hardship**, and the variance doesn't conflict with local policy.

It can vary only zoning rules on the **siting, size and dimensions** of buildings and structures. The most common variance is a relaxed **setback**. It can set a time limit for construction.

Example: a stream crosses the back of a lot, so the owner can't build the permitted floor area and still meet the **10 m front setback**. The board may allow a reduced front setback.

## Permits

Part 14 of the *Local Government Act* lets local governments vary rules for individual sites through permits. Three matter most here.

### Development permits

**Development permit areas** must first be designated in the **OCP**, for reasons such as:

- protecting the **natural environment**, or development from **hazards**
- protecting **farming**
- **revitalizing** a commercial area
- setting objectives for intensive residential, commercial, industrial or multi-family **form and character**, resort development, or **energy**, **water** or **greenhouse gas** goals

In a DP area, **no construction or land alteration** can happen without a **DP**, and a DP is often needed **before a building permit**. A typical example: a watercourse DP setting a construction setback from the top of the bank and requiring landscaping.

> 💡 **DPs on title.** A permit shows as a legal notation, e.g. "THIS TITLE MAY BE AFFECTED BY A PERMIT UNDER PART 14 OF THE LOCAL GOVERNMENT ACT, SEE …" (older ones mention Part 26 or Part 29). The notation doesn't give details; get the permit from the local government. For a finished house with no planned changes it rarely matters; for redevelopment or a change of use it **does**.

### Development variance permits

Issued by **council or the board**, a DVP can vary many bylaws (zoning, parking, signs, landscaping, subdivision servicing, minimum parcel sizes), but it **can't vary use or density**. Council doesn't have to grant one, and often sends applicants to rezone instead.

| | Board of variance | Development variance permit |
|---|---|---|
| Who decides | Appointed board | Elected council / board |
| What it can vary | Zoning rules on **siting, size, dimensions** only | Many bylaws, but **not use or density** |
| Test | **Minor** variance + **undue hardship** | Council's discretion |

### Temporary use permits

A **TUP** allows a temporary **commercial or industrial** use, for a set term (up to **3 years**), often with a requirement to **restore** the land afterwards. It can be **renewed only once**. Example: using a house as a vehicle repair shop for a limited time.

## Subdivision

Subdivision is governed by both the ***Land Title Act*** and the ***Local Government Act***. The **approving officer** (in a municipality, appointed by council, usually the engineer or planner) signs the **subdivision plan** prepared by a BC land surveyor.

The officer checks compliance with the **subdivision bylaw** (parcel size, road access, servicing) and can also refuse a plan that is **against the public interest** (*Land Title Act* s.85(3)).

> 🧭 **Case: Hlynsky v. West Vancouver (1989 BCCA).** A subdivision met all the zoning rules, but the approving officer refused it because it didn't fit the neighbourhood's character. A court can overturn such a decision only if it was made:
>
> 1. in **bad faith**,
> 2. to **discriminate** against an owner, or
> 3. on a "specious and totally inadequate" factual basis.
>
> None applied, so the refusal stood. Meeting zoning is **not** a guarantee of subdivision approval.

The subdivider usually pays for **roads, lighting, underground wiring, water, sewer, drainage and hydrants** to the bylaw standard, either before approval or secured by a **letter of credit**. They may have to dedicate up to **5% of the land for parks** and protect streams. Larger subdivisions involve a lot of negotiation.

### Development cost charges and amenity contributions

| | DCCs | CACs |
|---|---|---|
| Basis | Set by **DCC bylaw** (a standard calculation) | **Negotiated** with the developer during rezoning |
| Paid for | Water, sewer, drainage, major roads, parkland | Amenities: affordable housing, parks, libraries, child care, community centres |
| Triggered by | **Subdivision** or **building permit** (some residential projects under 4 units exempt at building permit) | Approval of a rezoning |
| Can the government require it? | Yes | **No**, it must be agreed |

> 🏢 **DCCs and CACs are deal costs.** On a development site, these charges can run to tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars per unit. They come straight off what a developer can pay for the land. A commercial licensee pricing development land needs to know the local DCC rates and what CACs the municipality typically expects.

## Building bylaws and inspection

**Building bylaws** protect **health and safety**. They work in two ways:

1. A **building permit** is needed to build, alter, repair, move or demolish a building, or install wiring, plumbing or heating. If the plans comply, the permit **must** be issued.
2. **Building inspectors** check the work matches the approved plans, and may inspect existing buildings for safety or zoning breaches.

For **complex buildings** (multi-family, larger commercial and industrial, institutional), the **BC Building Code** requires the owner to hire **registered professionals** (architects and engineers) to certify the plans and construction. The inspector may only spot-check. If there's a defect, the owner's remedy is usually against the **professional**, not the local government.

**Enforcement:** fines, or orders to **stop work**, **demolish**, **remove** or **upgrade**. The *Community Charter* allows a **notice on title** (s.57) warning buyers and lenders of a contravention, e.g. "BYLAW CONTRAVENTION NOTICE, COMMUNITY CHARTER, SECTION 57, SEE …".

> ⚠️ **No notice on title doesn't mean no order.** Always check the municipality's records too. If there **is** a s.57 notice, get the details from city hall, tell the buyer, and recommend legal advice.

## Licensing bylaws

Municipalities require **business licences** and regulate how businesses operate. They usually can't ban a business type outright, but can make the licence fee prohibitive.

> 🏢 **Zoning isn't enough: the pet store lesson.** A buyer bought a Burnaby property to open a pet store after their licensee confirmed the **zoning** allowed it. When they applied for a **business licence**, they found the city's licence bylaw **banned pet stores from selling cats and dogs**. They owned a building they couldn't use as planned.
>
> For any commercial purchase, check **zoning, business licensing, parking and signage bylaws** against the buyer's exact intended use, and make it a **subject** condition.

Also watch for **renoviction bylaws**: some municipalities (e.g. New Westminster, Port Coquitlam) require landlords to house tenants during renovations or limit rent increases after. A buyer planning to renovate a tenanted building needs to know.

> 🔑 **The golden rule of Chapter 18.** Never assume what's allowed. **Always ask** the municipality or regional district, and refer complex questions to a lawyer or planner.

## Taxes: information, not advice

Tax law is complex. Your job is to **know the taxes exist**, give **general information**, and **refer** your client to a tax professional (**Rules s.30(d)**: advise clients to get independent advice on matters outside your expertise).

> 🚨 **Licensees have been disciplined over tax.** Examples from the former Real Estate Council:
>
> - drafting a GST clause and not advising the client to get tax advice: **$2,500** penalty, **$1,500** expenses, remedial course
> - advertising a home as a new show home at "$699,000 **plus GST**" when the licensee was living in it: **60-day suspension**
> - suggesting a buyer pay **$42,000** of the price directly to the seller to get under a PTT threshold: **60-day suspension**, **$7,500** penalty and courses

There are two groups of taxes:

1. **Taxes on buying and selling**: GST, PTT, the foreign buyers' tax, non-resident withholding, capital gains and flipping taxes.
2. **Taxes on owning**: property tax, SVT, Vancouver's EHT and the federal UHT.

## GST on real estate

GST is a federal **value-added tax** at **5%** in BC, under the ***Excise Tax Act* (ETA)**. It's often treated as an afterthought, and that causes panicked calls on closing day, collapsed deals and lawsuits.

### The basic rule: taxable unless exempt

Every **supply** (sale or provision) of property, including **real property** and any interest in it (even an **option**), made in the course of a **commercial activity** is a **taxable supply**, **unless** it's an **exempt supply** listed in **Schedule V** of the ETA.

Schedule V exempts things like:

- most sales of **used residential** property (more precisely, sales by someone who **isn't a builder**)
- **long-term residential rentals**
- certain sales of **personal-use or vacant land** by individuals

> 🔑 **Default position: GST applies** unless you can point to an exemption. Never tell a client "there's no GST" without professional advice.

### Who pays, who collects

| Party | Obligation |
|---|---|
| **Buyer** (the "recipient") | Must **pay** the GST. |
| **Seller** | Usually must **collect** it and **remit** it, holding it in **trust** for the government. If the seller doesn't collect, the seller is **equally liable** and the CRA usually assesses the seller, who can then pursue the buyer. |

**Exceptions where the buyer self-assesses** (reports the GST on its own return instead of paying the seller):

1. The buyer is **GST-registered** at closing (common for **commercial** property). This **doesn't** apply to an **individual** buying a **residential complex**.
2. The **seller is a non-resident**.

If the buyer is entitled to a full **input tax credit**, it claims the credit on the same return, so the **net cost is zero** and there's no cash flow hit.

> 🧮 **Example.** A $100 million office tower: GST would be **$5 million**. A GST-registered buyer self-assesses the $5M and claims a $5M ITC on the same return. Net: $0, and no $5M sitting with the government for months.

**Deposits:** GST generally isn't payable on a deposit until it **belongs to the seller** (usually at closing).

### Getting GST back

- **Input tax credits**: businesses recover GST paid on inputs to the extent they're used in **commercial** (taxable) activities. A doctor who buys a building for a clinic (exempt) and leases part to a pharmacy (taxable) can claim an ITC only for the pharmacy portion.
- **Rebates**: for non-businesses. The **GST/HST New Housing Rebate** recovers part of the GST on new homes (originally aimed at homes under **$450,000**). Apply within **2 years**; a builder can credit it to the buyer on the **statement of adjustments**. It's often audited (e.g. was the home really the buyer's primary residence?).

**Disputes:** a CRA **assessment** is collectible right away. The taxpayer has **90 days** to file an **objection**, then **90 days** after confirmation to appeal to the **Tax Court of Canada**.

### Residential complexes and the hotel exclusion

A **residential complex** covers most homes and condos, but **not** hotel-like units. A home used for **short-term rentals** (Airbnb) may fall into the **hotel exclusion** and be taxable, however old it is.

Only land **reasonably necessary** for the home's use as a residence is part of the residential complex. **Excess land** is considered separately (it may or may not be taxable).

### Builders and the self-supply rule

There's no exemption for "used housing" as such. The exemption is for sales by someone **who isn't a builder**.

A **builder** under the ETA is much broader than the everyday word: anyone who holds an interest in property **while it's being built or substantially renovated**, or who holds newly built property **before anyone lives in it**. For an individual, it must be in the course of a business or an **adventure in the nature of trade** (i.e. a profit motive).

- **A builder selling new housing** must charge GST.
- **Self-supply rule**: if a builder **moves in**, or rents to a **long-term tenant**, before selling, they're deemed to have sold the home to themselves at fair market value and owe GST **then**. The later sale is exempt.
- If the buyer is charged GST that the builder should have self-assessed, it's paid **twice**; the buyer can apply for a refund within **2 years**.

> 🧠 **Two contrasting stories.**
>
> - **Mr. Jones** buys a new home to live in, but is transferred to Halifax before moving in and sells. **Not a builder**: no business or profit motive. No GST.
> - An owner does a **substantial renovation**, partly hoping to boost resale value, moves back briefly, then lists it as "**Brand new home. No GST.**" The sale **might** be taxable, and that ad practically invites a CRA audit. The licensee should have flagged the GST risk and urged expert advice.
>
> Newly built or heavily renovated homes are where GST advice is most often needed. In *703008 B.C. Ltd.* (2015 TCC), a developer that offered long-term leases with purchase options instead of sales still had to self-assess GST.

### Is GST included in the price?

If a contract is **silent**, courts have generally treated GST as **extra**. Since **November 2024**, the standard BC **Contract of Purchase and Sale** says the price **includes GST, if applicable**, unless the parties agree otherwise in writing. A seller who wants GST on top can use a clause like "The purchase price does not include any GST…", which should prompt the buyer to get advice.

### Current GST issues

- **Resort condos**: registering and self-assessing to "defer GST" while mainly using the unit personally isn't legitimate. For mixed use, the CRA counts **nights actually rented**, not nights listed. Example: 40 nights rented and 35 personal → 40 ÷ 75 = **53%** commercial use. A change in use of **10% or more** triggers a change-in-use adjustment. Keep detailed logs.
- **Assignment fees**: an assignor of a presale may be a builder, so GST may apply to the assignment fee.
- **Residential care facilities**: treated like apartment buildings; the first tenant triggers self-supply.
- **Very long leases** (e.g. 99 years): the developer may still have to self-assess.
- **First Nations land**: complex; get advice early.

## Property transfer tax

The ***Property Transfer Tax Act*** taxes **registered transfers** of interests in land: a fee simple sale, but also an **agreement for sale**, **life estate** or **Crown grant**.

- **Who pays:** the **buyer** (the transferee). The seller has **no** collection role.
- **When:** before the transfer can be registered, with a **PTT return** filed at the land title office.
- **On what:** **fair market value** at registration (usually the price). Includes **fixtures**.

### The rates

| Portion of fair market value | Rate |
|---|---|
| First **$200,000** | **1%** |
| $200,000 to **$2,000,000** | **2%** |
| Over **$2,000,000** | **3%** |
| Residential, over **$3,000,000** | **extra 2%** (so 5% total) |

> 🧮 **Worked examples.**
>
> - **$1,500,000 home:** 1% × $200,000 = $2,000; 2% × $1,300,000 = $26,000. **PTT = $28,000.**
> - **$3,000,000 home:** $2,000 + $36,000 + 3% × $1,000,000 = $30,000. **PTT = $68,000.**
> - **$4,000,000 home:** $2,000 + $36,000 + 3% × $2,000,000 ($60,000) + 2% × $1,000,000 ($20,000). **PTT = $118,000.**
> - **$4,000,000 commercial building** (no residential surcharge): $2,000 + $36,000 + $60,000 = **$98,000.**

**When fair market value isn't the price:**

- **Assignments**: on presale **strata** units, PTT is on the contract price **plus** any amounts paid to assignors and the developer (including upgrades), not the value at closing. **Non-strata** presales (e.g. custom-built detached homes) pay PTT on **market value at closing**.
- **Commercial fixtures**: machinery that counts as **fixtures** is included. In ***Zellstoff Celgar*** (2014 BCCA), treating a pulp mill's equipment as fixtures raised the PTT from under **$300,000** to **$4.5 million**.

### Exemptions

Common ones: transfers of a principal residence between **related individuals**; transfers between **spouses** under a separation agreement or court order; **family farms**.

| Program | Key conditions | Relief |
|---|---|---|
| **First Time Home Buyers'** | Citizen or PR; lived in BC **1 year** (generally); **never owned** a principal residence anywhere; will be principal residence; FMV ≤ **$835,000**; ≤ **0.5 ha** | No PTT on the first **$500,000**. Partial relief up to **$860,000**. |
| **Newly Built Home** | Individual; citizen or PR; principal residence; FMV ≤ **$1,100,000** | Full exemption; partial up to **$1,150,000**. |

> 🧮 **First-time buyer, $700,000 condo.** Normal PTT: $2,000 + 2% × $500,000 = **$12,000**. Tax on the first $500,000 ($2,000 + $6,000 = $8,000) is exempt, so the buyer pays only 2% × $200,000 = **$4,000**.

Two buyers who are each first-time buyers **can't combine** their thresholds. If one co-buyer has owned before, relief is limited to the other buyer's share.

**Bare trusts:** since 2016, a buyer using a **bare trust** (a nominee on title for the real, beneficial owner) must disclose the beneficial owners and their citizenship on the PTT return.

**PTT disputes:** audit → assessment → **objection** to the province → **BC Supreme Court** → BC Court of Appeal.

### The foreign buyers' tax

An **additional PTT** of **20%** (raised from 15% on **February 21, 2018**) on **residential** property in designated areas: **Metro Vancouver**, the **Capital Regional District**, the **Fraser Valley**, **Central Okanagan** and **Nanaimo Regional District**.

It applies when the transferee is a:

- **foreign national**: not a Canadian citizen or permanent resident
- **foreign corporation**: not incorporated in Canada, **or controlled** by foreign nationals or corporations (control is a complex test; a BC company isn't automatically safe)
- **taxable trustee**: a trustee who is foreign, or who holds title **for** a foreign national or corporation

> 🧮 **Example.** A foreign national buys a $1,200,000 condo in Metro Vancouver. Basic PTT: $2,000 + 2% × $1,000,000 = $22,000. Foreign buyers' tax: 20% × $1,200,000 = $240,000. **Total: $262,000.**

**Traps:**

- If **one** of several buyers is foreign, the **whole** purchase may be taxed. (Mixed residential/commercial property is split, though.)
- Some basic PTT exemptions **don't** apply to the foreign buyers' tax.
- An **anti-avoidance rule** catches arrangements that comply with the words of the law but not its spirit.
- Relief exists for **BC Provincial Nominee Program** buyers (principal residence) and a **rebate** if the buyer becomes a **PR or citizen within 1 year**.

> 🧭 **Case: Shave v. Century 21 Assurance Realty (2022 BCSC).** UK buyers' first offer correctly said they weren't citizens or PRs. Before the second offer, the licensee wrongly assumed they'd become PRs, changed the residency answer to "yes" without asking, and emailed the offer for e-signature without pointing out the change. The buyers were hit with about **$175,000** in foreign buyers' tax.
>
> Liability: **licensee 75%**, buyers' lawyer 20%, buyers 5%. You don't have to advise **whether** the tax applies, but you must **tell clients it exists** and send them for advice. And never change a document without confirming the facts with your client.

> 💡 **The federal ban.** The *Prohibition on the Purchase of Residential Property by Non-Canadians Act* bans most non-Canadians from buying residential property until **January 1, 2027** (with exceptions for some students, work permit holders and refugees). Because the definitions differ, a buyer may be allowed to buy under the ban and **still** owe the foreign buyers' tax.

## Non-resident sellers: withholding tax

A **non-resident** selling Canadian real estate is taxed on the gain. To make sure it gets paid, the **buyer must withhold** part of the **gross price** unless the seller provides a CRA **clearance certificate** by closing:

| Property | Holdback |
|---|---|
| Non-depreciable (e.g. the seller's residence) | **25%** of the gross price |
| **Depreciable** (a rental or business building) | **50%** of the gross price |

The holdback sits in the lawyer's or notary's trust account until the clearance certificate arrives; then the tax is paid and the rest released. It's a **temporary** withholding, not the final tax.

> 🚨 **The buyer pays if nobody checks.** If the buyer doesn't withhold and the non-resident seller doesn't pay, the **buyer** can be liable. The buyer is protected by making a **"reasonable inquiry"** into the seller's residency (*Income Tax Act* s.116(5)).
>
> In ***Mao v. Liu*** (2017 BCSC), a notary went ahead without proper residency confirmation on a court-ordered sale. One seller turned out to be a non-resident; the buyers were assessed **over $695,000**, and the notary was liable. If you have **any** hint the seller is a non-resident, tell your client's lawyer or notary.

## Capital gains and flipping

| Type of profit | Taxable portion |
|---|---|
| **Capital gain** | Usually **50%**, at the owner's tax rate |
| **Business income** | **100%** |
| Gain on a **principal residence** | Generally **exempt** (fully, if it was the principal residence every year and no part was used to earn income) |

Example: buy for $1,000,000, sell for $1,400,000. Profit $400,000. As a capital gain, $200,000 is taxable; as business income, all $400,000 is.

### Is it income or capital?

Courts look at the factors in ***Happy Valley Farms*** (1986): the nature of the property, how long it was owned, how often the owner does similar deals, work done on the property, why it was sold, and above all the owner's **motive**.

The **secondary intention** test: if, when buying, the owner had resale for profit in mind as an **operating motive**, the gain can be **business income**.

The CRA also looks at the **financing** (short-term financing suggests a quick sale), the owner's **profession** and real estate history, and the circumstances of the sale.

> 🧭 **Case: Wall v. Canada (2021 FCA).** A licensee bought, renovated and resold three Vancouver homes between 2004 and 2010, claiming each as his principal residence. The court wasn't convinced he lived in them; the profits were **business income**. Never tell a client a sale will be tax-free because it's their "principal residence", especially if they buy and sell often.

### Federal property flipping rule (2023)

From **January 1, 2023**, profit on a **residential** property (including an **assignment**) held for **less than 365 days** is deemed **business income**, and the principal residence exemption doesn't apply.

Life-event exceptions include: **death**, **marriage breakdown**, a threat to **personal safety**, an eligible **work relocation**, involuntary **job loss**, and **insolvency**. The CRA can still treat longer holds as business income under the normal tests.

### BC home flipping tax (2025)

From **January 1, 2025**, under the *Residential Property (Short-Term Holding) Profit Tax Act*:

- applies to **residential** property, **presales** and **assignments** held for **less than 730 days**
- **20%** of the profit if sold within **365 days**, then declining to **0** at **730 days**
- applies to **everyone** (individuals, companies, trusts), resident or not
- **separate** from income tax and the federal rule; its own return, due within **90 days** of the sale
- being a principal residence doesn't automatically exempt the sale

## Property tax

Property tax is the main tax on **owning** land. Licensees get asked about it constantly: how to split it on a sale, whether an assessment is fair, and how to appeal.

**The formula:**

> **Property tax = net taxable value ÷ 1,000 × mill rate**

The **net taxable value** is the assessed value less any exemptions. A **mill rate** of 3.11827 means $3.11827 per $1,000.

> 🧮 **Example.** Assessed value $850,000, total mill rate 2.9: $850,000 ÷ 1,000 × 2.9 = **$2,465**. A Metro Vancouver owner-occupier who gets the $570 home owner grant pays **$1,895**.

There's no single property tax statute; the system draws on the *Assessment Act*, *Assessment Authority Act*, *Community Charter*, *Home Owner Grant Act*, *Land Tax Deferment Act*, *Local Government Act*, *School Act*, *Taxation (Rural Area) Act* and *Vancouver Charter*. **BC Assessment** (a Crown corporation) values every property.

### Who pays

The **registered owner** as of **November 30** (the land title records the assessor uses) is liable, including a **life tenant** and the holder of the last registered **agreement for sale**. On Crown or municipal land, a **tenant or occupier** may be taxed directly.

**Statutory vs contractual liability:** the law makes the owner liable to the government, but a **lease** can make a tenant pay the owner, and a **contract of purchase and sale** splits the year's taxes between buyer and seller on the **statement of adjustments**.

### What's taxed

**Land** (including land under water, quarries, sand and gravel, but not coal or minerals) and **improvements**: buildings, fixtures and structures. Improvements **don't** include production machinery, things moved as a unit in daily use, or easily movable furniture and equipment.

Exceptions: pipelines, cables and similar continuous structures are assessed to whoever owns or operates them; each residential building on a **co-op** or multi-dwelling leased parcel is assessed separately.

### What value is taxed

**Actual value** = **market value** of the fee simple (land and improvements), as of **July 1**. The assessor may consider present use, location, cost, replacement cost, rent, sales of comparable properties, obsolescence and other factors. Some utility and industrial property has special valuation rules.

### Property classes

| Class | Use |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Residential** |
| 2 | Utilities |
| 3 | Supportive housing |
| 4 | Major industry |
| 5 | Light industry |
| 6 | **Business and other** |
| 7 | Managed forest land |
| 8 | Recreational / non-profit |
| 9 | Farm |

Each class can have a **different tax rate**. A property with more than one use (e.g. shops below, apartments above) gets a **split classification**.

> 🏢 **Class matters for commercial clients.** Class 6 (business) rates are usually several times the residential rate. On a mixed-use building, the split between Class 1 and Class 6 directly changes the tax bill and the NOI. In a **triple net lease**, the tenant pays those taxes, so check that the lease, the classification and the assessed value all make sense.

### Additional school tax

Every owner pays **school tax** as part of property tax. Residential properties assessed over **$3 million** also pay an **additional school tax**: **0.2%** on the value between $3M and $4M, and **0.4%** above $4M. Example: a $5M home pays $2,000 + $4,000 = **$6,000** extra.

### Who sets the rates

Taxing authorities set rates each **spring** to raise the money they need: municipalities, the **Surveyor of Taxes** (rural areas), the **Ministry of Education** (school tax), BC Assessment, the Municipal Finance Authority, regional districts, and transit. The bill groups them: general, school, hospital, regional district and other taxes.

> ⚠️ **A higher assessment doesn't automatically mean higher taxes.** Taxes depend on what each authority needs to raise and how your property's value changed **compared with others**.

## The assessment and tax calendar

| Date | What happens |
|---|---|
| **July 1** | **Valuation date** for the roll (physical condition and permitted use as of **October 31**) |
| **October 31** | Condition and use "freeze" date |
| **November 30** | Owner on the roll = owner on land title records |
| **December 31** | Assessment roll completed |
| Early January | **Assessment notices** mailed |
| **January 31** | Deadline for a written **complaint** to the assessor |
| **February 1 – March 15** | **Property Assessment Review Panels** (PARP) sit |
| **March 31** | Roll authenticated; final for the year (subject to further appeal) |
| **April 30** | Deadline to appeal to the **Property Assessment Appeal Board** (PAAB) |
| Spring | Tax rates set; **tax notices** sent |
| Around **July 1** | Property taxes **due** (some municipalities differ) |

> 🧠 **Two quirks.** A renovation finished **after October 31** usually isn't picked up until the next year's roll. And a sale registered **after November 30** won't change the owner on that roll.

> 💡 **Don't use assessed values as market value.** The roll is based on a **July 1** value and is out of date by the time it's mailed in a moving market. In ***Dosanjh v. Liang*** (2015 BCCA), the court refused to treat an assessment as evidence of true value. Use a **CMA** or **appraisal**.

### Appealing an assessment

**Anyone** (not just the owner) can complain that:

- a name or a property was wrongly included or left off the roll
- a property is valued **too high or too low**
- a property is **wrongly classified**
- an **exemption** was wrongly allowed or refused

The path: **assessor** (complaint by **Jan 31**) → **PARP** → **PAAB** (by **April 30**) → **BC Supreme Court** (point of law) → BC Court of Appeal → Supreme Court of Canada.

> 🔑 **You appeal the assessment, not the tax.** Owners can't appeal the tax bill itself, only the assessment that it's based on.

## Exemptions, grants and deferral

**Exemptions:** mandatory for property like **Crown** land (unless occupied by a non-exempt occupier) and **places of worship**; others are at the local government's discretion.

**Assessment relief (value other than market value):**

- **Farm land**: valued as a farm only.
- **Long-time residents**: if the owner has lived there continuously for **10 years**, applies **every year**, and the area has been rezoned for a higher use (or affected by neighbouring non-residential uses), the home can be assessed on its **current residential use**. Example: an owner since 1987 in a West End house on land zoned for towers.
- **Managed forest**: bare land value plus a value for timber when it's cut.
- **Golf courses and cemeteries**: council can agree on a fixed value for at least **10 years**.

### Home owner grant

For a **registered owner** who is a **Canadian citizen or PR**, **ordinarily resident in BC**, living in the home as their **principal residence**:

- basic grant **$570** in Metro Vancouver, Fraser Valley and the Capital Regional District; **$770** in northern and rural areas
- **phased out** above a value threshold that's set each year
- the owner must generally pay at least **$350** in tax before the grant applies
- **higher** grants for **seniors (65+)**, **veterans** and people with **disabilities**

### Property tax deferment

A **low-interest loan** from the province to pay the current year's property tax on a principal residence:

| Program | Who | Minimum equity |
|---|---|---|
| **Regular** | **55+**, a surviving spouse, or a person with a disability; citizen or PR; in BC **1 year** | **25%** |
| **Families with children** | Financially supporting a child; citizen or PR; in BC **1 year** | **15%** |

The deferral is a **registered charge** on title, with simple interest. It must be **repaid before the property can be sold or transferred**; once paid, a **Notice of Satisfaction** lets the land title office remove the charge.

> 💡 **Check title for a deferment charge.** On a listing, a tax deferment charge means part of the seller's proceeds must go to repay the province at closing.

## Speculation and Vacancy Tax

BC's **SVT** (since 2018) is an **annual** tax on **residential** property in designated **taxable areas** (mostly urban centres; not reserve or treaty land, and not islands reachable only by air or water, except Vancouver Island). Its goal is to turn empty homes into homes for people who live and work in BC.

- Every owner (including **each spouse**) must file a **declaration** by **March 31** for the previous year, even if exempt. **No declaration = taxed at the highest rate.**
- Owned on **December 31**, based on the **July 1** assessed value; payment due the next **July**.
- Since 2024, **registered leaseholders** (not the fee simple owner) are responsible for leasehold residential property.
- Owned by charities, housing co-ops, governments, Indigenous Nations and some non-profits: excluded.

**Rates (2026 onward):**

| Owner | Rate |
|---|---|
| Foreign owners and **satellite families** | **3%** |
| Canadian citizens and PRs (not satellite families) | **1%** |

A **satellite family** is one whose **unreported** (worldwide) income is greater than its income **reported in Canada** (spouses' incomes combined). Even Canadian citizens living in BC can be caught.

**Main exemptions:**

- **Principal residence**: a Canadian citizen or PR, BC resident for tax, not a satellite family. A couple generally gets **one**.
- **Rented**: occupied by a tenant at least **6 months** of the year, in periods of **1 month or longer**. An **arm's length** tenant needs a written **RTA** tenancy agreement; rules for **non-arm's length** tenants (family) depend on the owner's status.
- Others: renovations, hazardous or damaged property, year of purchase, medical absence, separation, death, land under development, licensed daycares, strata hotel units, and more.

**Tax credit:** BC-resident owners get a credit of up to **$4,000** (from 2026) on a secondary property. At 1%, that means a property worth up to **$400,000** pays no SVT, and on a $700,000 property the owner pays 1% × $700,000 − $4,000 = **$3,000**. Non-residents and foreign owners may get credits based on **BC income** earned.

Corporations, partnerships and trusts are also caught, through complex look-through rules. **Refer clients to a tax professional.**

## Vancouver's Empty Homes Tax

The City of Vancouver's **Vacancy Tax Bylaw** charges a **3% Empty Homes Tax (EHT)** on the **assessed value** of homes that are **vacant** (unoccupied for **6 months or more** of the year). It's **on top of** property tax and the SVT.

**Not vacant if:**

- someone's **principal residence** (owner, family or friend) for at least **6 months**, or
- **rented** under a written tenancy in periods of at least **30 consecutive days**, totalling at least **6 months** (the periods needn't be back to back)

A short-term rental (under 30 days) doesn't count as occupied.

**Process:**

- Every owner files a **Property Status Declaration (PSD)**, starting in December. **No PSD = deemed vacant.**
- **False declaration**: fine up to **$10,000**, plus the tax.
- Tax notices go out by **mid-March**; the EHT is due on the **10th business day of April**.
- Disputes: **Vacancy Tax Review Officer**, then the **Vacancy Tax Review Panel**.
- A late PSD can be filed up to **5 years** later, with a **5%** penalty.

**Exemptions** include: owner working full-time in Greater Vancouver for 6+ months, permitted **redevelopment or major renovation** carried out diligently, occupants in **care**, owner's **death**, **title transferred** that year, parking-only or unbuildable lots, a **court order** prohibiting occupancy, **unsold new inventory**, **hazards or disasters**, and a secondary residence near **medical treatment**.

> 🧮 **Example.** A $1,500,000 condo left empty all year in Vancouver: EHT = 3% × $1,500,000 = **$45,000**, plus any SVT.

> 💡 **Contract terms for Vancouver buyers.** Ask the seller about the property's occupancy this year and last year. Consider: a copy of the filed PSD; a statutory declaration at closing; a warranty that the home wasn't vacant over **180 days** in the current or prior year; a **holdback** if the city hasn't decided yet; and that any EHT is the **seller's** responsibility.

## Underused Housing Tax

The federal **UHT** is a **1%** annual tax on the value of residential property owned by **non-Canadians** (and some others), owned on **December 31**.

**Excluded owners** (no filing, no tax) include:

- **Canadian citizens and PRs** (unless holding as a partner or trustee)
- **public Canadian corporations** listed on a Canadian stock exchange
- trustees of **mutual fund trusts, REITs** and SIFT trusts
- **registered charities**, housing **co-ops**, municipalities, hospitals, schools and universities

**Affected owners** must file a return by **April 30** even if exempt. Minimum penalty for not filing: **$1,000** (individual) or **$2,000** (corporation).

**Main exemptions:** the owner's (or spouse's, or studying child's) **primary residence**; a **qualifying occupancy** of **180 days** or more (periods of at least one month by an arm's length tenant, a family member, etc.); new construction; uninhabitable or seasonal property; a **vacation property** in an eligible area used at least **28 days**; and new owners in their first year.

> 🧠 **The three "empty home" taxes side by side.**
>
> | | UHT | SVT | EHT |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Level | Federal | BC | Vancouver |
> | Rate | **1%** | **1%** or **3%** | **3%** |
> | Who | Mostly **non-Canadians** | Most owners in **taxable areas** must declare | All Vancouver residential owners must declare |
> | Filing | Return by **April 30** | Declaration by **March 31** | PSD (from December) |
> | Occupancy test | **180 days** | **6 months** rented | **6 months** |
>
> A vacant Vancouver home owned by a non-Canadian could face **all three**.

## Two alerts

> 🚨 **ALERT 1: Check zoning, permits and licensing for the actual intended use.** Rules s.30(h) requires reasonable efforts to discover relevant facts. Illegal suites, non-conforming uses, s.57 notices, heritage status, ALR status, DP areas and business licence limits all qualify. Ask the municipality, ideally in writing, and don't rely on how the property is being used today.

> 🚨 **ALERT 2: Flag the tax, don't advise on it.** Tell clients when GST, PTT, the foreign buyers' tax, non-resident withholding, flipping taxes or vacancy taxes **might** apply, and refer them to a tax professional (Rules s.30(d)). Never suggest ways to dodge a tax, never write "No GST" on a listing without advice, and never change a residency declaration without confirming it.

## Trap list: things the exam likes to twist

1. The federal government can't zone provincial land directly; it acts through its own powers (airports, fisheries) and federal land isn't subject to zoning.
2. The **ALR** is about **5 million ha**; new principal residences ≤ **500 m²**; the **ALC's records** beat the title notation.
3. ALC **exclusion** removes land from the ALR; approved **non-farm use** or **subdivision** leaves it in.
4. *Transportation Act*: rezoning within **800 m** of a controlled access highway intersection needs provincial approval.
5. Undesignated heritage sites are still protected (*Mackay*); title search alone isn't enough.
6. **Community Charter** = municipalities; **Local Government Act** = regional districts + planning for all; **Vancouver Charter** = Vancouver.
7. Council: mayor + **4–8 councillors**, **4-year** terms. **Improvement districts** have trustees and **no** land-use planning powers.
8. OCPs are **optional**; once adopted, bylaws must be **consistent**; OCP is **not** zoning and doesn't commit spending. Housing policy ≥ **5 years**; update every **5 years**.
9. Zoning = **current** use; OCP = current **and future**.
10. **FSR** = floor area ÷ lot area.
11. Rezoning: **three readings + final adoption**; public hearing if **inconsistent** with the OCP; generally **no** hearing for OCP-consistent residential projects.
12. **No compensation** for rezoning or OCP changes, or for terminated land use contracts.
13. Land use contracts: **1971–1978**; all ended **June 30, 2024**.
14. Non-conforming use lost after **6 months** discontinued (**90 days** Vancouver); rebuild to code if damaged **75%+** of value; can't extend onto **more land**.
15. **Board of variance**: every local government with zoning **must** have one; **minor** variance, **undue hardship**, siting/size/dimensions only.
16. **DVP**: council; can't vary **use or density**.
17. **TUP**: commercial/industrial; up to **3 years**; renew **once**.
18. **DP areas** must be designated in the **OCP** first.
19. *Hlynsky*: approving officer's refusal overturned only for **bad faith**, **discrimination** or a **specious** factual basis.
20. Park dedication up to **5%**. DCCs = **bylaw**; CACs = **negotiated**, can't be imposed.
21. If building plans comply, the permit **must** be issued. Defects in complex buildings → claim against the **registered professional**.
22. s.57 *Community Charter* notice warns of a bylaw contravention; no notice ≠ no order.
23. Zoning OK doesn't mean the **business licence** is OK (the pet store).
24. **STRAA** (2023): principal residence + one suite/ADU; municipal rules can be **stricter**.
25. GST is **5%**; taxable unless listed in **Schedule V**. Exemption is for sellers who **aren't builders**, not for "used homes".
26. Buyer **pays** GST; seller **collects**; seller liable if it doesn't. **Self-assess** if buyer is GST-registered (not an individual buying a residential complex) or seller is **non-resident**.
27. No GST on a **deposit** until it belongs to the seller. Objection **90 days**; Tax Court **90 days**.
28. **Self-supply**: builder moves in or rents long-term → GST owed then; later sale exempt.
29. Since **Nov 2024**, the standard CPS price **includes GST, if applicable**. If silent (old contracts), courts say **extra**.
30. PTT: **1%** to $200K, **2%** to $2M, **3%** above, **+2%** residential over **$3M**. Paid by the **buyer**; no seller collection.
31. First-time buyer: FMV ≤ **$835,000**, ≤ **0.5 ha**, exempt on first **$500,000**. Newly built: ≤ **$1,100,000**.
32. Foreign buyers' tax: **20%**, residential, designated areas; one foreign co-buyer can taint the **whole** purchase; rebate if PR/citizen within **1 year**.
33. *Shave*: licensee **75%** liable for not flagging the foreign buyers' tax and changing the residency answer.
34. Non-resident seller withholding: **25%** (non-depreciable) / **50%** (depreciable) of the **gross price**, unless a clearance certificate; buyer protected by a **reasonable inquiry**.
35. Capital gain **50%** taxable; business income **100%**; *Happy Valley Farms*: **motive** matters most; **secondary intention**.
36. Federal flipping: < **365 days** → business income, no PRE. BC flipping tax: < **730 days**, **20%** in year 1, separate return within **90 days**.
37. Property tax = value ÷ 1,000 × **mill rate**. Owner as of **Nov 30**. Valuation **July 1**; condition as of **Oct 31**.
38. Complaint by **Jan 31**; PARP **Feb 1–Mar 15**; roll final **Mar 31**; PAAB by **April 30**. Appeal the **assessment**, not the tax.
39. Home owner grant **$570** (Metro Van, Fraser Valley, CRD) / **$770** (north and rural); min **$350** tax paid.
40. Deferment: **55+** with **25%** equity, or families with children with **15%** equity; registered charge, repaid before sale.
41. SVT: **3%** foreign/satellite, **1%** Canadians; declaration by **March 31**; rented **6 months** in 1-month+ periods; BC resident credit **$4,000** (2026+).
42. EHT: **3%**, vacant **6+ months**; rentals must be **30+ days**; PSD; false declaration fine up to **$10,000**.
43. UHT: **1%**, mostly non-Canadians; return by **April 30** even if exempt; penalty from **$1,000 / $2,000**.

## Drill

**Q1.** Your buyer wants to turn a house in an RS zone into a small dental office. What should you check, and what tools might get them there?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Zoning**: an office use almost certainly isn't permitted in a single-family zone. A **home occupation** might allow limited business use, but probably not a dental clinic with non-resident staff.
- **OCP**: what's the land designated for? If the OCP supports commercial or mixed use there, a **rezoning** is possible. If not, the OCP must be amended first.
- **Rezoning** takes three readings plus final adoption, likely a **public hearing**, conditions (dedications, servicing) and months to years. There's no guarantee.
- A **DVP** can't help (it can't change **use**). A **TUP** could allow a temporary commercial use, but only for up to 3 years with one renewal.
- Also check **parking** requirements, **business licensing**, any **DP area** rules on form and character, and **building code** upgrades for a change of use.
- Make the offer **subject to** satisfactory confirmation from the municipality, and recommend the client speak to a planner or lawyer.

</details>

**Q2.** A Canadian first-time buyer buys a $790,000 townhouse as their home. What's the PTT, and what would it be if they weren't a first-time buyer?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Normal PTT: 1% × $200,000 = $2,000 + 2% × $590,000 = $11,800. **Total $13,800.**

As a first-time buyer (FMV ≤ $835,000, assuming the other conditions are met), the first $500,000 is exempt: tax on it would be $2,000 + 2% × $300,000 = $8,000. They pay **$13,800 − $8,000 = $5,800** (which is 2% × $290,000).

</details>

**Q3.** A foreign national buys a $2,600,000 house in Victoria. Calculate the total PTT.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Basic PTT: $2,000 (first $200,000) + $36,000 (next $1,800,000) + 3% × $600,000 = $18,000. **Basic = $56,000.** (No residential surcharge; it starts above $3M.)

Victoria is in the **Capital Regional District**, so the **20%** foreign buyers' tax applies: 20% × $2,600,000 = **$520,000**.

**Total PTT = $576,000.**

</details>

**Q4.** A house was built 30 years ago with a 1.5 m side yard. The zoning now requires 2.5 m. A fire destroys about 80% of the house's value. The owner wants to rebuild it exactly as it was. Can they?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

No. The house was **lawfully non-conforming as to siting**. Because it was damaged to **75% or more** of its value, it must be rebuilt in line with the **current** bylaw, including the **2.5 m** side yard. The owner could apply to the **board of variance** for a **minor variance** if complying would cause **undue hardship**, or ask council for a **DVP** (siting can be varied; use and density can't).

</details>

**Q5.** You're selling a client's Burnaby rental building. Their home is in Singapore and they haven't lived in Canada for years. The price is $3,200,000. What should the buyer's side know?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- The seller appears to be a **non-resident**. A rental building is **depreciable** property, so unless the seller provides a CRA **clearance certificate** by closing, the buyer must **withhold 50%** of the **gross** price: **$1,600,000**, held in the lawyer's or notary's trust account.
- If the buyer doesn't withhold and the seller doesn't pay, the **buyer** may be liable. The buyer should make a **reasonable inquiry** into residency.
- Tell the seller early to apply for the clearance certificate (it takes time) and to get tax advice.
- **GST**: a residential rental building sold by a non-builder is generally **exempt**, but if any part is commercial, GST may apply and, because the seller is a **non-resident**, the **buyer self-assesses**.
- The buyer pays **PTT** (and the 20% foreign buyers' tax if the buyer is foreign and it's residential property in Metro Vancouver).

</details>

**Q6.** A BC-resident Canadian owns a $900,000 Kelowna condo (an SVT taxable area) as a second property and leaves it empty. Calculate the 2026 SVT. How could they avoid it?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

Rate for a Canadian citizen who isn't in a satellite family: **1%**. 1% × $900,000 = $9,000, less the BC-resident credit of **$4,000** = **$5,000**.

To avoid it: rent it to an arm's length tenant under a written tenancy agreement for at least **6 months** of the year, in periods of at least **1 month** (the rental exemption), or qualify for another exemption (e.g. renovation, year of acquisition). They must still file the declaration by **March 31**, or they'll be taxed at the highest rate.

</details>

## Mindset: the municipality and the taxman are silent parties to every deal

Every property comes with invisible partners. The **municipality** decides what can be built and used there. The **governments** take a share when it's bought, owned and sold. A great deal can turn into a disaster if either partner disagrees with your client's plan.

You don't need to memorize every bylaw or tax rule. You need two habits: **always ask** (city hall, the title, the ALC, the Archaeology Branch), and **always refer** (planners, lawyers, accountants). A licensee who says "let's confirm that with the city and your accountant before you commit" is protecting both the client and themselves.

> 🏢 **For commercial specifically.** This is where commercial licensees earn their fees.
>
> - **Highest and best use** starts with the **OCP** and **zoning**. Land designated for more density is worth more as a **development site**; that's how land assemblies get priced.
> - **Development economics**: rezoning timelines (months to years), **DCCs**, **CACs**, **park dedications**, servicing costs and **DP** requirements all come off the land value a developer can pay.
> - **Use checks**: for every tenant or owner-user, confirm zoning, **business licensing**, parking, signage and **non-conforming** status. Build them into **subject clauses**.
> - **GST** on commercial property is the norm; registered buyers **self-assess** and claim ITCs, so make sure your buyer is registered before closing.
> - **PTT** includes **fixtures**: on industrial deals, the line between fixtures and chattels can swing the tax by millions (*Zellstoff Celgar*).
> - **Property tax classes** (Class 6 business vs Class 1 residential) drive operating costs and **NOI**. Check the assessment, and appeal by **January 31** if it's wrong.
> - **Non-resident sellers** of income property face a **50%** holdback. Spot it early so the deal doesn't stall at closing.

> **This week's habit.** Look up your own municipality's **OCP land use map** and **zoning map** online. Pick one commercial street. What zone is it? What does the OCP say it should become? Then find the city's DCC bylaw and note the charge per apartment unit.
